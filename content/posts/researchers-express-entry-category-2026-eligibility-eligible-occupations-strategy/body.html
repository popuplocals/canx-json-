Canada has long positioned itself as a destination for scientific talent — but immigration policy has not always kept pace with that ambition. The 2026 introduction of a dedicated Express Entry category for researchers with Canadian work experience is a meaningful step toward closing that gap.

This category is designed for the scientists, academics, and innovation professionals already contributing to Canada’s research ecosystem on temporary status. Whether you are a postdoctoral fellow at a Canadian university, a research scientist at a biotech firm, or an industrial researcher at a government laboratory, this new stream could be your pathway to making Canada your permanent home. This blog covers everything currently known about the researchers category and how to position yourself for success.

Why Canada Needed a Dedicated Research Category

Canada’s research and innovation sector faces a persistent challenge: it attracts world-class talent through universities, research grants, and industry partnerships — but then struggles to retain that talent permanently. International researchers often arrive in Canada on study permits or temporary work authorizations, spend years building expertise and contributing to Canadian knowledge production, and then face uncertain immigration futures when those permits approach expiry.

The consequence is significant. Canada invests substantial public resources in training and hosting international researchers — through university funding, tri-council research grants (NSERC, SSHRC, CIHR), and innovation ecosystem support. When those researchers leave because of immigration uncertainty, that investment follows them to other countries. The United States, Germany, the United Kingdom, and Australia are aggressive competitors for the same talent pool.

Budget 2025 explicitly identified research capacity as a pillar of Canada’s long-term economic competitiveness strategy. The researchers category under Express Entry is the immigration system’s contribution to that strategy — a direct tool for retaining people whose work drives innovation, publication, patenting, and commercialization in Canada.

What We Know About Eligible Occupations

As of the date of this writing, IRCC has not published the final, complete list of eligible NOC codes for the researchers category. The department confirmed the category’s creation on February 18, 2026, but indicated that full operational details — including the definitive list of qualifying occupations — would be published as the category is implemented throughout 2026.

However, the policy framing provided by IRCC and subsequent government publications gives us strong indicators of which occupations will be included. The researchers category is described as targeting candidates who are “highly educated individuals who contribute to innovation, research, and specialized sectors of the economy.” More specifically, it is expected to cover:

  • Scientific researchers in natural sciences, including biology, chemistry, physics, environmental science, and related disciplines.
  • Academic researchers employed by universities, colleges, and research institutes in tenured or research-track positions.
  • Industrial researchers working in R&D departments at private sector companies, particularly in STEM-adjacent industries like pharmaceuticals, artificial intelligence, clean technology, and advanced manufacturing.
  • Graduate researchers in postdoctoral positions — a classification that historically has been difficult to fit into Express Entry’s work experience framework.
  • Government and Crown corporation researchers working in federal or provincial research agencies.

The STEM occupations category, which predates the researchers category and continues in 2026, covers a broader range of technology and engineering professionals. The researchers category appears to be designed for deeper, more research-intensive roles that may not fit neatly within the STEM category’s existing NOC codes. There may be some overlap, and candidates who qualify under both categories will benefit from eligibility in multiple streams.

The Postdoctoral Fellow Question: Will PostDocs Qualify?

This is one of the most frequently asked questions about the researchers category, and the answer is: quite possibly, but with important nuances.

Postdoctoral fellowships occupy an ambiguous position in Canada’s employment taxonomy. PostDocs are research professionals with doctoral degrees who are employed in research roles at universities or research institutes — but they are often classified by institutions as “trainees” rather than employees. This classification has historically created challenges for Express Entry eligibility because the program requires genuine skilled employment rather than educational training.

IRCC’s recent policy direction suggests a recognition that postdoctoral positions are, in practice, skilled professional employment rather than study. The researchers category appears designed in part to address this gap. However, the specific rules governing postdoctoral work experience eligibility will depend on the final ministerial instructions for the category, which have not yet been fully published.

If you are a postdoctoral researcher in Canada, the critical question is whether your position is structured as an employment contract (with a T4 slip and standard employment benefits) versus a scholarship or stipend arrangement. The former is more likely to qualify as work experience for Express Entry purposes. We strongly recommend getting a professional assessment of your specific situation before relying on this category in your immigration planning.

The 12-Month Canadian Experience Requirement

Like the physicians and senior managers categories, the researchers stream requires Canadian work experience specifically. International research experience — however impressive on an academic CV — does not count toward the 12-month Canadian work experience threshold for this category.

This is not as limiting as it might initially appear, because the researchers category is precisely targeted at people who are already in Canada. IRCC’s intent is retention of resident talent, not recruitment from abroad. For those already here, the calculation is about how many months of eligible research employment they have accumulated in Canada, whether on an open work permit, a closed work permit tied to an employer, or a PGWP (Post-Graduate Work Permit) following doctoral studies.

The 12-month requirement applies within a rolling three-year window. If you have had breaks in employment or periods of part-time research work, those affect your total calculation. Part-time experience is recognized on a pro-rated basis — generally, two weeks of part-time work equivalent to one week of full-time work.

The Intersection with the STEM Category

Canada’s STEM occupations category has been part of the Express Entry framework since 2023, and it continues in 2026. For many researchers — particularly those working in applied technology, engineering, or computer science — the STEM category may already capture them. So what does the researchers category add?

The answer is that the researchers category appears designed to capture deeper research roles that may not neatly fit the STEM category’s existing NOC code list, which tends to skew toward applied technical occupations (software engineers, data scientists, engineering professionals) rather than fundamental or academic research.

Researchers in fields like ecology, sociology, medicine, economics, and humanities research may not qualify under STEM but could qualify under the researchers category. Similarly, academic researchers whose primary output is publications, grant-funded projects, and institutional knowledge generation — rather than technology development — represent a cohort that the researchers category specifically targets.

The practical advice is to assess your eligibility under both categories and ensure your Express Entry profile accurately reflects occupations that could qualify you for multiple category draws. This multi-stream eligibility strategy is one of the most powerful tools available to candidates.

How Canadian Research Funding Shapes Immigration Eligibility

Canada’s research funding landscape has direct implications for the employment arrangements of international researchers — and therefore for their Express Entry eligibility. Understanding this landscape helps researchers know how to structure and document their work experience for immigration purposes.

Tri-council grants (from NSERC, CIHR, and SSHRC) fund much of Canada’s academic research. Researchers funded through these grants may be employed directly by universities, employed as research associates, or receive fellowship payments. The employment structure — not the funding source — determines Express Entry eligibility. Researchers employed under a formal contract that includes standard employment documentation (ROE, T4, pay stubs) are in the strongest position.

Industry-funded research partnerships, Canada Foundation for Innovation projects, and federal government research positions tend to have clearer employment structures that align well with Express Entry documentation requirements. If you are working in one of these frameworks, your eligibility documentation pathway is likely cleaner than if you are in a purely grant-funded academic position.

Building Your Application: Documentation for Researchers

Researchers face some unique documentation challenges when applying through Express Entry because their work may produce outputs (publications, reports, patents, datasets) that are difficult to quantify in the terms Express Entry uses. Here is what you should be compiling:

  • Employment records: T4 slips, pay stubs, Records of Employment (ROE), and employment contracts for all periods of Canadian research employment. These are the most fundamental proof of qualifying work experience.
  • Reference letters from supervisors or principal investigators: These letters should specifically describe your research duties, your employment status, your hours worked, and the duration of your employment. Generic letters of recommendation from academic colleagues are not sufficient — they need to be structured around the specific requirements IRCC sets for work experience letters.
  • Proof of credentials: Your doctoral degree assessment from a designated assessment organization is likely necessary. IRCC’s credential assessment process for academics can take several months, so initiating it early is important.
  • Language test results: Research positions often attract people from non-English-speaking countries. Your language scores are a significant CRS component, and investing in preparation to achieve CLB 9 or higher can meaningfully boost your score and your chance of receiving an ITA.
