A procedural fairness letter (PFL) is one of the most important documents you can receive from IRCC. It means an officer has a concern that could lead to refusal, such as doubts about your work experience, your reference letters, your funds or the truthfulness of your application, and is giving you a chance to respond before deciding. A strong response can save your application. A weak or late one can lead to refusal and, in some cases, a five-year ban for misrepresentation.

This guide explains the legal basis for procedural fairness letters, what they usually concern, how to prepare an effective response and the mistakes to avoid.

Quick Summary

A procedural fairness letter gives you notice of an officer’s concerns and an opportunity to respond before a decision is made. It flows from the common law duty of procedural fairness recognized by the Supreme Court of Canada in Baker v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), [1999] 2 S.C.R. 817. In Express Entry, letters often concern work experience, NOC codes, reference letters, funds or possible misrepresentation under section 40 of IRPA. Respond before the deadline in your letter, address every concern directly and support your explanations with evidence.

The Legal Basis for Procedural Fairness Letters

IRPA and the IRPR do not contain a general rule requiring officers to send procedural fairness letters. The duty comes from administrative law. In Baker, the Supreme Court of Canada confirmed that decision-makers owe a duty of procedural fairness, the content of which varies with the context. In immigration applications, this generally means that when an officer has concerns about the credibility, accuracy or genuineness of information you submitted, or relies on information you have not seen, you should usually be given a chance to respond.

The Federal Court has also recognized limits on this duty. In Hassani v. Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration), 2006 FC 1283, the Court explained that officers generally do not need to alert applicants to concerns arising directly from the legal requirements, such as missing evidence the law already requires. This is why some Express Entry applications are refused without a PFL.

What Express Entry PFLs Commonly Concern

  • Work experience that may not match the claimed NOC code.
  • Reference letters that could not be verified or appear inconsistent.
  • Employers that do not appear to exist or operate as described.
  • Proof of funds, including the source of large deposits.
  • Undeclared family members, previous refusals or travel history.
  • Language test or education credential concerns.
  • Possible misrepresentation under section 40 of IRPA.

Your Deadline

The letter states your deadline. Many PFLs give around 30 days, but the actual time varies, so always follow the date in your letter. If you need more time for a genuine reason, such as waiting for documents from abroad, request an extension before the deadline and explain why. An officer is not required to grant it, so do not rely on an extension.

How to Prepare an Effective Response

Step 1: Identify every concern

Read the letter carefully and list each concern the officer raised. Your response must address every one.

Step 2: Consider requesting your file notes

Global Case Management System (GCMS) notes may show more about the officer’s reasoning. Requesting them takes time, so weigh this against your deadline.

Step 3: Gather evidence

Answer concerns with documents, not just explanations. Examples include payroll records, tax slips, bank statements, updated employer letters, business registration documents and sworn statements.

Step 4: Write a clear, organized submission

Use headings that match each concern. Refer to the relevant legal requirements, such as the lead statement and main duties test in sections 75 and 87.1 of the IRPR, and explain how your evidence meets them.

Step 5: Stay respectful and factual

Officers respond best to clear, polite and well-supported submissions. Avoid accusatory language.

Step 6: Submit on time through the correct channel

Follow the submission instructions in the letter exactly and keep proof of submission.

Mistakes to Avoid

  • Missing the deadline.
  • Responding to only some concerns.
  • Providing explanations without evidence.
  • Submitting new documents that contradict earlier ones without explanation.
  • Creating documents after the fact that are presented as originals.
  • Ignoring the possibility of misrepresentation, which carries a five-year inadmissibility under paragraph 40(2)(a) of IRPA.

What Happens After You Respond

The officer considers your response and makes a decision. If the application is refused, possible next steps include requesting reconsideration in limited circumstances, reapplying with a stronger application or seeking leave for judicial review in the Federal Court under section 72 of IRPA. Under paragraph 72(2)(b), the application for leave must be filed within 15 days for matters arising in Canada and 60 days for matters arising outside Canada.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a procedural fairness letter in Express Entry?

A procedural fairness letter is notice from IRCC that an officer has a concern that could lead to refusal, such as doubts about your work experience, documents or truthfulness. It gives you an opportunity to respond with explanations and evidence before the officer decides. The duty to provide this opportunity comes from administrative law principles recognized in Baker.

How long do I have to respond to a procedural fairness letter?

The deadline is stated in your letter. Many letters give around 30 days, but the time varies. Always follow the date in your letter. If you need more time for a genuine reason, request an extension before the deadline and explain why, but do not assume it will be granted.

Can IRCC refuse my Express Entry application without a procedural fairness letter?

Yes, in some cases. Officers generally do not have to warn you about evidence the law already requires you to provide. The Federal Court discussed this in Hassani. A PFL is usually expected when concerns relate to credibility, accuracy or genuineness, or when the officer relies on outside information.

Can a procedural fairness letter lead to a misrepresentation ban?

Yes. If the letter raises possible misrepresentation and the officer is not satisfied with your response, the officer may find you inadmissible under paragraph 40(1)(a) of IRPA. Under paragraph 40(2)(a), that inadmissibility lasts five years. Take misrepresentation PFLs extremely seriously and consider professional help.
