Part 2 of our Inland Spousal Sponsorship Series: how to prepare for your IRCC interview

You filed your inland spousal sponsorship application months ago. You submitted the forms, the photographs, the joint lease, the bank statements, the relationship letter. Then one day an email arrives from IRCC: you and your spouse or partner are invited to an interview.

This article reflects IRCC guidance and public information available as of early October 2026. Interview procedures can change. Always follow the instructions in your own interview letter.

For a growing number of inland couples, this is now part of the process. In our practice, and among colleagues across Canada, we are seeing interview requests in inland spousal files far more often than we did a few years ago. IRCC does not publish statistics on how many inland applicants are interviewed, so we cannot give you a percentage. But if you are filing an inland application today, you should assume an interview is a real possibility and prepare for it from the day you file.

Here is the message we want every couple to take away from this article:

The interview is often the turning point of an inland spousal application. Your documents still matter, and the officer will consider the whole file. But when an officer has concerns serious enough to call you in, what you say in that room, and whether you and your partner tell the same true story, can outweigh everything in your application package.

A strong file does not protect you from a weak interview. And a genuine relationship does not speak for itself. You have to be able to speak for it.

Part 1: It Does Not Matter Who Prepared Your Application

Some couples hired a lawyer. Some hired a consultant. Some prepared the application themselves. At the interview, none of that matters.

Your representative cannot answer for you. In an interview, the officer asks you, the applicant, and your sponsor, about your own lives. Even where a representative is permitted to attend, the questions are directed to you, and your answers are the evidence.

The legal responsibility is yours. Under section 16(1) of the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act, a person who makes an application must answer truthfully all questions put to them for the purpose of the examination. The burden of showing that the relationship is genuine and was not entered into primarily for immigration purposes rests with the applicant and sponsor, not with whoever filled in the forms.

Everything in the application is treated as your statement. You signed the forms. You declared that the information was true and complete. If the forms contain a date, an address, or a fact you do not recognize, the officer will not accept “my consultant wrote that” as an explanation. If you do not know what was submitted on your behalf, you are walking into the interview blind.

No one knows your story better than you. A professional can organize evidence and present the law. Only the two of you lived the relationship. Before you face an officer’s questions, you must know your own story, your partner’s story, and the story you told IRCC on paper.

Part 2: Why IRCC Calls Couples for Interviews

2.1 What IRCC's instructions say

IRCC updated its program delivery instructions in January 2026 to set out the interview referral process for family class and spouse or common-law partner in Canada class applications. According to the instructions, for inland applications, if a decision maker determines that further review is required, including a possible interview, the application is assessed within IRCC’s Humanitarian and Identity Operations Branch.

IRCC’s instructions describe interviews as a way to confirm identities and relationships, address admissibility concerns, and resolve uncertainties about the information submitted. The instructions also allow for the sponsor and applicant to be interviewed separately, particularly where a relationship of convenience or a forced marriage is suspected.

2.2 What usually triggers an interview

In our experience, interviews tend to follow one or more of these concerns:

  • Gaps or thin evidence about the development of the relationship
  • Inconsistencies between the current application and previous immigration applications (for example, marital status, addresses, or dates)
  • Timing of the marriage or cohabitation close to the end of the applicant’s status or after a refusal
  • A short relationship before marriage
  • Previous marriages, previous sponsorships, or previous relationship-based applications
  • Significant differences in age, background, language, or religion
  • Concerns about whether the couple is actually living together
  • Information from other sources that does not match the application

An interview invitation does not mean the officer has decided your relationship is not genuine. It means the officer has questions that the documents did not answer. The interview is your opportunity to answer them.

2.3 What the officer is assessing

Under section 4 of the Immigration and Refugee Protection Regulations, the officer must decide whether the marriage or common-law relationship is genuine, and whether it was entered into primarily for the purpose of acquiring status or privilege under the Act. Either finding is enough for refusal.

In the interview, the officer is essentially testing three things:

  • Knowledge: do you both know the things that partners in a genuine relationship would ordinarily know about each other and their shared life?
  • Consistency: do your answers match each other, and do they match the application and your immigration history?
  • Credibility: do your explanations make sense, and do you answer openly and naturally?

Part 3: What the Interview Looks Like

Formats and procedures vary by office and over time. Read your interview letter carefully and follow its instructions exactly. In general:

  • Interviews may take place in person at an IRCC office or, in some cases, by video. Your letter will tell you which.
  • The sponsor and the applicant may be interviewed together, separately, or both. Separate interviews are common where the officer wants to test consistency.
  • The interview letter will usually list the documents you must bring or have available, such as passports, identity documents, and original civil documents.
  • If you need an interpreter, raise this with IRCC in advance, following the instructions in your letter. Do not assume a family member or friend will be permitted to interpret for you.
  • If you have a representative, ask them in advance whether they can attend and what role they may play. IRCC policy generally allows counsel to be present in the interest of fairness, but the officer controls the interview, and the questions are for you to answer.
  • The officer will take notes. Those notes become part of your file and are often relied on in the decision.

Interviews can be short or can last a few hours. Plan for a full day, including travel and waiting time.

Part 4: The Three Stories You Must Know

Before any interview, each partner must be fully comfortable with three stories.

4.1 Your own story

It sounds obvious, but many applicants struggle with questions about their own lives because they have never had to say the details out loud, in order, under pressure. Know:

  • Your full immigration history in Canada: every permit, every application, every refusal, every extension, with approximate dates
  • Your education and employment history, including employer names, job titles, and dates
  • Your previous relationships and marriages, including how and when they ended
  • Your addresses, in Canada and abroad, over the relevant period
  • Your family members: names, ages, where they live, what they do
  • Your travel history

4.2 Your partner's story

Partners in a genuine relationship usually know a great deal about each other’s lives. The officer will expect you to know your partner’s:

  • Full name, date of birth, place of birth, and age
  • Parents’ and siblings’ names, where they live, what they do, and how often you are in contact with them
  • Education, current job, employer, work schedule, and approximate income
  • Previous relationships and marriages, if any, and children, if any
  • Immigration history in Canada, in general terms
  • Daily routine, habits, likes and dislikes, health issues, religious practices
  • Close friends and how you know them
  • Important dates: birthday, anniversary, family events

4.3 Your shared story, and the story on paper

The most important preparation step is to read your entire application again, together, before the interview. That includes:

  • The sponsorship and application forms, especially the relationship information and sponsorship evaluation form
  • Your relationship narrative or letter
  • Every document and photograph you submitted, and what it shows
  • Any letters of explanation, responses to IRCC requests, or PFL responses
  • Previous applications filed by either of you, to the extent you can obtain copies

If you used a representative, ask them for a complete copy of everything that was submitted. You are entitled to know what was filed in your name.

Where you find an error in the application, do not try to memorize the error. Tell the truth at the interview, and explain how the error happened. It is far better to correct a mistake openly than to be caught defending a fact that is not true.

Part 5: The Questions Officers Commonly Ask

There is no official public list of interview questions, and every officer and every file is different. The questions below are examples of the kinds of topics officers commonly explore, based on practice experience. Use them to check your knowledge, not to memorize answers.

5.1 How you met and how the relationship developed

  • How, when, and where did you first meet? Who introduced you?
  • What was your first impression of each other?
  • When did you first meet in person? Where did you go?
  • How did you communicate in the early stages? How often?
  • When did the relationship become serious? What made it serious?
  • When and how did you decide to marry or move in together? Who proposed, and how?

5.2 Families and the wedding

  • When did each of your families learn about the relationship? How did they react?
  • Have you met each other’s parents and siblings? When and where?
  • Who attended the wedding? Who did not, and why?
  • What ceremonies or traditions took place? Who paid for what?
  • For arranged marriages: how were the families introduced, who was involved, and what steps were followed?

5.3 Daily life and the home

  • Describe your home: number of rooms, layout, what is in the bedroom and kitchen
  • Who else lives with you, if anyone?
  • What time do each of you wake up and go to work? How do you get there?
  • Who does the cooking, cleaning, groceries?
  • What did you do last weekend? What did you eat last night?
  • Do you have pets? Cars? Who drives?

5.4 Finances

  • Do you have joint bank accounts? Where? Who pays rent, utilities, phone bills?
  • What does each of you earn, approximately?
  • Do you send money to family abroad?
  • Are you named as beneficiaries on each other’s insurance or benefits?

5.5 Knowledge of each other

  • What is your partner’s job? Where do they work? What hours?
  • What did your partner study?
  • What are your partner’s hobbies, favourite foods, and health conditions, if any?
  • What religion does your partner practise, and how?
  • When is your partner’s birthday? How did you celebrate it last year?

5.6 Immigration history and timing

  • Why did you marry when you did?
  • Were you aware the applicant’s status was expiring at the time?
  • Why does a previous application show a different marital status or address?
  • Have either of you been previously married, sponsored, or sponsored someone?

5.7 Future plans

  • Where do you see yourselves in five years?
  • Do you plan to have children? Where will you live?
  • What are your plans if the application is refused?

5.8 Sensitive and personal questions

Officers sometimes ask personal questions about the relationship. Answer honestly and respectfully. If a question makes you uncomfortable, you may say so politely, but understand that the officer is trying to assess the genuineness of the relationship. Refusing to engage may affect how your evidence is assessed.

Part 6: How to Prepare: A Practical Plan

Start preparing as soon as you receive the interview invitation, ideally several weeks before the date. A practical approach:

Step 1: Get the complete file

Obtain a full copy of your application and all correspondence with IRCC. If you used a representative, request the complete submitted package. Where useful, consider requesting your GCMS notes through an access to information or privacy request to see what the officer has recorded so far. These requests take time, so start early. You may want to check the current processing time for these requests, as it may not arrive before your interview.

Step 2: Read everything together

Sit down together and read every form, letter, and document. Mark anything that is wrong, unclear, or that you do not remember.

Step 3: Build your timeline

Create a single, shared timeline of the relationship: first contact, first meeting, key dates, family introductions, engagement, wedding, moving in together, important trips, and major life events. Check it against your documents.

Step 4: Prepare your own history

Each partner should separately write out their own immigration, education, employment, address, and relationship history. Check it against what you have filed with IRCC in this and previous applications.

Step 5: Learn about each other, naturally

Talk about each other’s families, past, work, and daily life. Many couples discover they have never discussed things that an officer may ask about. These conversations are not about memorizing facts. They are about making sure you genuinely know what you would be expected to know.

Step 6: Identify the officer's likely concerns

Look at your file honestly, as an officer would. What would worry you? A short courtship? A marriage close to a status expiry? A discrepancy in an earlier application? Be prepared to explain each concern in your own words, truthfully, with supporting evidence where possible.

Step 7: Gather updated evidence

Bring evidence of your relationship since the application was filed: recent bank statements, bills, lease renewals, photographs, travel, and anything showing your continued life together. Bring originals of documents you previously submitted, as listed in your letter.

Step 8: Practise speaking, not reciting

Practise answering questions out loud, separately. Ask a trusted person to ask you questions in random order. The goal is to become comfortable speaking naturally about your life, not to memorize polished answers.

Step 9: Prepare the practical details

Confirm the location, time, travel route, required documents, interpreter arrangements, and representative attendance. For video interviews, test your technology, internet connection, camera, and microphone in advance, and arrange a quiet, private space.

Part 7: On the Day of the Interview

  • Arrive early. Late arrival can create unnecessary stress and may affect the interview.
  • Bring every document listed in the interview letter, plus original identity documents and your updated evidence, organized and labelled.
  • Dress respectfully, as you would for any important appointment.
  • Listen carefully to each question. Answer the question that was asked.
  • If you do not understand a question, ask the officer to repeat or rephrase it.
  • If you do not know or do not remember something, say so. “I do not remember the exact date, but it was around early spring” is a far better answer than a guess.
  • Do not guess, exaggerate, or invent details to fill gaps.
  • Keep answers clear and natural. You do not need to give speeches, but give enough detail that the officer can understand.
  • If you realize you made a mistake earlier in the interview, correct it immediately. Say “I need to correct something I said earlier.”
  • Stay calm and respectful, even if a question feels repetitive or personal. Officers often ask the same question in different ways.
  • Do not argue with the officer. If you disagree with something the officer says, explain your position calmly.
  • At the end, if the officer raises concerns, ask whether you may provide additional documents or a written response, and if so, by when.

For video interviews, unless your letter permits otherwise, no one else should be in the room, and you should not read from notes or use other devices during the interview.

Part 8: Small Differences Versus Material Contradictions

Couples often worry that any difference in their answers will lead to refusal. That is not how it usually works.

Officers understand that people remember things differently. One partner may remember the first date as a Friday and the other as a Saturday. One may say the wedding had 150 guests and the other 200. Minor differences like these are normal in genuine relationships.

What creates serious problems are material contradictions:

  • Different accounts of how and where you met
  • Different accounts of when you started living together
  • One partner not knowing the other’s job, family members, or previous marriage
  • Different descriptions of your home or who lives there
  • Answers that contradict the documents or previous immigration applications
  • Explanations that change during the interview

The best protection is not rehearsal. It is genuine shared knowledge, careful review of the file, and honesty.

Part 9: What Not to Do

  • Do not memorize scripted answers. Officers recognize rehearsed responses, and scripts tend to collapse when the question is asked differently.
  • Do not memorize details you did not actually experience. If something in the application is wrong, correct it.
  • Do not contact each other during separate interviews or try to signal answers.
  • Do not bring false or altered documents.
  • Do not hide previous relationships, refusals, or immigration history.
  • Do not blame your representative for inconsistencies without first understanding what was submitted and why.
  • Do not treat the interview as a formality, even if your application was well documented.

Providing false information can lead to a finding of misrepresentation under section 40 of the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act, which generally results in five years of inadmissibility.

Part 10: After the Interview

10.1 Possible outcomes

After the interview, the officer may:

  • Approve the application, or move it forward to the next stage
  • Ask for additional documents
  • Issue a procedural fairness letter setting out concerns and giving you an opportunity to respond
  • Refuse the application

10.2 Write it down immediately

As soon as the interview ends, each partner should separately write down the questions asked and the answers given, as best you can remember. This record is valuable if you need to respond to concerns or challenge a decision later.

10.3 Follow up on anything you promised

If the officer requested documents or allowed you to provide a written response, submit it fully and before the deadline. Address every concern raised.

10.4 Responding to a procedural fairness letter

If you receive a PFL after the interview, treat it with the seriousness it deserves. Address each concern specifically, with evidence, and correct any misunderstanding of what you said at the interview. Professional help is strongly recommended at this stage.

10.5 If the application is refused

Inland spousal sponsorship refusals do not carry an appeal to the Immigration Appeal Division. The remedy is an application for leave and judicial review to the Federal Court. For decisions made in Canada, the deadline to file is generally 15 days from when you are notified of the decision. This deadline is short, so obtain legal advice immediately. You should also request your GCMS notes, which usually contain the officer’s interview notes and reasons.

Conclusion

The interview is where the paper application meets real life. For many inland couples today, it is the deciding moment.

It does not matter whether you hired a lawyer, a consultant, or did the application yourself. In that room, you are the evidence. The officer is listening to two people describe one shared life. If that life is real, and you have prepared by knowing your own story, your partner’s story, and exactly what you told IRCC, you will be in the strongest possible position to show it.

Prepare early. Read your file together. Know each other’s lives. Tell the truth. And if anything in your file needs to be explained, explain it before the officer has to ask.
