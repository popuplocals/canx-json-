IRCC’s answer looks simple. On its high-wage occupations page, Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) states that an occupation’s level “is not determined based on a person’s specific job position or salary.” From November 3, 2026, what earns the new Comprehensive Ranking System (CRS) points is your National Occupational Classification (NOC) code, not your paycheque.

But the entire list rests on wages. An occupation makes the list only because people working in it across Canada earn at least 1.3, 1.5, or 2 times the average median wage. So what happens when a candidate claims a high-wage occupation but earns far less than that occupation typically pays? On paper, the salary does not matter. In practice, we believe it will matter a great deal, not as a points rule, but as evidence. This post explains why, and how candidates can prepare.

Quick Summary

IRCC awards high-wage CRS points based on your NOC code, not your salary. However, an officer must still be satisfied that you actually performed the duties of that NOC, and, for job offers, that the offer is genuine. A wage far below what an occupation normally pays will not cost you points on its own, but it may lead an officer to look more closely at whether your job truly fits the NOC you claimed. Candidates in this situation should expect questions and prepare clear evidence of their duties, and a reasonable explanation for their pay, before they apply.

What IRCC Has Actually Said

IRCC’s guidance on high-wage occupations makes three points clear:

  • An occupation counts as high-wage if the median wage of everyone in Canada working in that NOC is at least 1.3 times the average median wage of the Canadian workforce.
  • The level is based on all people in Canada working under the same NOC.
  • It is not determined based on a person’s specific job position or salary.

IRCC has not published any minimum salary that a candidate must earn to receive high-wage points, and it has not said that officers will compare a candidate’s pay with the occupation’s median wage. We should be careful not to read rules into the policy that are not there. At the same time, IRCC has not said that salary is irrelevant to an officer’s overall assessment. That is the gray area.

The Gray Area, Explained With an Example

Consider a hypothetical candidate who claims NOC 60020, retail and wholesale trade managers, a Level 3 high-wage occupation. Assume, for illustration only, that people in this occupation typically earn around $45 an hour, while this candidate earns $25 an hour.

Under the points rules, the candidate’s wage does not change the outcome. If the officer accepts that the candidate worked as a retail and wholesale trade manager for at least 12 months, the candidate receives 20 high-wage points for Canadian work experience.

The real question is whether the officer will accept that the candidate worked in that occupation at all. A pay rate far below the occupation’s typical range can suggest that the role may be closer to a different, lower-paid occupation, such as a supervisory or sales role. The salary does not decide the points, but it can raise doubt about the NOC, and the NOC does decide the points.

Note: The wage figures in this example are illustrative only. Check Job Bank for current wage data for your occupation and region.

Why Officers May Still Look at Your Salary

Officers Assess Duties, Not Titles

The work experience rules for Express Entry have always required more than a job title. Under the regulations, a candidate must have performed the actions described in the lead statement for their NOC and a substantial number of its main duties, including all of the essential duties. Officers assess this using reference letters, contracts, and other evidence.

Pay is one of the ways an officer can test whether the duties described are realistic. A manager’s wage that looks like an entry-level wage may lead the officer to ask whether the candidate truly carried out management duties, such as planning, budgeting, and supervising staff.

The Stakes Are Now Higher

Before November 3, choosing between two similar NOC codes often made no difference to a CRS score. Now it can be worth 20 to 70 points for work experience, or 50 to 100 points for a job offer. When a code carries that much value, officers have good reason to check that the code fits, and candidates have more reason to be accurate.

Job Offers Face a Genuineness Test

For job offer points, IRCC officers must decide whether the offer is genuine. They consider whether the offer reflects the employer’s reasonable employment needs and whether the employer can reasonably meet its terms. A job offer for a high-wage occupation at a wage far below what that occupation normally pays may lead an officer to question whether the offer reflects a real position in that occupation.

Legitimate Reasons a Salary May Be Lower

A lower salary does not mean a claim is false. Medians describe the middle of a range, and many genuine workers earn below the median. Common, legitimate reasons include:

  • Region: Wages vary across provinces and between large cities and smaller communities.
  • Business size: A smaller employer may pay less than a large corporation for the same occupation.
  • Career stage: Someone newer to an occupation often earns less than the median.
  • Compensation structure: Some roles, such as sales management, include commissions, bonuses, or benefits that a base salary does not show.
  • Hours: A part-time or reduced schedule lowers total earnings, although qualifying experience must still add up to the equivalent of 12 months of full-time work.

The issue is not whether a salary is below the median. It is whether the salary, taken together with the duties and the employer’s circumstances, makes sense for the occupation claimed. A modest gap with a clear reason is very different from a wage that only makes sense for a different job.

How Officers May Respond

IRCC has not published instructions on this point, so we cannot say exactly how officers will approach it. Based on how officers assess work experience and job offers today, possible outcomes include:

  • accepting the claim when duties, evidence, and pay together make sense
  • requesting more documents to confirm the duties performed
  • sending a procedural fairness letter that sets out concerns and gives the candidate a chance to respond
  • finding that the experience does not match the NOC claimed, which can remove the points and affect eligibility

If an officer concludes that a candidate deliberately claimed the wrong occupation, the matter could become one of misrepresentation, which carries a five-year ban on applying to come to Canada. That is why accuracy matters more than the number of points.

How to Prepare If Your Salary Is Below the Occupation's Median

  1. Confirm your NOC honestly. Compare your real duties with the lead statement and main duties of your NOC. If another code fits better, use it, even if it earns fewer points.
  2. Check wage data. Look up your occupation on Job Bank for your province and region. Knowing where your pay sits helps you anticipate questions.
  3. Get a detailed reference letter. Ask your employer to describe your duties clearly, including the people you supervise, budgets you manage, and decisions you make.
  4. Show the full picture of your pay. Include commissions, bonuses, and benefits if they form part of your compensation, with supporting records.
  5. Explain the gap, where there is one. A short, factual explanation, for example relating to region, business size, or career stage, can help an officer understand your situation.
  6. Keep every document consistent. Your Express Entry profile, reference letters, pay records, and tax documents should all match.

For Employers Making Job Offers

Employers offering a position for Express Entry points should set wages that are realistic for the occupation and region. Job Bank wage data is a useful reference point. A wage well below the normal range may weaken the offer during the officer’s genuineness review. The wage must also meet provincial employment standards, at minimum.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to earn a high salary to get high-wage CRS points?

No. IRCC states that an occupation’s high-wage level is not based on a person’s specific job position or salary. Points depend on your NOC code. However, you must still show that you actually performed the duties of that NOC, and your salary can be one piece of evidence an officer considers when deciding whether your experience fits the code.

Can an IRCC officer question my salary?

IRCC has not published a specific rule on this. However, officers assess whether candidates performed the duties of their claimed NOC, and whether job offers are genuine. A salary well below what an occupation normally pays could lead an officer to look more closely. Be prepared with clear evidence of your duties and a reasonable explanation for your pay.

What if my salary is lower because I work in a small town?

That can be a legitimate reason. Wages vary by region and employer size, and medians describe the middle of a national range. Many genuine workers earn below the median. Keep evidence of your duties and, where helpful, a short explanation of why your pay reflects your local market or employer.

Should I choose a different NOC code if my salary is low?

You should choose the NOC code that genuinely matches your duties, regardless of points. If your duties match a high-wage occupation, claim it and support it well. If they better match another occupation, use that code. Claiming the wrong NOC to gain points can lead to refusal and, in serious cases, a misrepresentation finding.

What should I do if I receive a procedural fairness letter about my NOC?

Read it carefully and respond within the deadline given. Address each concern directly, with documents such as detailed reference letters, pay records, and organizational charts. A clear, respectful, evidence-based response gives you the best chance. Consider getting help from a licensed immigration consultant or lawyer before you respond.
