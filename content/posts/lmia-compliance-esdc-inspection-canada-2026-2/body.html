Many employers who go through the LMIA process treat compliance as something that matters only during the application stage. They invest heavily in preparing the application, satisfying ESDC’s requirements to obtain the positive LMIA, and then gradually shift their attention back to running the business once the worker arrives. The worker is productive, the paperwork is filed away, and the compliance obligations fade into the background.

This is the posture that ESDC’s inspection program exists specifically to disrupt. The positive LMIA is not the end of ESDC’s involvement with the employer; it is the beginning of a compliance relationship that continues for the full duration of the worker’s employment and for six years after the LMIA is issued. ESDC inspectors can and do show up at workplaces without advance notice. They can interview workers privately. They can demand payroll records, time sheets, written contracts, housing documentation, and any other evidence relevant to the six compliance conditions. And when they find non-compliance, the consequences are real and serious.

This article is the definitive guide to LMIA compliance under the TFWP in 2026. It covers the legal framework governing inspections, the six conditions every employer is assessed against, how inspections are triggered and what they look like in practice, the most common compliance failures identified by ESDC, and the concrete steps employers should take to ensure they can survive an inspection regardless of when it occurs.

The Legal Framework for TFWP Compliance Inspections

ESDC’s authority to conduct compliance inspections is grounded in sections 209.11 to 209.996 of the Immigration and Refugee Protection Regulations (IRPR), which create the employer compliance regime for the TFWP and establish the powers of ESDC officers, the standards against which employers are assessed, and the range of consequences for non-compliance.

The compliance framework was substantially strengthened in 2015 following a series of high-profile cases of employer abuse in the TFWP, and has been incrementally reinforced since then with enhanced inspection authority, higher penalty thresholds, expanded grounds for employer bans, and broader public disclosure of non-compliant employers. In 2026, the compliance regime is more robust and more actively enforced than at any previous point in the program’s history.

Under IRPR 209.11, designated ESDC officers have broad powers to enter employer premises, examine records, interview workers, and collect any information or documents relevant to the employer’s compliance with LMIA conditions. These powers apply without the need for a warrant when the employer consents to the inspection, which ESDC’s inspection protocols effectively make the default in most circumstances. Employers who obstruct or refuse an inspection face additional penalties on top of any substantive compliance findings.

The Six Compliance Conditions Every LMIA Employer Is Assessed Against

The compliance assessment under IRPR 209.3 covers six specific conditions. Every LMIA employer must satisfy all six. A finding of non-compliance with any condition, regardless of how the employer performs against the other five, constitutes a compliance failure and triggers the penalty framework.

Condition 1: Wages

The employer must pay the foreign worker the wage stated in the LMIA and the job offer for the duration of the employment. Wage compliance is the most frequently inspected condition and the one that generates the most findings of non-compliance. Officers review payroll records, bank statements, pay stubs, and T4 records to confirm that the actual wages paid match the wages committed to in the LMIA application.

There are several wage-related violations that appear with regularity. The most straightforward is underpayment: the employer pays the worker less than the committed wage, sometimes starting from the first pay period. A more subtle violation is the clawback: the employer pays the stated wage but then recovers portions of it through illegitimate deductions for tools, uniforms, accommodation at rates above the permissible maximum, immigration fees, or other unauthorized deductions. A third pattern involves wage theft over time: the employer initially pays the correct wage but reduces it after several months without ESDC notification and approval.

ESDC also checks whether the wages paid to the TFW are equal to wages paid to Canadian employees performing the same duties at the same location. If Canadians doing the same job earn more than the TFW, this constitutes a wage compliance failure regardless of whether the TFW wage meets the ESDC median. The equal pay for equal work principle is built into the compliance conditions and is checked in every comprehensive inspection.

Condition 2: Working Conditions

The employer must provide working conditions consistent with those described in the LMIA application and that comply with applicable provincial and territorial employment standards. This condition covers hours of work, overtime entitlements, rest periods, vacation entitlements, statutory holidays, notice periods, and any other conditions of employment governed by provincial employment standards legislation.

Working conditions that were represented in the LMIA application as part of the employment offer but are not actually provided constitute a condition violation. An employer who represented that the position included paid overtime in accordance with provincial standards but then required workers to work overtime hours without compensation has violated this condition. An employer who described a 40-hour work week in the LMIA and then regularly scheduled workers for 55 or 60 hours without overtime pay has violated both the hours and the compensation conditions.

ESDC officers may interview workers privately during an inspection to gather information about actual working conditions. Workers who describe conditions materially different from what the employer’s records show are a significant red flag in the inspection assessment. The discrepancy between what the employer’s records say and what workers report experiencing is, in ESDC’s view, evidence that the employer’s records may be inaccurate or falsified.

Condition 3: Occupation

The employer must employ the foreign worker in the occupation described in the LMIA. A worker hired as a cook under an LMIA for a cook position cannot be deployed primarily as a dishwasher, server, or cleaner. A worker hired as a software engineer cannot be assigned primarily to hardware installation or customer service duties. The occupation condition ensures that the labour market assessment ESDC conducted for the specific position is honoured in the actual employment relationship.

This condition is violated more frequently than employers realize, because the deployment of workers across duties is often driven by operational need rather than deliberate intent to circumvent the LMIA. A restaurant that experiences a sudden shortage of front-of-house staff and asks its TFW cook to spend significant time serving customers is violating the occupation condition even if the employer has no bad intent. The LMIA describes the occupation; the worker must be employed in that occupation for the majority of their working hours.

Material changes to the occupation after LMIA approval require a new LMIA application for the changed position. Employers who need a worker to perform significantly different duties than those described in the LMIA cannot unilaterally reassign the worker and expect ESDC compliance. The proper process is to apply for a new LMIA reflecting the actual duties and for the worker to obtain a new work permit if necessary.

Condition 4: Location of Work

The employer must employ the foreign worker at the work location described in the LMIA. A worker hired to work at an employer’s Toronto facility cannot be regularly deployed to a facility in Hamilton without notification to ESDC and, where required, a new LMIA reflecting the actual work location.

Location compliance is an area that has become more complex with the prevalence of hybrid and remote work arrangements. An LMIA that describes a specific office address as the work location may not clearly accommodate a work-from-home arrangement that was not contemplated at the time of the application. Employers who have shifted TFW workers to remote or hybrid work arrangements since their LMIA was issued should review their compliance position and consider whether a formal amendment or new application is necessary.

Condition 5: Genuine Temporary Resident Status and Information Provided to Workers

This condition encompasses two distinct requirements. First, the employer must provide the foreign worker with accurate information about their rights and responsibilities in Canada. At the time of hiring, the employer is required to provide the worker with a document describing their rights under federal and provincial law, including the right to change employers, the right to access healthcare, the right to contact ESDC or a worker protection organization, and the prohibition on employers charging workers for LMIA costs.

Second, the employer must not engage in conduct that interferes with the worker’s temporary resident status or that prevents the worker from exercising their rights under the program. Confiscating a worker’s passport or immigration documents is an extreme example that is both a criminal offence and a compliance violation. More subtle violations include threatening workers with deportation for raising workplace concerns, preventing workers from contacting government authorities, and coercing workers to sign documents waiving their employment rights.

The rights information document requirement is often overlooked by employers who are focused on the technical aspects of the LMIA and work permit process but do not give sufficient attention to the worker-facing obligations that arise once employment begins. ESDC inspectors ask workers whether they were provided with this information and whether they understood their rights. Employers who cannot demonstrate that this information was provided, and understood, are in violation of this condition.

Condition 6: Employer Legitimacy and Genuine Business

The employer must continue to operate a genuine, legitimate business for the duration of the TFW’s employment. If the employer ceases to operate, is sold, changes ownership in a way that creates a different legal entity, or otherwise ceases to be the employer named in the LMIA, the compliance conditions are affected. A TFW whose employer has ceased to operate effectively has a work permit that no longer reflects a genuine employment situation, which creates compliance issues for both the employer and, potentially, the worker’s immigration status.

Business legitimacy concerns arise in ESDC inspections when the employer’s operational activity appears inconsistent with the employment being offered. A business with minimal revenue and operational activity that has a TFW earning a substantial salary may face questions about whether the employment relationship is genuine. Inspectors may ask for current financial records, client contracts, operational documentation, and other evidence of ongoing business activity.

How ESDC Triggers Inspections: Three Pathways

Pathway 1: Complaint-Driven Inspections

The most common trigger for a targeted ESDC compliance inspection is a complaint. Complaints can come from a range of sources: the TFW themselves, a co-worker (Canadian or foreign), a union representative, a community organization, a medical professional who treated an injured worker, a provincial labour standards office that encountered the worker during their own investigation, or a member of the public. Complaints can be made confidentially, and ESDC takes all credible complaints seriously.

From a practical standpoint, this means that the single most reliable early warning system for an impending ESDC inspection is the state of the employment relationship with the TFW. Workers who feel fairly treated, appropriately compensated, and genuinely supported in their rights are significantly less likely to generate a complaint. The connection between genuine employer compliance and protection from complaint-driven inspections is direct.

The corollary is also true. Employers who are attempting to conceal non-compliance from ESDC by maintaining dual records, coaching workers on what to say during inspections, or retaliating against workers who raise concerns are creating precisely the conditions that generate complaints. Workers in these situations almost always find ways to communicate their concerns to ESDC or to worker support organizations, and these communications trigger exactly the kind of detailed, adversarial inspection that non-compliant employers most fear.

Pathway 2: Random Inspections

ESDC conducts random compliance inspections as part of a proactive monitoring mandate. The randomness is genuine: an employer who has fully complied with every LMIA condition can receive a random inspection notice. The purpose is to create an inspection environment where compliance is maintained not only when employers believe they might be watched, but continuously, because any employer might be selected at any time.

Random inspections tend to be more methodical and less adversarial than complaint-driven inspections, because the inspector arrives with an open file rather than a specific set of allegations to investigate. The scope is typically a review of the six conditions across all active TFW positions at the employer’s location. Employers with complete, organized compliance documentation are able to satisfy random inspections relatively quickly. Those with incomplete records or undocumented practices are exposed to the full scrutiny of a detailed review.

Pathway 3: Risk-Based Inspections

ESDC also conducts inspections based on a risk assessment of specific employer files. Risk factors that elevate the likelihood of a targeted inspection include: previous compliance findings or warnings, industry sector (employers in food service, accommodation, agriculture, and private domestic employment have historically faced higher rates of non-compliance and are subject to heightened monitoring), employer size relative to their TFW workforce proportion, LMIAs applied for in rapid succession suggesting high turnover, complaints that were closed without a full inspection but flagged in the employer’s file, and media reports or labour standards findings from provincial authorities.

Employers who have received any previous contact from ESDC regarding their TFWP compliance, even a minor administrative query, should understand that this contact may have elevated their risk profile and increased the probability of a future inspection. Responding to all ESDC communications promptly, professionally, and completely is both a compliance obligation and a risk management measure.

What an ESDC Compliance Inspection Actually Looks Like

Notice or No Notice?

ESDC inspections can be conducted with or without advance notice. Announced inspections are typically scheduled by letter or phone call, giving the employer a defined date and time for the inspection team’s arrival. Unannounced inspections involve inspectors arriving at the workplace without prior notification, presenting their identification and authority, and requesting immediate access to the premises and records.

The possibility of unannounced inspections is one of the most important reasons why compliance documentation must be maintained continuously rather than assembled in response to a notice. An employer who receives an unannounced inspection and cannot immediately produce payroll records, attendance logs, and worker rights documentation is in a difficult position from the first minutes of the inspection.

The Inspection Process

A standard ESDC compliance inspection follows a structured sequence. Inspectors identify themselves and their authority, explain the scope of the inspection, and request access to the relevant documents. Employers have a legal obligation to cooperate with the inspection and to produce the requested documents. Refusing or obstructing the inspection is itself a separate violation that can result in additional penalties.

Inspectors typically request documents in categories aligned with the six compliance conditions: payroll records for the period of TFW employment, time and attendance records, the original job offer and any subsequent amendments, the LMIA letter, the worker’s work permit, housing documentation (for Low-Wage stream employers), and any written information provided to the worker about their rights. They also typically conduct a workplace walkthrough and may photograph working conditions relevant to the assessment.

Worker interviews are an important part of the compliance inspection process. ESDC inspectors have the authority to interview TFWs privately, meaning without the employer present. The purpose is to gather an unfiltered account of the worker’s experience of the employment conditions. Workers cannot be disciplined or retaliated against for participating in an ESDC inspection interview, and this anti-retaliation protection is explained to workers during the interview process.

What Happens After the Inspection

After the on-site visit, ESDC inspectors prepare an inspection report documenting their findings. This report is reviewed internally and results in one of several possible outcomes: a finding of compliance with no further action, a compliance warning identifying minor deficiencies with a remediation period, a finding of non-compliance with administrative monetary penalties, or a finding of non-compliance with a recommendation for an employer ban from the TFWP.

Employers receive written notification of the inspection outcome and, where findings of non-compliance are made, a detailed description of the conditions found to be violated and the basis for the finding. The penalty framework and the process for responding to penalty assessments are covered in depth in Post 26 of this series.

Preparing for an ESDC Inspection: Proactive Steps Every LMIA Employer Should Take

Maintain a Compliance File for Every TFW Position

The single most effective thing an employer can do to prepare for an ESDC inspection is to maintain a dedicated compliance file for every TFW position that contains all the documents an inspector would ask to see. This file should include the original LMIA letter, the worker’s work permit, the signed job offer, payroll records organized by pay period, time and attendance records, proof that the rights information document was provided to the worker, accommodation documentation (Low-Wage stream), and notes from any workplace changes that were made during the employment period.

Organizing these materials proactively, rather than scrambling to assemble them in response to an inspection notice, serves two purposes. It ensures that the documentation actually exists and is retrievable on short notice, and it reveals in advance any gaps in the compliance record that need to be addressed before an inspector asks for them.

Conduct Internal Compliance Audits

At least annually, and ideally every six months, employers with active TFW positions should conduct an internal compliance audit against the six ESDC conditions. This audit should review payroll records to confirm wages are consistent with the LMIA commitments, confirm that the worker’s duties align with the LMIA occupation, check that working conditions continue to meet provincial employment standards, verify that housing and transportation documentation is current for Low-Wage stream employers, and confirm that no prohibited deductions have been made from the worker’s wages.

Findings from the internal audit should be addressed immediately. An employer who identifies a payroll error during an internal audit and corrects it before an ESDC inspection is in a materially better position than one who discovers the same error when the inspector is reviewing the records. Voluntary correction and documentation of the correction demonstrates genuine commitment to compliance rather than deliberate concealment.

Train HR and Supervisory Staff on TFWP Compliance

Many compliance violations occur not because employers are deliberately non-compliant, but because supervisory staff who make day-to-day decisions about scheduling, task assignment, and payroll are not aware of the LMIA conditions that constrain those decisions. A supervisor who assigns a TFW cook to serving duties for three months because it is operationally convenient is not thinking about ESDC compliance; they are solving an immediate staffing problem. The employer’s LMIA compliance depends on this supervisor understanding that the TFW’s duties must remain consistent with the LMIA occupation.

HR training on TFWP compliance obligations should be part of the onboarding process for any staff member who supervises or makes decisions affecting TFW workers. This training should cover the six compliance conditions, the prohibition on unauthorized wage deductions, the rights information document requirement, the anti-retaliation obligation, and what to do if a TFW raises a workplace concern.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does an ESDC LMIA compliance inspection involve?

An ESDC compliance inspection assesses whether an employer with a positive LMIA is meeting all six compliance conditions: wages, working conditions, occupation, work location, information provided to workers, and business legitimacy. Inspectors can arrive with or without advance notice, request access to all relevant business and payroll records, conduct private interviews with foreign workers, and tour the workplace. After the inspection, ESDC issues a written outcome ranging from a finding of compliance to administrative monetary penalties or an employer ban from the TFWP.

Can ESDC inspect my business without notice?

Yes. ESDC has the authority to conduct unannounced compliance inspections of TFWP employers. Inspectors present their identification and authority upon arrival and request immediate access to the premises and records. Employers have a legal obligation to cooperate with inspections. Refusing or obstructing an inspection is itself a violation that can result in additional penalties. The possibility of unannounced inspections is the primary reason why compliance documentation must be maintained continuously rather than assembled only when a notice is received.

What conditions must employers meet after an LMIA is approved?

Employers must comply with six conditions throughout the worker’s employment: pay the wages stated in the LMIA and job offer; provide working conditions consistent with the LMIA and applicable provincial employment standards; employ the worker in the occupation described in the LMIA; employ the worker at the location described in the LMIA; provide workers with written information about their rights under the program; and continue to operate a genuine, legitimate business. These conditions apply for the full duration of the worker’s employment, not just at the time of the LMIA application.

How long must I keep LMIA compliance records?

Employers must retain all documentation relevant to LMIA compliance conditions for six years from the date of the LMIA issuance. This includes payroll records, time and attendance records, the original LMIA and job offer, housing documentation for Low-Wage stream positions, and proof that the worker rights information document was provided. ESDC inspectors can request records from any point within this six-year retention window during a compliance inspection.

What triggers an ESDC compliance inspection?

ESDC compliance inspections are triggered by three mechanisms: complaint-driven inspections responding to reports from workers, co-workers, unions, community organizations, or provincial labour authorities; random inspections selected from the pool of active TFWP employers as part of proactive monitoring; and risk-based inspections targeting employers whose profiles indicate elevated compliance risk, such as employers with previous compliance findings, employers in high-risk industries, or employers with unusual TFW hiring patterns. Any employer who has received a positive LMIA is subject to inspection through any of these three pathways.
