The technology sector’s relationship with Canadian immigration is qualitatively different from other sectors’ relationship with the TFWP. Restaurants hire foreign workers because Canadians are not taking specific jobs. Construction companies hire foreign tradespeople because the apprenticeship pipeline is insufficient. Technology companies hire foreign workers for a more nuanced reason: they are competing globally for talent that Canada produces in insufficient quantity at the frontier of artificial intelligence, machine learning, quantum computing, cybersecurity, and advanced software engineering.

This distinction, between filling existing jobs that Canadians are not doing and competing globally for world-class talent that Canada needs to attract, is the policy rationale behind the Global Talent Stream. The GTS was not designed to help technology companies avoid the TFWP’s labour market protection requirements. It was designed to recognize that Canada’s economic interest in attracting frontier technology talent is served by faster, less friction-laden immigration than the standard TFWP provides.

Understanding this policy rationale helps technology employers use the GTS correctly and avoid the common mistake of treating it as simply a faster version of the standard LMIA. The GTS has specific eligibility requirements that are tied to this rationale, and applications that do not genuinely reflect the underlying policy intent face scrutiny that the two-week timeline does not protect against.

Step 1 for Every Tech Employer: Evaluate GTS Before Everything Else

Before any technology employer invests time in planning a standard LMIA application, they must answer one question: does this position qualify for the Global Talent Stream? The GTS has a defined In-Demand Occupations list for Category B, which covers the most commonly sought tech roles in Canada, and if the position is on that list, the employer should be using GTS rather than the standard stream.

The cost of defaulting to the standard LMIA stream when GTS applies is measured in months. A standard High-Wage LMIA for a software engineering position takes 8 to 20 weeks to process. A GTS Category B application for the same position targets two weeks. For a technology company that has identified a specific candidate for a product-critical role and has extended a conditional offer of employment, the difference between a two-week and a twenty-week timeline is operationally enormous.

GTS Category B: The In-Demand Occupations List

Category B of the Global Talent Stream allows employers to access two-week LMIA processing for positions that fall on ESDC’s defined In-Demand Occupations list. This list is reviewed and updated periodically and includes a range of technology and innovation roles where Canada has documented difficulty finding sufficient qualified domestic candidates.

As of 2026, the In-Demand Occupations list for GTS Category B has included positions in the following NOC categories. Employers must verify the current list at Canada.ca at the time of application, as occupations may be added, removed, or reclassified:

NOC 21211 — Data scientists

NOC 21220 — Computer and information systems managers

NOC 21230 — Computer engineers (except software engineers and designers)

NOC 21231 — Software engineers and designers

NOC 21232 — Software developers and programmers

NOC 21233 — Web designers

NOC 21234 — Web developers and programmers

NOC 21235 — User experience designers

NOC 21240 — Computer network and web technicians

NOC 21241 — Telecommunications carriers managers

NOC 21300 — Civil engineers

NOC 21310 — Mechanical engineers

NOC 21320 — Electrical and electronics engineers

NOC 21330 — Chemical engineers

NOC 21390 — Other professional engineers

NOC 21222 — Information systems analysts and consultants

NOC 21223 — Database analysts and data administrators

For employers in the artificial intelligence, machine learning, and advanced data science space, most of the core technical roles in their teams will be on this list. For hardware engineering, embedded systems, and specialized manufacturing technology roles, the list coverage is somewhat narrower and requires specific NOC code verification.

GTS Category A: Unique and Specialized Talent

Category A of the GTS is for situations where the position does not fit neatly onto the In-Demand Occupations list because the role is so specialized, cross-disciplinary, or frontier-focused that it cannot be classified under a standard NOC code with the same confidence as Category B positions. Category A requires an endorsement letter from a designated referral organization.

Designated referral organizations for GTS Category A include federal government agencies (the National Research Council, Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada, Canadian Trade Commissioners), provincial economic development bodies, and certain recognized innovation organizations. The referral organization assesses whether the employer’s hiring need and the specific talent being sought genuinely meet the unique and specialized threshold.

Category A is used less frequently than Category B because the referral process adds time and uncertainty to an otherwise fast pathway. Employers who are hiring in genuinely frontier areas, where the worker’s specific capabilities are not represented in standard occupational classifications, should pursue Category A through a designated referral organization. Employers who are using Category A because their occupation is not on the Category B list, but is otherwise a standard technology role, are misusing the pathway and will likely face scrutiny.

The Labour Market Benefits Plan: The GTS Alternative to a Transition Plan

GTS employers do not submit a Transition Plan. Instead, they submit a Labour Market Benefits Plan (LMBP), which serves a forward-looking commitment function but is oriented toward demonstrating positive economic contributions to Canada rather than reducing foreign worker dependence.

Mandatory Commitments

The LMBP requires specific mandatory commitments to either job creation or skills investment for Canadians. Employers must describe a concrete, measurable commitment in one of these categories. A technology company might commit to hiring a defined number of Canadian software developers or data scientists as the business grows, to investing a specific budget in training existing Canadian employees in emerging technology skills, or to creating a partnership with a Canadian university or polytechnic for co-op placements or research collaboration.

Complementary Commitments

In addition to the mandatory element, the LMBP requires complementary commitments in areas such as: increasing wages or benefits for Canadian workers, supporting official languages development, promoting workplace diversity and inclusion, investing in research and development in Canada, or other activities with documented positive impact on Canadian workers or the innovation ecosystem.

Technology employers who are genuinely growing their Canadian teams, investing in training, and building partnerships with Canadian educational institutions find the LMBP requirements easy to align with their existing growth plans. The LMBP for a scaling tech company that is already hiring Canadians and has an existing university relationship essentially documents what the company is already doing. It becomes problematic only for employers who use GTS to access fast processing without genuine commitment to the plan’s activities.

LMBP Review in Subsequent GTS Applications

Like the Transition Plan in the standard TFWP stream, the LMBP is reviewed in subsequent GTS applications from the same employer. If an employer submitted an LMBP in a previous GTS application and is now applying again, ESDC will assess whether the committed activities were implemented. Employers who submit identical plans across multiple GTS applications without reporting on implementation or updating their commitments are signaling to ESDC that the plan is not genuine.

GTS Wage Requirements: Competitive Pay Is Part of the Assessment

GTS employers must pay wages consistent with the prevailing wage for the In-Demand occupation in the province. For most GTS-eligible tech positions, the prevailing wages are substantially above provincial median wages because these are high-skill, high-demand roles. The In-Demand Occupations are by definition occupations where Canada cannot easily find domestic candidates, which means the wage market for these roles is driven by global competition for scarce talent.

An LMIA officer reviewing a GTS application will compare the offered wage against the ESDC Wage Report for the specific NOC in the province and against the broader market context for the role. An employer who claims to be hiring a world-class machine learning engineer but is offering a wage at the bottom of the applicable NOC range will face credibility questions about whether the candidate is genuinely as specialized as claimed and whether the GTS threshold is actually met.

In practice, well-run technology companies hiring through GTS pay wages that are competitive in the global market for the specific skills being hired, which means these wages are comfortably above the ESDC minimums. The wage credibility concern arises primarily for smaller employers who are accessing GTS for cost-conscious hiring rather than because they are genuinely competing for frontier talent.

Standard LMIA for Technology Roles: When It Applies and How to Navigate It

Not every technology hiring situation qualifies for GTS, and even among GTS-eligible situations, some employers choose the standard LMIA stream for specific operational or strategic reasons. Understanding when the standard stream applies and what it looks like for technology positions is essential for complete tech employer LMIA literacy.

When Standard LMIA Applies to Tech Roles

Standard LMIA applies to technology positions where the specific NOC code is not on the GTS In-Demand Occupations list and where the Category A unique and specialized threshold is not met. Technology positions in customer support, IT helpdesk, basic database administration, routine QA testing, and similar roles that, while tech-adjacent, do not reflect the frontier technical skills the GTS targets may fall into this category.

It also applies when an employer has a qualifying tech role but has not been able to obtain a Category A referral, or where the employer for operational reasons prefers the standard LMIA process. Some multinationals with established TFWP relationships and well-developed internal application processes continue to use standard LMIA for positions that might qualify for GTS, because the additional administrative steps of GTS registration and LMBP preparation are not worth the processing time advantage for their specific operational model.

Standard Tech LMIA: The High-Wage Stream Default

Most technology positions in the standard LMIA stream are High-Wage relative to provincial medians, meaning they require a Transition Plan rather than an Accommodation Plan. The Transition Plan for a technology employer should focus on activities that develop Canadian technical talent: hiring Canadian computer science or engineering co-op students, sponsoring professional development certifications for Canadian staff, partnering with coding bootcamps or university programs, or committing to apprenticeship-style mentorship for Canadian junior developers under the supervision of experienced foreign staff.

Standard tech LMIA applications also require the full recruitment advertising campaign on Job Bank and at least two additional platforms appropriate for the technical occupation. Technology-specific platforms are expected: LinkedIn with appropriate targeting, Stack Overflow Jobs, GitHub Jobs (where active), and domain-specific boards for the particular technology specialty. An employer hiring a machine learning engineer who advertises only on a general job board without any ML-specific channels will face questions about the thoroughness of their Canadian recruitment effort.

Worker Benefits Under GTS: Why the Pathway Matters for Tech Talent

Expedited Work Permit Processing

The two-week GTS processing target applies not only to the LMIA but to the associated work permit application under IRCC’s Global Skills Strategy. A tech worker hired through GTS can typically expect their work permit to be processed within two weeks of a complete application submission. The combined effect is that a GTS hire can be authorized to work in Canada in as little as four to six weeks from the date a complete application package is submitted, compared to the five to nine months that a standard TFWP process typically takes.

Spousal Work Permits

Spouses of GTS workers may be eligible for open work permits under the Global Skills Strategy’s family facilitation provisions, allowing the spouse to work for any employer in Canada without an LMIA. For technology workers who are partnered with other professionals, this spousal work authorization is a significant lifestyle consideration that makes Canada more attractive as a destination relative to countries with more restrictive spousal employment provisions.

PR Pathways Through Express Entry

GTS positions are almost universally in NOC TEER 0, 1, or 2 categories, qualifying workers for the Federal Skilled Worker Program and, after one year of Canadian work experience, the Canadian Experience Class. Category-based Express Entry draws have targeted technology workers in specific draws at CRS thresholds below the general pool minimum, creating genuine pathways to PR for GTS workers that bypass the competitive general Express Entry environment. A technology worker on a GTS work permit who actively maintains an Express Entry profile and monitors category-based draw announcements is in one of the strongest positions in the Canadian immigration landscape for moving from temporary status to permanent residence.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a software developer need an LMIA to work in Canada?

It depends on the pathway. Software developers (NOC 21232) who are US or Mexican citizens may qualify for a CUSMA work permit without any LMIA. Software developers being transferred from a multinational’s foreign affiliate may qualify for an intra-company transfer (C10) exemption. For all others, an LMIA is required. Software developers typically qualify for the Global Talent Stream (GTS) Category B In-Demand Occupations list, which offers two-week processing rather than the standard 8 to 20 weeks. Employers should always evaluate GTS before defaulting to the standard LMIA stream for software developer positions.

What is the Global Talent Stream occupation list for 2026?

The GTS Category B In-Demand Occupations list for 2026 includes software engineers and designers (NOC 21231), software developers and programmers (NOC 21232), data scientists (NOC 21211), computer and information systems managers (NOC 21220), web designers and developers (NOC 21233, 21234), user experience designers (NOC 21235), database analysts (NOC 21223), and several engineering disciplines (NOC 21230, 21320, and others). The complete current list must be verified at Canada.ca at the time of application, as ESDC reviews and updates the list periodically. Confirming that your specific NOC code is currently on the list before submitting is mandatory.

How long does a GTS LMIA take compared to standard?

The Global Talent Stream targets two-week LMIA processing from receipt of a complete application. IRCC also targets two-week processing for the associated work permit under the Global Skills Strategy. Standard TFWP LMIA applications for technology positions take approximately 8 to 20 weeks. The combined total timeline for a GTS hire, from complete application submission to the worker receiving a work permit, is typically four to six weeks. For a standard LMIA route for the same position, the total timeline from application to work permit is typically five to nine months.

What does a Labour Market Benefits Plan need to contain for GTS?

The LMBP must include mandatory commitments to either job creation or skills investment for Canadians (for example, committing to hire a specified number of Canadian employees in the target role category, or investing a specified amount in training existing Canadian staff in advanced technology skills), and complementary commitments in additional benefit areas such as wage increases for Canadian staff, diversity initiatives, official language development, or R&D investment. Both elements must be specific, measurable, and realistic for the employer’s size and business model. The LMBP is reviewed in subsequent GTS applications; employers must be prepared to report on implementation of previous commitments.

Can a startup use the Global Talent Stream?

Yes, provided the startup’s position meets the eligibility criteria for Category B (on the In-Demand Occupations list) or Category A (unique and specialized talent with a designated organization endorsement). Startups using Category A must navigate the referral organization process, which can include engagement with Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada, provincial economic development bodies, or recognized innovation accelerators. For early-stage startups, the Category A referral process may involve demonstrating the business’s growth potential and the strategic importance of the specific talent being sought, not just the vacancy. Many Canadian technology accelerators and incubators have relationships with GTS-designated organizations that can facilitate this process.
