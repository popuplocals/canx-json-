Since IRCC announced that Express Entry job offers will no longer need a Labour Market Impact Assessment (LMIA) from November 3, 2026, one debate has dominated conversations among employers, candidates, and practitioners. Does an employer need to advertise a job, and try to hire Canadians, before making an Express Entry job offer to a foreign national?

One side says no: IRCC has not asked for advertising, so an employer can simply write a job offer, skip the cost of recruitment, and let the candidate claim the points. The other side says that a job offer with no recruitment behind it, made to someone who has never worked for the employer, looks like exactly what it may be: a paper job offer with no connection to the real labour market. Both sides have a point. This post sets out what the law actually requires, where the gray areas lie, and why an offer made with no recruitment history, to a person the employer has never employed, is a red flag that employers and candidates should take seriously.

Quick Summary

IRCC has not published a requirement for employers to advertise before making an Express Entry job offer, and outside the LMIA process there is no general legal rule in Canada requiring employers to advertise jobs. However, IRCC officers must assess whether each job offer is genuine, including whether it reflects the employer’s reasonable employment needs and whether the employer complies with federal and provincial employment and recruitment laws. Several provinces regulate how employers recruit foreign nationals. An offer to someone the employer has never employed, with no recruitment process behind it, may lead an officer to question whether the job is real. Advertising is not mandatory, but a documented, genuine hiring process is the strongest protection.

What IRCC Actually Requires

IRCC’s October 16, 2026 backgrounder lists the factors an officer must consider when deciding whether a job offer is genuine:

  • whether the employer is actively engaged in the business for which the offer is made
  • whether the offer reflects the employer’s reasonable employment needs
  • whether the employer can reasonably meet the terms of the offer
  • whether the candidate is qualified, able to perform the work, and likely to accept and carry out the employment
  • whether the employer has complied with applicable federal and provincial employment and recruitment laws

Notice what is on the list and what is not. There is no requirement to advertise, no minimum recruitment period, and no test of whether a Canadian was available. At the same time, the list includes compliance with recruitment laws and a test of whether the offer reflects a real employment need. That combination is where the debate begins.

Where the Advertising Requirement Came From

The rule that employers must advertise and try to hire Canadians first comes from the Temporary Foreign Worker Program. To get an LMIA, employers generally had to advertise the position for a set period, use several recruitment methods, and show that no qualified Canadian or permanent resident was available. The LMIA existed precisely to test whether hiring a foreign worker would harm the Canadian labour market, and the principle behind it was that foreign workers are a last resort.

That test belongs to the LMIA process. When IRCC removed the LMIA from Express Entry job offers, it also removed the formal labour market test and the advertising rules that came with it. IRCC has not replaced them with a recruitment requirement of its own. So, strictly speaking, the argument that advertising is legally required for an Express Entry job offer is not supported by any published IRCC rule.

Recruitment Laws Still Apply

That does not mean recruitment is unregulated. The genuineness test specifically asks whether the employer has complied with federal and provincial employment and recruitment laws. Several provinces have laws that govern how employers and recruiters deal with foreign nationals. Examples include British Columbia’s Temporary Foreign Worker Protection Act, Manitoba’s Worker Recruitment and Protection Act, Saskatchewan’s Foreign Worker Recruitment and Immigration Services Act, and Ontario’s Employment Protection for Foreign Nationals Act, along with provisions in other provinces.

These laws differ, but depending on the province and the circumstances, they may:

  • require employers to register with the province before recruiting foreign nationals
  • require recruiters and immigration representatives involved in recruitment to be licensed
  • prohibit charging foreign workers fees for finding or securing a job
  • prohibit recovering recruitment costs from workers

Whether a specific provincial law applies to a particular Express Entry job offer depends on its wording and the facts. But employers should not assume that the absence of an LMIA means the absence of rules. Check the requirements in your province before you make an offer to a foreign national.

The Real Issue: Is the Job Offer Genuine?

Even where no law requires advertising, the way a job offer came about can still tell an officer a great deal about whether it is real. In a normal labour market, employers who need staff look for them. They post jobs, ask for referrals, use recruiters, review applications, and hold interviews. A job offer that appears with none of this, made to someone the employer has never worked with, raises obvious questions:

  • How did the employer find this candidate?
  • Did the employer look for anyone else?
  • Why was this person chosen over everyone else in the labour market?
  • Does the business actually need this role filled, or does the offer exist to generate CRS points?

None of these questions has a single right answer. But a candidate and employer who cannot answer them convincingly may struggle to satisfy an officer that the offer reflects a reasonable employment need.

Two Very Different Scenarios

An Existing Employee: A Natural Fit

When an employer offers a permanent position to someone who already works for it, the recruitment question largely answers itself. The employer knows the candidate’s work, the role exists, and the relationship is documented through payroll, tax records, and work history. An officer is unlikely to expect the employer to advertise a job it intends to keep giving to a valued worker.

A New Hire With No Recruitment Trail: A Red Flag

When an employer offers a high-wage position to a foreign national who has never worked for it, and there is no posting, no interview record, no referral, and no explanation of how the two came together, the offer starts to look like a paper exercise. It may be entirely legal on its face, but it gives the officer little reason to believe the job is real. This is the situation most likely to draw requests for further information or a procedural fairness letter.

The Arguments on Both Sides

The Case Against Requiring Advertising

  • IRCC has not published any advertising requirement for Express Entry job offers.
  • The government’s stated purpose is to cut red tape and make job offers simpler and more efficient.
  • Outside the LMIA process, Canadian employers are generally free to hire whomever they choose, through any lawful method.
  • Advertising can be costly and time-consuming, especially for small businesses.

The Case for Showing Genuine Recruitment

  • Officers must assess whether the offer reflects a reasonable employment need, and a real hiring process is strong evidence of that need.
  • Compliance with recruitment laws is an explicit genuineness factor.
  • Job offers can now be worth up to 100 CRS points, so officers have strong reasons to test them carefully.
  • A paper job offer that fails can cost the candidate the points, and possibly the application, and can lead to a finding of misrepresentation.

Our View

Advertising is not a legal requirement for an Express Entry job offer, and we will not tell clients otherwise. But a genuine, documented hiring process is the best evidence that a job offer is real. For existing employees, that evidence already exists. For new hires, employers should be able to show how they found the candidate and why they chose them. Advertising is one good way to do that, but not the only one.

How Employers Can Protect a Job Offer

  1. Document how you found the candidate. A job posting, a referral, a recruiter, a university career fair, or a professional network are all legitimate routes. Keep records.
  2. Keep a record of your selection process. Interview notes, assessments, and reference checks show that you chose the candidate on merit.
  3. Explain the business need. A short memo on why the role exists and why it needs filling now helps an officer understand the offer.
  4. Comply with provincial rules. Register with your province if required, use only licensed recruiters, and never charge or pass recruitment costs to a worker.
  5. Consider advertising. It is not required, but a posting on Job Bank or another job board is a simple, low-cost way to show a real hiring process.
  6. Pay a fair wage. A wage in line with the local market supports the genuineness of the offer.

What Candidates Should Watch For

  • An employer who offers you a job without ever interviewing you or checking your qualifications.
  • Anyone who asks you to pay for a job offer, or to repay recruitment costs.
  • An offer that promises CRS points or an invitation, rather than a real job.
  • An employer with no clear business need for the role.

If something feels like a shortcut, treat it as a warning. A finding of misrepresentation can bar you from applying to come to Canada for five years.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do employers need to advertise a job before making an Express Entry job offer?

IRCC has not published any advertising requirement for Express Entry job offers under the new rules. However, officers assess whether each offer is genuine, including whether it reflects a reasonable employment need and whether the employer complies with recruitment laws. A documented hiring process, which may include advertising, is strong evidence that an offer is real.

Does the "Canadians first" rule still apply without an LMIA?

The formal requirement to show that no Canadian or permanent resident was available is part of the LMIA process. IRCC has not applied that test to Express Entry job offers from November 3, 2026. However, officers will still look at whether the offer reflects the employer’s genuine needs, and the way the employer found the candidate can be part of that picture.

Is a job offer to someone who has never worked for the employer a problem?

Not automatically. Employers hire new people all the time. But an offer to a new hire with no recruitment record, no interview, and no explanation of how the employer found them may raise questions about whether the job is real. Keep clear records of how the candidate was found and selected.

What provincial recruitment laws might apply to my job offer?

Several provinces, including British Columbia, Manitoba, Saskatchewan, and Ontario, have laws governing the recruitment of foreign nationals. Depending on the province and circumstances, they may require employer registration, licensed recruiters, and a ban on charging workers fees. Check the rules in your province, or speak with a licensed consultant, before making an offer.

Can I pay someone to get an Express Entry job offer?

No. Paying for a job offer is a serious red flag, and many provincial laws prohibit charging workers fees to find or secure a job. An offer obtained this way is unlikely to be considered genuine, and relying on it could lead to a finding of misrepresentation and a five-year ban on applying to come to Canada.
