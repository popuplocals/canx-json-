Here is a question we are hearing more and more since IRCC announced its Express Entry Canada 2026 reforms. A candidate works in Canada in an occupation that is not on the list of 186 high-wage occupations. They do not want to change their NOC code, and they should not. But they have received, or hope to receive, a job offer in one of the listed occupations. Can they claim the job offer points?

The short answer is yes, it is legally possible. IRCC does not require your job offer to be in the same occupation as your current or past work. But it is also one of the most closely examined situations under the new rules. From November 3, 2026, the officer reviewing your application must be satisfied that the job offer is genuine, and that you are qualified for it, able to do it, and likely to accept it. If your work history points in a different direction, you will need strong evidence to show why the offer makes sense.

Quick Summary

You can claim job offer points for a qualifying offer in a high-wage occupation even if you currently work in a different, unlisted occupation. Your work history keeps its correct NOC code, and the offer is assessed on its own. The core test is simple: you must meet the requirements of the offered position. IRCC officers will ask whether you are qualified, able to perform the work, and likely to accept it. If you hold an employer-specific work permit, you generally cannot start the new job before permanent residence without new work authorization, but the offer itself is for work after you become a permanent resident.

Why This Situation Is Legal

Under the new rules, a qualifying job offer in a high-wage occupation can earn 100, 70, or 50 CRS points, depending on the occupation’s level. Nothing in IRCC’s announcement requires the offer to match your current occupation. IRCC’s job offer guidance states that, from November 3, 2026, job offers can be for an existing or a new position, with any employer, and that officers will assess whether each offer is genuine.

This also reflects how careers really work. People retrain, earn new degrees, return to their original profession after working in survival jobs, or get promoted into new roles. A candidate who worked as a software developer in their home country, but has worked in a warehouse since arriving in Canada, may be fully qualified for a software developer position. The law does not lock candidates into the occupation they happen to hold today.

Your Work History and Your Job Offer Are Separate

In your Express Entry profile, your work history and your job offer are recorded separately. Your current job keeps its correct NOC code, based on your actual duties. The job offer carries its own NOC code, based on the duties of the offered position. You do not need to change, and should not change, the NOC code of work you have already done.

This matters for points too. Your current, unlisted experience still earns regular Canadian work experience points. The job offer can add 50 to 100 points on top of that. Because you have no high-wage work experience, the rule that job offer and work experience points do not stack works in your favour: the job offer points are simply the higher option.

The Core Test: Do You Meet the Requirements of the Offered Job?

Everything comes back to one question: can you actually do this job? IRCC’s backgrounder says the officer must determine whether the candidate is qualified and able to perform the work and is likely to accept and carry out the employment. In practice, officers are likely to look at:

  • Education: Does your education match what the job normally requires? A data scientist offer will look stronger with a degree in data science, statistics, or computer science.
  • Past work experience: Have you done this type of work before, in Canada or abroad? Foreign experience in the same occupation can be powerful evidence.
  • Licensing or certification: If the job is regulated, are you licensed, or likely to qualify for a licence? For a job offer to count, officers must be satisfied you can meet the licensing requirements.
  • Language ability: Does your language level fit the communication demands of the role?
  • The employer’s own requirements: Do you meet the qualifications the employer itself would expect for anyone in this position?

The bigger the gap between your current work and the offered job, the more evidence you need. A candidate moving from a related role into a more senior or specialized one tells a natural story. A candidate with no relevant background moving into a senior professional role tells a story an officer is likely to question.

Plausible Scenarios Versus Red Flags

Scenarios That Can Make Sense

  • A candidate with a computer science degree and three years as a software developer abroad, now working in Canadian retail, receives a software developer offer.
  • An internationally trained accountant working in an administrative role while completing Canadian accounting requirements receives an offer as a financial auditor and accountant.
  • A licensed electrician from abroad, working as a general labourer while obtaining provincial certification, receives an industrial electrician offer.
  • A candidate who completed a Canadian master’s degree in data analytics, while working part-time in hospitality, receives a data scientist offer.

Situations Likely to Raise Questions

  • A candidate with no management experience receives a senior management offer from a very small business.
  • A candidate with no relevant education or experience receives an offer in a technical or professional occupation.
  • The offer comes from a friend, relative, or a business connected to the candidate, with little evidence of a real need for the role.
  • The candidate paid money to obtain the offer.
  • The offered wage is far below what the occupation normally pays.

Paying for a job offer is never acceptable and should stop you immediately. The other warning signs do not automatically mean an offer will fail, but each one puts more weight on your evidence.

If You Hold an Employer-Specific Work Permit

Many candidates in this situation hold employer-specific work permits, often based on a Labour Market Impact Assessment (LMIA). This raises an important legal distinction between the job offer and your current work authorization.

The Job Offer Is for Work After Permanent Residence

A qualifying Express Entry job offer must generally be for full-time, continuous work for at least one year after you become a permanent resident. You do not need to be working in the offered job today to claim the points. The offer is about your future employment as a permanent resident.

You Cannot Simply Start the New Job Now

An employer-specific work permit generally authorizes you to work only for the employer, and in the occupation, named on the permit. Starting work in the new occupation, or for a new employer, before you have proper authorization would be unauthorized work, which can seriously harm your immigration status and future applications. If you want to start the new job before permanent residence, the new employer will generally need to support a new work permit, either through an LMIA or an LMIA-exempt category. In some cases, candidates who have submitted a complete permanent residence application may be eligible for other work permit options. Check IRCC’s current rules for your situation.

Staying in Your Current Job Is Often the Safer Path

For many candidates, the cleanest route is to keep working legally in their current job, claim the job offer points in their Express Entry profile, and start the new job after they become permanent residents. The officer will still need to believe you genuinely intend to take the job, so your employer and the offer must be real, and your plans consistent.

The Legal Analysis

  • Genuineness factors: IRCC officers must assess whether the employer is actively engaged in its business, whether the offer reflects the employer’s reasonable employment needs, whether the employer can meet the terms, whether the candidate is qualified and likely to carry out the work, and whether the employer complies with employment and recruitment laws.
  • Intention to accept: If you claim job offer points with no real intention of taking the job, that undermines the offer’s genuineness and could amount to misrepresentation.
  • Misrepresentation: Under the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act, misrepresenting a material fact can lead to a five-year ban on applying to come to Canada. A job offer that earns your invitation is clearly material.
  • Revocation: IRCC has said a revoked job offer no longer qualifies. If losing the job offer points drops your score below the cut-off of the round in which you were invited, your application could be refused.
  • Program eligibility: A temporary public policy removes LMIA requirements for job offers used to establish Federal Skilled Worker or Federal Skilled Trades eligibility, for candidates invited on or after November 3, 2026, who meet its conditions. You must still meet every other program requirement.

How to Build a Strong Case

  1. Compare yourself with the job requirements. Read the NOC description and the employer’s job posting. List exactly how your education, experience, and credentials meet each requirement.
  2. Gather proof of your qualifications. Degrees, transcripts, Educational Credential Assessments, licences, certifications, and reference letters from past roles in the same occupation.
  3. Ask the employer for supporting documents. Business registration, financial records, an organizational chart, and a short explanation of why the business needs the role and why you were chosen.
  4. Explain the transition. A brief, factual explanation of why you are currently working in a different field, and how you are qualified for the offered role, helps an officer understand your story.
  5. Keep your current work legal. Do not start the new job until you are authorized to do so.
  6. Update your profile before November 3. Add the job offer details if you want them counted when IRCC recalculates scores.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I claim job offer points in an occupation I don't currently work in?

Yes. IRCC does not require your job offer to be in the same occupation as your current work. However, the officer must be satisfied that you are qualified and able to perform the offered job, and that you are likely to accept it. The further the offer is from your background, the stronger your evidence needs to be.

Do I need to change my NOC code to claim the job offer points?

No. Your work history keeps the NOC codes that match the work you actually did. The job offer is recorded separately with its own NOC code. Changing the code for past work to match the offer, when your duties did not change, could be treated as misrepresentation.

I have an employer-specific work permit. Can I accept a job offer from another employer?

You can accept an offer for work that starts after you become a permanent resident and claim the points. But you generally cannot start working for the new employer, or in a new occupation, until you have new work authorization, often through an LMIA or an LMIA-exempt work permit. Working without authorization can seriously harm your status and applications.

What if I have experience in the offered occupation from my home country?

That can be strong evidence that you are qualified. Provide detailed reference letters describing your duties, dates, and hours, along with proof of your education and any licences. Foreign experience in the same occupation helps show that the offer makes sense, even if your current Canadian job is different.

Will an officer question why I am not already working in that occupation?

They may. Officers assess whether you are qualified and likely to accept the job. A short, honest explanation, such as completing Canadian licensing, adjusting after arrival, or permit restrictions, can help. Supporting documents matter more than the explanation itself, so make sure your qualifications are well documented.
