On paper, the November 3, 2026 changes to Express Entry Canada 2026 look like a gift. A National Occupational Classification (NOC) code on IRCC’s list of 186 high-wage occupations can add up to 70 Comprehensive Ranking System (CRS) points for Canadian work experience, or up to 100 for a qualifying job offer. For a candidate stuck just below the cut-off, that can look like the difference between waiting and an invitation.

So it is no surprise that many candidates whose occupations are not on the list are now asking a tempting question: can I just change my NOC code? Some are thinking about relabelling their current job. Others are thinking about switching careers into a listed occupation. Before you change anything in your profile, you need to understand the difference between a legitimate change and a dangerous one, because the consequences of getting it wrong are far more serious than missing out on points.

Quick Summary

Your NOC code must reflect the work you actually do, not the points you want. Correcting a genuinely wrong code, or genuinely moving into a new occupation, is legitimate. Changing your code to claim high-wage points for work you did not do is misrepresentation, which can lead to refusal and a five-year ban on applying to come to Canada. Even a genuine career change has costs: you need at least 12 months of eligible Canadian experience in the new occupation before you earn the new work experience points. Why Candidates Are Tempted

The new points are large, and the list is broad. Many common jobs sit right next to listed occupations, sometimes in the same field. A construction electrician (72200) is not on the list, but an industrial electrician (72201) is. A carpenter is not, but a carpentry contractor or supervisor (72013) is. A web designer is not, but a web developer (21234) is. A retail salesperson is not, but a retail and wholesale trade manager (60020) is.

When the gap between two codes is one job title and 20 to 70 points, it is easy to see why candidates might want to move across. But IRCC does not award points for titles. It awards points for occupations, and it checks.

Three Very Different Situations

Not every NOC change is the same. It helps to separate three situations:

1. Correcting a Genuinely Wrong Code: Legitimate

Some candidates chose the wrong NOC code when they created their profile, often because they relied on a job title instead of the duties. If your actual duties clearly match a different code, correcting it is the right thing to do, whether or not the correct code is on the high-wage list. The key test is the one IRCC has always used: your duties must match the lead statement of the NOC and a substantial number of its main duties, including all of the essential duties.

2. Genuinely Moving Into a New Occupation: Legitimate, but Slow

If you take on a new job, or a promotion, in a listed occupation, your new experience can count toward the high-wage points. This is a real and legitimate strategy. But it takes time, because you need at least 12 months of eligible Canadian work experience in high-wage occupations to earn the work experience points.

3. Relabelling Your Current Job to Claim Points: High Risk

If your duties have not changed, and you change your NOC code only to claim high-wage points, you are claiming experience you do not have. That is not a profile update. It is a misrepresentation, and it can have serious, long-lasting consequences.

The Pros of a Genuine NOC Change

  • More CRS points: A listed occupation can add 20 to 70 points for Canadian work experience, or 50 to 100 for a qualifying job offer.
  • Better long-term career value: Listed occupations, by definition, have higher median wages across Canada.
  • Wider job offer options: From November 3, 2026, job offers in listed occupations no longer need a Labour Market Impact Assessment (LMIA) to earn CRS points.
  • Protection once invited: If IRCC updates the high-wage list after you receive an Invitation to Apply (ITA), it keeps the level that applied on the date you were invited.

The Cons and Hidden Costs

  • The 12-month clock: The new work experience points require at least 12 months in high-wage occupations. A career change today may not pay off in points for a year or more.
  • Permit limits: If you hold an employer-specific work permit, you generally cannot simply change jobs or occupations. You may need a new permit first.
  • Eligibility shifts: Your NOC code affects more than points. It can affect your program eligibility, such as the TEER level needed for the Canadian Experience Class, and which category-based draws you qualify for. A change could close one door while opening another.
  • Licensing barriers: Many listed occupations are regulated. Moving into them may require provincial licensing or certification before you can legally work.
  • No points for the old job: Experience in your previous, unlisted occupation will still earn regular CRS points, but it will not count toward the high-wage months.

The Risk Factors: What Officers Can See

Changing a dropdown in your profile is easy. Proving the change is not. When you apply for permanent residence, an IRCC officer may review:

  • Reference letters: Your employer’s description of your duties must match the NOC you claim.
  • Pay records and tax documents: Job titles, employers, and earnings should line up with your claim. As we explained in a previous post, a wage far below what an occupation normally pays can prompt closer questions.
  • Your immigration history: IRCC may compare your claim with earlier applications, such as work permit applications, Labour Market Impact Assessments, or previous Express Entry profiles. A sudden change in occupation with no change in employer or duties can stand out.
  • Your employer: Officers can contact employers to verify duties and positions.

If the evidence does not support the occupation you claimed, the officer can refuse the points. If your score without those points falls below the cut-off of the round in which you were invited, your application could be refused.

The Biggest Risk: Misrepresentation

Under Canada’s Immigration and Refugee Protection Act, misrepresenting or withholding material facts in an application can make a person inadmissible. A finding of misrepresentation carries a five-year ban on applying to come to Canada. It can also follow you into future applications and damage your credibility with immigration authorities.

A false NOC claim is a classic example of a material fact, because it directly affects your CRS score and whether you receive an invitation. Unlike a simple mistake in a minor detail, claiming high-wage experience you did not have goes to the heart of the application. No number of extra points is worth that risk.

A Safer Way to Decide

  1. Read the NOC description carefully. Compare your real, day-to-day duties with the lead statement and main duties of both your current code and the code you are considering.
  2. Ask whether your duties have changed. If they have not, your code should not change either.
  3. Check your documents. Would your reference letter, pay stubs, and tax records support the new code? If not, stop.
  4. Look at your full profile. Consider how a change affects your program eligibility, category draws, and work permit conditions.
  5. Plan a genuine move if it fits. If a listed occupation suits your skills and career goals, build real experience in it, and claim the points when you have earned them.
  6. Get professional advice. A licensed consultant can review your duties and documents before you make any change.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I change my NOC code in my Express Entry profile?

Yes, you can update your profile, and you should correct a code that does not match your actual duties. However, any NOC code you claim must be supported by evidence of the duties you performed. Changing your code only to gain high-wage points, without any change in your work, can lead to refusal and a finding of misrepresentation.

What happens if I claim the wrong NOC code?

If an officer finds that your experience does not match the NOC you claimed, they can refuse the related points. If your score then falls below the cut-off of your invitation round, your application could be refused. If the officer finds you deliberately misrepresented your occupation, you could face a five-year ban on applying to come to Canada.

If I start a new job in a listed occupation, when do I get the points?

You need at least 12 months of eligible Canadian work experience in high-wage occupations to earn the new work experience points. Your experience in the new role counts from when you start performing its duties. If you have a qualifying job offer in a listed occupation, you may be able to claim job offer points sooner.

My job title matches a listed occupation. Is that enough?

No. IRCC assesses your duties, not your title. Many job titles appear under several NOC codes. Your duties must match the lead statement and a substantial number of the main duties of the code you claim, including all essential duties. Check the official NOC description and make sure your reference letter reflects your real work.

Can I claim both my old NOC and my new NOC?

You can list each job you have held with its own correct NOC code. Your experience in an unlisted occupation will still earn regular CRS points. Only your experience in listed occupations counts toward the 12 months needed for high-wage points.
