Receiving an open work permit extension refusal can feel frightening because it affects more than one immigration document. For many people, it affects employment, income, family stability, rent, future permanent residence plans, and the ability to remain in Canada legally. The refusal letter may look short, but the consequences can feel very heavy.

The most important thing is not to panic or rush into another application without understanding what happened. A refusal does not always mean every option is gone. In many cases, applicants may still have options such as restoration of status, changing to visitor status, reapplying under a stronger category, correcting eligibility issues, or reviewing whether the officer made an error.

Understand What Was Refused

The first step is identifying the exact type of open work permit extension that was refused. An applicant may have applied for a spousal open work permit, bridging open work permit, post-graduation-related public policy permit, open work permit under a pilot program, vulnerable worker open work permit, or another temporary public policy category.

This matters because each category has different eligibility rules. A refusal in one category does not automatically mean the person cannot qualify under another category. For example, a refused spousal open work permit may involve problems with the spouse’s eligibility, while a refused bridging open work permit may involve timing, permanent residence stage, or status issues.

Check Your Status Immediately

After refusal, the most urgent question is whether you still have valid temporary resident status in Canada. If the extension was filed before the previous permit expired, the applicant may have had maintained status while waiting for a decision. Once the extension is refused, maintained status usually ends, and the person must carefully assess next steps. IRCC explains that maintained status may allow a person to stay in Canada until a decision is made on the new work permit application, provided the requirements are met. (IRCC)

This is where many people make mistakes. Some continue working because they believe they are still covered, while others leave Canada unnecessarily without checking whether restoration or another option exists. Status, work authorization, and future eligibility must be reviewed separately.

Understand the 90-Day Restoration Option

In many situations, a person who loses temporary resident status may apply to restore status within 90 days of losing it. IRCC’s worker guide states that a person may be able to restore status as a visitor, student, or worker within 90 days if they lost status because they failed to comply with certain conditions. (Canada)

Restoration is often one of the most important options after refusal, but it is not automatic approval. The applicant must still show why restoration should be granted and must submit the correct application with proper explanation and supporting documents.

Restoration Does Not Usually Let You Keep Working

This is one of the biggest misunderstandings. Many applicants believe that once they submit a restoration application, they can continue working while waiting for a decision. That is usually incorrect.

IRCC states that a person who applies to restore a work permit may stay in Canada while waiting for further instructions, but must stop working until the restored work permit is issued. (IRCC) This distinction is critical because unauthorized work can create serious issues in future immigration applications.

Consider Changing Status to Visitor

If the applicant no longer qualifies for an open work permit, changing status to visitor may be a practical option. Visitor status does not allow work, but it may help the person remain in Canada legally while reviewing future options, waiting for a spouse’s eligibility to change, preparing a new work permit pathway, or deciding whether to leave Canada.

This option is often useful where the work permit category is no longer available or where reapplying immediately would likely lead to another refusal. However, the applicant must be financially prepared because visitor status does not permit employment.

Review Whether You Can Reapply

A refusal does not always prevent a fresh application. In many cases, applicants can reapply if they still qualify or if the weakness in the refused application can be corrected.

The key is not to resubmit the same file. A reapplication should address the refusal reason directly, include stronger evidence, clarify eligibility, correct inconsistencies, and explain any change in circumstances. If the underlying problem still exists, another refusal is likely.

If It Was a Spousal Open Work Permit Refusal

Spousal open work permit refusals often happen because the applicant focuses only on the relationship and ignores the principal applicant’s eligibility. The spouse or partner may need to prove that the principal applicant meets the current requirements, including their work permit category, employment situation, study program, or pathway to permanent residence.

Relationship evidence also matters. IRCC’s instructions for family members of foreign workers state that applicants must provide documents proving eligibility, including proof of relationship for spouses and common-law partners. (Canada) A weak marriage or common-law evidence package can lead to refusal even where the relationship is genuine.

If It Was a Bridging Open Work Permit Refusal

A bridging open work permit refusal often happens because the applicant applied too early, applied under the wrong permanent residence category, did not have the correct acknowledgement or processing stage, or did not meet status requirements. IRCC confirms that a BOWP lets certain permanent residence applicants keep working while waiting for a decision, but eligibility depends on the PR program and other requirements. (Canada)

Applicants should not assume that every permanent residence application creates BOWP eligibility. The refusal should be reviewed carefully to see whether the issue was timing, status, PR category, missing proof, or a deeper eligibility problem.

If It Was Based on a Public Policy

Some open work permits are based on temporary public policies or special measures. These policies can have strict eligibility windows, document requirements, and expiry dates.

A common mistake is relying on old information from social media or friends. If the public policy expired or the applicant no longer meets the conditions, reapplying under the same category may not help unless a new policy or valid alternative exists.

Request Officer Notes When the Refusal Is Unclear

Many refusal letters are brief and do not fully explain the officer’s reasoning. In these situations, officer notes may help identify the real problem behind the refusal.

Notes can reveal whether the officer had concerns about eligibility, documents, relationship evidence, status, timing, prior immigration history, or credibility. This can be very useful before deciding whether to reapply, restore status, request reconsideration, or pursue another pathway.

Consider Reconsideration Only When There Is a Strong Reason

A reconsideration request may be appropriate where the refusal appears to involve a clear officer error, overlooked evidence, or a misunderstanding of the facts. It is not simply a second chance to submit a better application.

If the original file was weak, incomplete, or missing key evidence, a fresh application may be more practical than reconsideration. The right option depends on the refusal reason and whether the officer’s decision was actually unreasonable or just unfavorable.

Judicial Review May Be an Option in Serious Cases

In some cases, an applicant may consider judicial review at the Federal Court. This is generally used where there may be a legal error, procedural fairness issue, or unreasonable decision.

Judicial review is not the same as reapplying. It is a legal process and usually requires a lawyer. It can be costly and time-sensitive, so it should be considered carefully after reviewing the refusal and facts.

Do Not Keep Working Without Authorization

After a refusal, the biggest mistake is continuing to work without proper authorization. Even if the refusal feels unfair, working without authorization can create bigger problems than the refusal itself.

Future officers may review employment history, tax records, pay stubs, applications, and status history. Unauthorized work can affect future work permits, restoration applications, permanent residence applications, and credibility.

Do Not Ignore the Refusal

Some applicants avoid dealing with the refusal because they feel overwhelmed. This can be dangerous because immigration deadlines continue to run.

If restoration is available, the timeline may be limited. If status has expired, delaying action can make the situation worse. If the person must stop working, continuing employment may create additional consequences.

Prepare a Strong Explanation for the Next Step

Whether the applicant applies for restoration, visitor status, a new work permit, or reconsideration, a strong explanation is often important. The officer should be able to understand what happened, why the applicant is taking the next step, and how the new application resolves the previous concern.

A strong explanation should be honest, specific, and supported by documents. It should not blame the officer or simply repeat that the applicant wants to stay in Canada. It should address eligibility clearly.

Final Thoughts

An open work permit extension refusal is stressful, but it does not always mean the end of your Canadian immigration journey. The right response depends on the type of permit refused, your current status, the refusal reason, your eligibility for restoration, and whether another pathway is available.
