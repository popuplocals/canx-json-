Staffing and recruitment agencies occupy a unique position in the Canadian immigration landscape. They are not merely intermediaries who connect employers with workers. In many cases, they are themselves the employers of record for temporary foreign workers, carrying full LMIA and compliance obligations. In other cases, they act as facilitators for client employers who hold the LMIA, creating a different but equally complex set of legal relationships. And in some cases, they operate in grey areas between these two models that attract ESDC scrutiny.

Canada’s demand for international recruitment services has grown substantially in recent years as domestic labour shortages have persisted across healthcare, construction, manufacturing, agriculture, and hospitality. Staffing agencies that have developed expertise in LMIA-based international recruitment are meeting genuine market demand. But the regulatory framework governing how that demand can be met is significantly more complex than many agencies, and more than a few immigration practitioners, fully appreciate.

This guide is written for staffing agency owners, HR directors, and compliance managers who need to understand not just whether they can apply for LMIAs, but how to do so in a way that correctly identifies the employer, satisfies ESDC’s third-party employer requirements, allocates compliance obligations appropriately, and builds the kind of documented, scalable international recruitment model that can survive an ESDC inspection.

The Foundational Question: Can a Staffing Agency Apply for an LMIA?

Yes. A staffing agency can apply for an LMIA. ESDC does not prohibit staffing agencies, labour suppliers, or recruitment companies from submitting LMIA applications. However, the question of whether a staffing agency can apply is separate from the question of whether it should apply, which turns on who actually employs the foreign worker in a legally meaningful sense.

The LMIA is an employer-driven assessment. The entity that applies for the LMIA is asserting that it is the employer of the foreign worker for the position described in the application. That assertion carries legal consequences. The employer named in the LMIA takes on the full suite of compliance obligations for the duration of the worker’s employment: wage compliance, working conditions, occupation alignment, work location, provision of worker rights information, and business legitimacy. These obligations do not transfer to the client business where the worker is physically placed unless the client is also named as a co-employer or becomes the employer through a formal arrangement.

The Three Structural Models for Staffing Agency LMIA Applications

Model 1: The Agency as Employer of Record

In this model, the staffing agency is the legal employer of the foreign worker. The agency applies for the LMIA in its own name, the work permit names the agency as the employer, the agency pays wages, withholds taxes, and administers all employment obligations. The client business pays the agency a placement or staffing fee for the worker’s services, but the worker is employed by the agency, not the client.

This model has significant advantages for agencies that want to build a scalable international recruitment practice. The agency controls the LMIA process, the compliance documentation, and the employment relationship. Client businesses do not need to navigate the LMIA process themselves. Agencies can develop institutional knowledge and process efficiency across large volumes of applications.

The disadvantages are equally significant. The agency bears the entire compliance risk. If the client business fails to pay the agency’s invoices, the agency still owes the worker their wages. If the client business assigns the worker to duties outside the LMIA occupation, the agency is the entity ESDC holds responsible for the occupation condition violation. If the work location changes, the agency must manage the compliance implications. The employer-of-record model gives agencies control and creates client value, but it transfers substantial legal and financial risk to the agency.

Critical Compliance Requirement for Employer of Record Agencies

An agency acting as employer of record must be able to demonstrate to ESDC that it is a genuine, operating business with the financial capacity to employ the worker and pay the committed wages. Agencies whose primary revenue is the placement fee from client businesses — and whose own financial records show minimal independent revenue — may face questions about whether they have the financial capacity to employ the worker if the client relationship ends. Agencies in this model should ensure their financial records robustly support their status as the genuine employer.

Model 2: The Client Employer Applies with Agency Support

In this model, the client business applies for the LMIA in its own name, with the staffing agency providing consulting, preparation, and process management services. The client is the employer of record. The agency is a service provider. The work permit names the client as the employer.

This model is cleaner from a compliance perspective because the employer and the ESDC-named employer are the same entity. ESDC scrutiny of whether the named employer is genuine, legitimate, and has the capacity to employ the worker is straightforward. The client pays the wages, administers the employment, and bears the compliance obligations.

For staffing agencies, this model is lower risk but generates different economics. The agency earns a consulting or management fee rather than a staffing margin. The relationship may be less sticky because the client, once experienced with the LMIA process, may choose to manage future applications without the agency.

Model 3: The Hybrid or Split-Entity Arrangement

Some agencies attempt to structure arrangements in which one entity holds the LMIA and another entity operates the physical workplace, with the worker nominally employed by the LMIA holder but functionally deployed by a related but separate entity. This structure attempts to separate the immigration compliance responsibility from the operational employer.

ESDC is explicitly aware of these structures and applies the real employer test. Officers look at who actually controls the worker’s day-to-day duties, who sets and pays the wages, who supervises the work, and who has the genuine employment relationship with the worker. If the entity named in the LMIA is not the entity that actually operates as the employer in substance, ESDC may find that the LMIA was applied for by the wrong entity, which constitutes a misrepresentation in the application and a compliance failure from the first day of employment.

What ESDC Looks For When Assessing Agency LMIA Applications

Officers reviewing LMIA applications from staffing agencies apply the same assessment standards as for any employer, but with additional attention to several agency-specific concerns.

Genuine Business Legitimacy

ESDC must be satisfied that the agency is a genuine, operating business with legitimate revenue, appropriate business registration, CRA Business Number, and the financial capacity to pay the workers it is hiring. An agency whose only discernible business activity is applying for LMIAs for foreign workers, without an established track record of Canadian employment services, will face significant legitimacy scrutiny.

Strong agency applications include documentation of the agency’s operating history, client contracts, payroll records showing the agency employs Canadian workers as well as TFW workers, and financial statements that demonstrate the agency is a going concern with genuine revenue independent of the TFW placement fees. The larger and more established the agency’s Canadian employment services operation, the more credible its LMIA applications will be.

The Genuine Work Location

The LMIA application must identify the specific work location where the foreign worker will be employed. For a staffing agency acting as employer of record, this is typically the client’s physical address. If the client’s address is the work location, the LMIA application effectively discloses the client relationship to ESDC, even if the client’s name is not on the application. Officers may assess whether the described work location is consistent with the employer named in the LMIA.

If the agency lists its own office address as the work location but the worker will actually work at a client’s premises, this creates an inconsistency that will raise questions. Officers may ask where the worker will actually perform their duties and who will supervise them on a day-to-day basis. Answers that reveal a client deployment arrangement when the agency is named as employer will prompt further scrutiny of the employment structure.

Recruitment Effort Authenticity

Agencies applying for LMIAs must demonstrate the same genuine recruitment effort as any other employer. The recruitment advertising must be in the agency’s name if the agency is the employer, and the positions advertised must be genuine positions with the agency, not placeholders for client deployments. An agency that runs generic recruitment advertising without specifying the actual employment terms or the work location will face questions about the authenticity of the recruitment effort.

If the recruitment advertising describes a role at a client’s facility with specific operational context (for example, ‘production work at a food processing plant in Leamington, Ontario’), the advertising more accurately describes the actual position even when the agency is the employer. Transparency about the operational context in the advertising is more defensible than vague advertising that obscures the actual nature of the work.

Agency Compliance Obligations: Who Is Responsible for What

This is the most practically significant aspect of the agency LMIA model, and the area where the most costly misunderstandings occur. In the employer-of-record model, the agency is responsible for all six LMIA compliance conditions regardless of whether the operational failure occurs at the agency or at the client’s facility.

The Wage Obligation Cannot Be Transferred to the Client

The agency must pay the worker the wage committed to in the LMIA, from the agency’s own funds, on the agency’s own payroll, with the agency’s own payroll deductions. If the client business fails to pay the agency’s service invoice, the agency cannot withhold wages from the TFW. The worker is owed their wages by the employer named in the LMIA. Withholding wages because of a client payment dispute is a wage compliance violation with the same consequences as any other underpayment.

Agencies building LMIA-based staffing models must capitalize their operations sufficiently to carry payroll independent of client payment timing. An agency that cannot pay worker wages if a client is 60 days late on an invoice is an agency that will eventually face a wage compliance violation.

The Occupation and Location Conditions Follow the Worker

If the client business deploys the TFW in duties substantially different from those described in the LMIA, the agency has an occupation compliance failure. If the client business moves the TFW to a different facility, the agency has a location compliance failure. The agency cannot simply say ‘the client made that decision.’ The agency, as employer of record, is responsible for ensuring the worker is deployed consistently with the LMIA conditions.

In practice, this means agencies need contractual arrangements with client businesses that govern how TFW workers are deployed, that prohibit clients from reassigning workers to duties outside the LMIA occupation without agency notification and ESDC approval, and that require clients to report any changes in work location. These are not standard staffing contract terms, and many agency-client contracts do not include them. Adding these provisions is a compliance necessity for agencies using the employer-of-record model.

Worker Rights Information is the Agency’s Obligation

The obligation to provide TFW workers with written information about their rights under the TFWP rests with the employer named in the LMIA. If that employer is the agency, the agency must provide this information at the time the worker begins employment, before the worker is deployed to the client site. Delegating this obligation to the client without maintaining evidence that it was fulfilled creates a compliance gap the agency will not be able to close during an inspection.

How Agencies Can Build a Scalable, Compliant LMIA Pipeline

Sector Focus and Stream Expertise

The most successful agency LMIA models are built around specific sectors and streams rather than attempting to cover the entire TFWP landscape. An agency that develops deep expertise in agricultural LMIA applications, for example, builds institutional knowledge of SAWP protocols, agricultural wage rates, housing inspection standards, and source country liaison requirements that makes its applications more credible, faster, and more likely to succeed than those of a generalist agency working from a general understanding of the rules.

Similarly, an agency that focuses on technology sector placements through the Global Talent Stream builds relationships with designated referral organizations for Category A applications, develops LMBP templates appropriate for tech sector clients, and understands the specific wage requirements and In-Demand Occupation list verification steps that make GTS applications succeed or fail.

Sector focus also allows agencies to build genuine advertising channels, genuine relationships with post-secondary institutions for recruitment, and genuine understanding of the labour market conditions in the sector — all of which make recruitment evidence more credible to ESDC officers who review hundreds of applications from the sector and know what genuine recruitment looks like.

Client Education and Contractual Framework

One of the most consistent failure points in agency LMIA models is the gap between the agency’s understanding of the compliance obligations and the client business’s understanding of what they need to do to support compliance. A client who does not understand that they cannot reassign the TFW to different duties, cannot change the work location without notification, cannot pressure the worker not to report workplace concerns, and cannot deduct any costs from the worker’s wages is a liability for the agency’s compliance record.

Agencies should develop a client education package specifically for TFWP engagements that covers the client’s obligations under the employment arrangement, the consequences of non-compliance for both the agency and the client, the escalation process for reporting duty or location changes to the agency, and the prohibition on cost recovery from TFW workers. This package should be provided to every client before the first TFW placement, and client acknowledgment of receipt should be retained.

Record Management Infrastructure

Agencies managing multiple LMIA positions across multiple clients need records management infrastructure that allows them to track compliance documentation for each TFW position across all clients simultaneously. A spreadsheet-based system may be adequate for small volumes but becomes unreliable as the number of placements grows. Larger agencies should invest in HR information systems or compliance management platforms that track:

  • LMIA issuance dates and six-month expiry dates for each position.
  • Work permit expiry dates for each worker and renewal timelines.
  • Payroll records confirming wages paid against LMIA commitments for each pay period.
  • Client deployment records confirming occupation and location consistency for each worker.
  • Worker rights document provision records for each worker.
  • Six-year documentation retention schedules for each LMIA file.

The six-year ESDC documentation retention requirement applies to the agency’s files, not the client’s. An agency that has closed a client relationship and archived or discarded compliance documentation is not protected from an ESDC inspection that covers the period when that client’s TFW workers were employed. Retention policies must treat the six-year clock as running from the LMIA issuance date, not from the end of the client engagement.

Immigration Consulting Partner Relationships

Agencies that are serious about scaling their LMIA practices typically benefit from established relationships with Regulated Canadian Immigration Consultants or immigration lawyers who can provide application-specific guidance, review applications before submission, respond to RFIs professionally, and advise on emerging ESDC policy changes as they occur.

The agency-consultant relationship works best when the agency has internal staff who understand the LMIA process at a functional level and who manage the day-to-day relationship with clients and workers, while the consultant provides expert oversight, quality control, and advice on complex or borderline situations. An agency that outsources all immigration judgment to an external consultant without developing internal capability will be slow to adapt to policy changes and will struggle to identify compliance issues before they become inspection findings.

Staffing Agencies Cannot Charge Workers for Placement Services

This prohibition applies under both federal immigration regulations and most provincial employment agency legislation. A staffing agency cannot charge foreign workers for placement into a Canadian work permit position, cannot charge for LMIA application preparation services, cannot charge for immigration consultation services as part of the placement fee, and cannot recover any of these costs through wage deductions.

The prohibition is absolute and applies regardless of whether the worker consents to the charge. Consent from a foreign worker whose immigration status depends on the agency’s cooperation is not meaningful consent under Canadian law. Agencies that charge workers for placement services face both provincial employment agency regulatory consequences and federal TFWP compliance penalties including employer bans.

This prohibition extends to informal arrangements in which the worker is told that a portion of their wages will be remitted to the agency for administrative services. Any arrangement that results in the worker paying a cost that is properly the agency’s obligation under the TFWP is prohibited.

The Impact of 2026 Policy Changes on Agency LMIA Operations

The April 1, 2026 changes to low-wage LMIA advertising requirements directly affect agencies that run LMIA applications for clients in low-wage sectors. The extension of the minimum advertising period from four to eight consecutive weeks for low-wage stream applications means that agencies must now build 10 to 12 weeks of pre-submission preparation into their low-wage LMIA timelines rather than the previously adequate six to eight weeks.

Agencies that operate in the food service, accommodation, and retail sectors must also account for the ongoing 10% workforce cap for low-wage TFW workers. Managing cap compliance across multiple client locations requires systematic tracking of each client’s TFW proportion to ensure that new LMIA applications can be submitted and approved before exceeding the cap at any single location.

For agencies working in rural areas, the temporary expansion of the low-wage cap to 15% (effective April 1, 2026 through March 31, 2027) creates an additional placement capacity that may be operationally significant for clients with rural workforces. Agencies should be aware that this expansion is temporary and plan client expectations accordingly.

The CMA unemployment rate moratorium on low-wage LMIA processing remains in effect for regions with unemployment above 6%, with the list updated quarterly. Agencies managing applications for clients in major urban centres must verify the current moratorium status of the specific postal code before committing to an application timeline. An application submitted for a location that is in the moratorium will be refused to process, wasting the application fee and the advertising investment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a staffing agency apply for an LMIA on behalf of a client?

Not precisely. ESDC requires that the entity named in the LMIA is the genuine employer of the foreign worker. If a staffing agency is the actual employer of record for the worker, the agency applies in its own name. If the client business is the employer, the client applies in their own name (with the agency providing preparation and management services). Applying for an LMIA in the agency’s name while intending to have the client be the actual employer misrepresents the employment relationship and constitutes a compliance failure. The key question is who genuinely employs the worker, and that entity is the one that should apply.

Who is responsible for LMIA compliance — the agency or the client?

The entity named in the LMIA as the employer bears all six compliance obligations: wages, working conditions, occupation, work location, worker rights information, and business legitimacy. If the agency is the employer of record, the agency is responsible for all compliance conditions regardless of where the worker is physically deployed. If the client is the employer of record, the client bears all compliance obligations. Agencies acting as employer of record cannot transfer compliance responsibility to clients simply by contract — the ESDC compliance obligation follows the LMIA employer designation.

What is a third-party LMIA employer?

ESDC uses the concept of third-party employer to describe situations where the employer named in the LMIA sends the TFW worker to perform duties for a different business entity (the third party or client). ESDC scrutinizes these arrangements because they can obscure who actually controls the worker’s employment. The compliance obligations remain with the LMIA employer regardless of the deployment arrangement. ESDC officers apply a ‘real employer’ test that looks at who controls the worker’s duties, pays their wages, and maintains the genuine employment relationship.

How do staffing agencies manage multiple LMIA applications?

Successful multi-application management requires sector focus, strong internal process management, dedicated compliance tracking systems, and typically an ongoing relationship with an immigration consultant or lawyer for application quality control. Agencies should maintain separate LMIA files for each position, track LMIA expiry dates and work permit renewal timelines systematically, and conduct regular internal compliance audits to identify and correct issues before they become ESDC inspection findings. Many larger agencies invest in HR information systems that support TFWP compliance documentation.

Can staffing agencies charge workers for LMIA placement?

No. Staffing agencies are prohibited from charging foreign workers for placement into LMIA-based positions under both federal TFWP regulations and most provincial employment agency legislation. This prohibition covers direct placement fees, charges for LMIA preparation services, immigration consultation fees charged as part of the placement, and any indirect cost recovery through wage deductions. The prohibition applies regardless of whether the worker consents, because consent from a worker whose status depends on the agency is not legally meaningful consent.
