Many foreign workers believe that once they receive a Canadian job offer, the hardest part of the process is over. Employers often think the same way. After spending months searching for candidates, completing recruitment efforts, and preparing documents, it is easy to assume that a job offer automatically strengthens a work permit application.

Unfortunately, that is not how immigration processing works. A job offer alone does not guarantee approval. Immigration officers and, in many situations, Service Canada officers carefully assess whether the position being offered is genuine. In today’s immigration environment, authorities increasingly examine whether the employment opportunity actually makes sense and whether the entire arrangement reflects a real need rather than an immigration-driven transaction.

This review has become increasingly important because concerns surrounding fraudulent job offers, non-genuine employment arrangements, and immigration misuse have resulted in greater scrutiny. Understanding how officers assess job offer genuineness can help both employers and workers avoid common mistakes and build stronger applications.

A Job Offer Is More Than Just a Letter

Many people think a job offer means a signed letter showing salary, job title, and start date. While these details matter, officers assess much more than the document itself.

Immigration officers examine the overall circumstances surrounding the offer. They often assess the employer, business operations, staffing needs, wage levels, job duties, and whether the position logically fits within the employer’s operations. Officers are trying to determine whether the role genuinely exists and whether the employer truly intends to hire the worker under the conditions described.

Officers Assess Whether the Business Itself Appears Genuine

Before officers even evaluate the worker, they often begin by looking closely at the employer.

Authorities increasingly review whether the company actively operates, whether it has a physical presence, whether it employs staff, and whether operational activities support hiring additional workers. Businesses with limited history, weak documentation, or inconsistent operational evidence sometimes face additional scrutiny.

Officers may review websites, payroll records, tax filings, contracts, business licenses, online presence, and financial information. Small businesses can absolutely hire foreign workers successfully, but businesses with limited operational evidence often need stronger supporting documentation.

Active Business Operations Matter More Than Many Employers Realize

One of the most common issues in work permit and LMIA assessments involves questions surrounding active engagement in business.

Officers frequently want confidence that businesses genuinely operate and that hiring requests align with real operational needs. Questions may arise where companies report minimal staffing activity, limited revenue, or operational patterns inconsistent with the position being requested.

For example, a small business requesting multiple employees despite limited operations may attract additional review because officers want to understand how staffing needs fit within overall business realities.

Officers Evaluate Whether the Position Makes Business Sense

A job offer may look complete on paper but still create concerns if the position itself does not reasonably fit within the employer’s operations.

Officers often ask practical questions. Does the business genuinely require this role? Does the number of requested workers align with business size? Do staffing structures support the proposed position? Does the role appear necessary based on operational activity?

Applications sometimes encounter difficulties because employers focus on preparing documents while overlooking whether the overall business story appears logical.

Job Duties Receive Significant Attention

Many employers place substantial focus on selecting job titles without realizing that officers care much more about actual duties.

Officers regularly compare job responsibilities against occupation classifications and labour market standards. Titles alone rarely determine assessments because businesses can label positions however they choose.

For example, a company may describe a position as management while listing duties resembling front-line work. Similarly, a supervisor role may actually involve responsibilities inconsistent with supervisory classifications. Where duties and occupations fail to align properly, officers frequently begin questioning the authenticity of the offer itself.

Wage Levels Can Raise Questions

Wages often receive more attention than many employers realize.

Officers compare compensation against labour market information and regional standards. Positions offering wages significantly below market levels may create obvious concerns. Surprisingly, unusually high wages sometimes create concerns as well.

Where compensation appears substantially outside expected ranges, officers may question whether the role genuinely reflects labour market realities or whether the position was structured primarily to support immigration objectives.

Officers Review Recruitment and Hiring History

Where LMIA processes apply, recruitment efforts become important evidence supporting genuineness.

Authorities increasingly review whether employers genuinely attempted to hire Canadian citizens and permanent residents before seeking foreign workers. Recruitment records often help officers understand why international hiring became necessary.

Businesses that cannot clearly explain hiring needs or provide meaningful recruitment evidence occasionally encounter concerns because officers may question whether the role was created primarily for immigration purposes.

Previous Employer Compliance History Can Affect Assessments

Employers sometimes overlook the importance of previous interactions with immigration programs.

Businesses that previously hired foreign workers may have compliance histories reviewed during future assessments. Authorities increasingly examine whether employers followed approved conditions involving wages, duties, hours, and working conditions.

Past compliance concerns do not automatically prevent future approvals, but previous issues occasionally influence overall credibility assessments.

Immigration Officers Also Assess the Foreign Worker

Genuine job offer assessments do not focus only on employers.

Immigration officers also evaluate whether the foreign worker realistically appears capable of performing the role being offered. This review frequently includes education, work history, qualifications, and supporting documentation.

For example, concerns sometimes arise where applicants possess backgrounds entirely unrelated to the position offered or where employment history appears inconsistent with the proposed role.

Work Experience and Qualifications Need to Support the Offer

Officers frequently ask whether the worker reasonably appears qualified for the position.

Applicants do not always need extensive experience, particularly for entry-level occupations. However, officers usually expect some connection between the individual’s background and the work being offered.

Employment letters, training records, education, certifications, and transferable skills often become important because they help establish whether applicants realistically fit the proposed role.

Inconsistent Information Can Create Credibility Problems

One of the most avoidable reasons applications encounter problems involves inconsistencies.

Differences involving wages, employment dates, duties, addresses, staffing structures, and business information sometimes appear small from the applicant’s perspective. However, officers compare information carefully across multiple documents.

Even relatively minor contradictions occasionally create broader concerns because they affect overall credibility and confidence in the application.

Relationships Between Employers and Workers Sometimes Receive Attention

In some situations, officers may examine whether personal relationships exist between employers and workers.

Family relationships or close personal connections do not automatically create problems. However, where relationships exist, officers may review applications more carefully to ensure positions genuinely reflect business needs rather than arrangements created primarily for immigration purposes.

Where relationships exist, stronger evidence frequently becomes important to demonstrate authenticity.

Fraudulent Job Offers Have Increased Government Scrutiny

The immigration system has experienced increasing concerns involving fake job offers, unauthorized recruiters, and arrangements created solely for immigration purposes.

As a result, officers today often review applications more carefully than in previous years. Greater scrutiny does not mean genuine employers and workers should panic, but it does mean applications increasingly require consistency and stronger supporting evidence.

The focus has shifted from simply reviewing forms toward understanding the overall story presented by the application.

How to Strengthen a Genuine Job Offer Application

Strong applications usually tell a logical and complete story. Employers benefit from maintaining detailed recruitment records, ensuring duties align with occupations, supporting wage structures appropriately, and demonstrating genuine operational needs.

Workers also strengthen applications by ensuring employment history, education, and qualifications clearly support the proposed position. When both sides present clear and consistent evidence, officers often gain greater confidence regarding the authenticity of the offer.

Final Thoughts

Receiving a job offer remains an important milestone, but approval depends on much more than a letter containing salary and duties. Officers increasingly assess whether employment opportunities genuinely exist and whether the overall circumstances make sense from both business and immigration perspectives.
