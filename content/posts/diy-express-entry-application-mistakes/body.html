Thousands of people file their own Express Entry applications every year, and many succeed. Filing yourself is legal, and IRCC does not require anyone to hire a representative. But an Express Entry application for permanent residence is not just a set of online forms. It is a legal application assessed under the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act (IRPA) and the Immigration and Refugee Protection Regulations (IRPR), and officers apply those rules strictly. A self-filed application that misses one legal requirement can be returned, refused or, in the worst cases, lead to a five-year ban for misrepresentation.

This guide explains the legal framework behind every Express Entry decision, the mistakes we see most often in self-filed applications and the practical steps you can take to protect your application, whether you file alone or with professional help.

Quick Summary

Self-filed Express Entry applications most often fail because of non-compliant reference letters, wrong National Occupational Classification (NOC) codes, missing documents, unsupported Comprehensive Ranking System (CRS) claims, proof of funds problems and incomplete personal history. Under section 11.2 of IRPA, you must meet the program criteria and have the qualifications you were ranked on both when IRCC issued your invitation and when it received your application. Under section 16 of IRPA, you must answer truthfully and provide the evidence an officer reasonably requires. Careful preparation, not just completed forms, is what protects your application.

The Legal Framework Every DIY Applicant Must Understand

Express Entry is not a program in itself. It is an application management system created under Division 0.1 of IRPA (sections 10.1 to 10.4), which allows the Minister to issue instructions on how candidates are ranked and invited. The actual programs, the Federal Skilled Worker Class, the Canadian Experience Class and the Federal Skilled Trades Class, are defined in the IRPR.

Why DIY Applications Run Into Trouble

Filing yourself is not the problem. The problem is that the online system accepts many things that the law does not. The portal will let you upload a weak reference letter, select a NOC code that does not match your duties or claim points you cannot prove. Nothing in the system stops you. The officer, however, assesses your application against the law, not against what the portal accepted.

A few points worth knowing before you file:

  • The officer is not required to warn you about missing evidence that the law already requires you to provide.
  • An Invitation to Apply (ITA) is conditional; it does not mean your claims have been verified.
  • You have 60 days from your ITA to submit a complete application, which is not much time to fix document problems.

The 10 Most Common DIY Mistakes

1. Non-compliant employment reference letters

Reference letters that lack hours per week, salary, exact dates or detailed duties are one of the most frequent reasons work experience is not accepted. Without accepted work experience, you may no longer meet sections 75 or 87.1 of the IRPR.

2. Choosing the wrong NOC code

Officers compare your actual duties with the lead statement and main duties of the NOC. Under paragraphs 75(2)(b) and (c) and 87.1(2)(b) and (c) of the IRPR, you must have performed the actions in the lead statement and a substantial number of the main duties, including all of the essential duties.

3. Counting work experience that the law excludes

Under paragraph 87.1(3)(a) of the IRPR, work done while you were a full-time student does not count for the Canadian Experience Class. Under paragraph 87.1(3)(b), self-employment and unauthorized work also do not count. Many DIY applicants count these periods by mistake.

4. Missing category-based draw requirements

If you were invited in a category-based draw, you must have met that category’s requirements when invited. In 2026, most occupation categories require at least 12 months of full-time work experience, or the equivalent in part-time work, in a single eligible occupation within the last three years.

5. Incomplete or invalid police certificates

Police certificates are a leading cause of incomplete applications. You generally need them for each country where you lived for six months or more in a row since age 18.

6. Missing upfront medical exam information

Since August 21, 2025, IRCC has required Express Entry applicants to submit immigration medical exam information with their application. Since October 21, 2025, applications without it may be rejected.

7. Proof of funds problems

Under subparagraph 76(1)(b)(i) of the IRPR, Federal Skilled Worker applicants without qualifying arranged employment must hold transferable and available funds, unencumbered by debts or other obligations. Borrowed money and property equity do not count.

8. Gaps in personal history

Every period in your personal history must be accounted for. Gaps raise questions about your background and can lead to requests, delays or refusal.

9. Undeclared family members

Failing to declare a spouse, common-law partner or dependent child can lead to misrepresentation findings and, under paragraph 117(9)(d) of the IRPR, can prevent you from sponsoring that family member later.

10. CRS claims you cannot prove

If your documents do not support the points you claimed, the officer may recalculate your score. If your recalculated score falls below the cutoff for your draw, section 11.2 of IRPA can lead to refusal.

What the Data Does and Does Not Say

You may see online claims that self-represented applicants are refused far more often than represented applicants. We have not found reliable, published IRCC data comparing refusal rates for self-filed and represented Express Entry applications specifically, so treat such claims with caution. What experience does show is that refusals usually come from preventable legal and documentary errors, and those errors are more common when applicants are unfamiliar with the regulations.

How to Protect a Self-Filed Application

  • Read the actual legal requirements for your program in the IRPR, not just summaries.
  • Compare your duties line by line with the official NOC description before you create your profile.
  • Get compliant reference letters before you enter the pool.
  • Keep evidence for every CRS point you claim.
  • Order police certificates and book your medical exam early.
  • Account for every month in your personal history.
  • Declare every family member, even if they are not coming with you.
  • Consider a professional file review before you submit, even if you do the rest yourself.

Getting Help Legally

Under section 91 of IRPA, only authorized representatives can charge a fee to advise or represent you in an immigration application. That includes Regulated Canadian Immigration Consultants (RCICs) licensed by the College of Immigration and Citizenship Consultants, lawyers and paralegals licensed by a provincial law society and notaries in Quebec. Friends and family can help for free, but unlicensed paid “agents” are a serious risk.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I file my Express Entry application without a consultant?

Yes. IRCC does not require you to use a representative, and many people file successfully on their own. However, your application is assessed under IRPA and IRPR, and the online portal does not stop you from making legal errors. A professional review before you submit can catch problems with reference letters, NOC codes and proof of funds.

What is the most common reason DIY Express Entry applications are refused?

Work experience problems are among the most common, including reference letters that lack required details and NOC codes that do not match actual duties. Under sections 75 and 87.1 of the IRPR, your duties must match the lead statement and a substantial number of main duties, including all essential duties, of your NOC.

Will IRCC tell me if something is missing before refusing my application?

Not always. Officers generally have no duty to warn you about evidence the law already requires you to provide. A procedural fairness letter is usually issued when an officer has concerns about credibility, accuracy or genuineness, or relies on information you have not seen. Submitting complete evidence the first time is the safest approach.

What does section 11.2 of IRPA mean for Express Entry applicants?

Section 11.2 prevents an officer from issuing a permanent resident visa if, at the time of your invitation or when IRCC received your application, you did not meet the criteria or hold the qualifications on which you were ranked. Limited exceptions exist, such as a birthday that lowered your age points after the invitation.
