For more than a decade, a job offer in Express Entry has meant one thing: a Labour Market Impact Assessment (LMIA). From November 3, 2026, that changes. A written job offer, without an LMIA, can earn up to 100 Comprehensive Ranking System (CRS) points and can satisfy job offer requirements under the Federal Skilled Worker and Federal Skilled Trades programs.

That sounds simple, and in one sense it is. But the LMIA did more than create paperwork. It gave a separate government department the job of testing the offer before it ever reached an immigration officer. With that step gone, the full weight of the decision now sits with Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) officers. A job offer that looks like a plain sheet of paper will need solid evidence behind it. This guide explains what officers will look for, and how candidates and employers can build a job offer that holds up.

Quick Summary

From November 3, 2026, Express Entry job offers no longer need an LMIA. IRCC officers will decide whether each offer is genuine by looking at five factors: whether the employer actively runs the business, whether the offer reflects a reasonable need, whether the employer can meet its terms, whether the candidate can do the job and is likely to take it, and whether the employer complies with employment and recruitment laws. Officers now carry the full burden of that review, so the strongest job offers will come with clear, consistent supporting evidence from day one.

What Changed: From LMIA Approval to Officer Discretion

How It Worked Before

Under the old model, an employer applied to Employment and Social Development Canada (ESDC), through Service Canada, for an LMIA. ESDC reviewed the business, the wage, the employer’s recruitment efforts in Canada, and the effect of hiring a foreign worker on the Canadian labour market. Employers often spent months and paid a processing fee before receiving a decision. A positive LMIA told IRCC that another department had already tested the job.

How It Works From November 3, 2026

IRCC’s job offer page now states that an LMIA is no longer required for a job offer to qualify, and that holding an LMIA will not add CRS points or give a profile priority. Job offers can be for an existing or a new position, with any employer. IRCC officers will assess whether each offer is genuine.

In practice, this moves the test from before the application to during it. ESDC no longer filters weak or questionable offers in advance. An officer reviewing your application for permanent residence will now decide, often on the documents alone, whether the offer is real. That makes the quality of your evidence far more important than before.

The Five Factors IRCC Officers Will Assess

IRCC’s October 16, 2026 backgrounder lists five questions an officer must answer before accepting a job offer as genuine. The first four closely mirror the genuineness factors that officers already apply to work permit job offers under section 200(5) of the Immigration and Refugee Protection Regulations, so the approach is not new to officers. What is new is its central role in Express Entry.

1. Is the Employer Actively Engaged in the Business?

The officer wants to see a real, operating business that offers the type of work in the job offer. A newly registered company with no revenue, no staff, and no premises will struggle. So will an offer for work that has nothing to do with what the business actually does.

Evidence that commonly helps includes business registration and licences, a Canada Revenue Agency business number, recent financial statements or tax filings, payroll records, a lease or proof of premises, and a website or other public presence that matches the business.

2. Does the Offer Reflect a Reasonable Employment Need?

The officer asks whether this business would realistically need this role. A five-person restaurant offering a full-time chief financial officer position will raise questions. A growing engineering firm hiring a civil engineer will not.

Employers can support this with an organizational chart showing where the role fits, a short explanation of why the business needs the position, and evidence of growth, new contracts, or a vacancy. If the role is new, explain what changed in the business to create it.

3. Can the Employer Meet the Terms of the Offer?

The officer checks whether the employer can actually pay the salary and provide full-time, continuous work for at least one year after the candidate becomes a permanent resident. IRCC has not published a minimum wage for these job offers. However, a salary in line with what the occupation typically pays in that region makes the offer more believable, and a salary the business clearly cannot afford makes it less so.

Financial statements, bank records, and payroll history help show that the business can sustain the role. Larger employers may need less, but every employer should be ready to show capacity.

4. Is the Candidate Qualified and Likely to Accept the Job?

This factor looks at the candidate, not the employer. The officer considers whether your education, experience, and language ability fit the job, and whether you are likely to take the position and carry it out. A job offer in a field unrelated to your work history will draw scrutiny.

If the job is regulated in Canada, you must also show you are likely to qualify for the required licence or certification. Provinces and territories set their own rules for regulated professions and trades, so contact the regulatory body in your intended province early. For trades, the Red Seal program is a useful starting point.

5. Does the Employer Comply With Employment and Recruitment Laws?

The officer reviews whether the employer has followed federal and provincial laws on employment and recruitment. Past violations, unpaid wages, or improper recruitment practices can sink an otherwise strong offer.

Some provinces also regulate how employers recruit foreign workers, and some require employers or recruiters to register or hold a licence. Employers should confirm the rules in their province before making an offer. Candidates should never pay an employer or recruiter for a job offer, and an employer should never recover recruitment costs from a worker.

What a Valid Express Entry Job Offer Letter Must Include

IRCC requires a valid job offer to be recent and in writing. It must not come from an embassy, high commission, or consulate in Canada. A work permit on its own does not count as a job offer, even an open work permit. The letter should set out the job clearly, including:

  • the employer’s legal name, address, and contact details
  • the job title and the National Occupational Classification (NOC) code
  • the main duties of the position
  • the salary or hourly wage, and the deductions
  • the hours of work and other conditions of employment
  • the start date, and confirmation that the job is full-time, continuous, and for at least one year after permanent residence

For Federal Skilled Worker eligibility, the offer must come from one employer, be paid, full-time (at least 30 hours a week), non-seasonal, and in a TEER 0, 1, 2, or 3 occupation. For Federal Skilled Trades, the offer can come from up to two employers and must be in an eligible trade occupation. Specific rules also apply to physicians providing publicly funded medical services.

How to Build a Job Offer That Holds Up

For Candidates

  1. Check the NOC code first. The new CRS points only apply to the 186 high-wage occupations. Make sure the duties in your offer match the NOC description, not just the job title.
  2. Match the offer to your background. An officer will compare the job with your education and work history. Gaps or unrelated roles need an explanation.
  3. Ask the employer for supporting documents early. Business registration, financial records, and an explanation of the role will make your application stronger.
  4. Keep every document consistent. The job title, duties, salary, and dates must match across your profile, the offer letter, and any reference letters.
  5. Update your profile before November 3. IRCC will only award job offer points if you add the job offer details yourself.

For Employers

  1. Write a complete offer. Include every detail IRCC expects, and sign and date it on company letterhead.
  2. Document the business need. A short memo explaining why you need the role, and how it fits your organization, gives the officer context.
  3. Pay a realistic wage. Use local wage data for the occupation as a reference point.
  4. Keep your records in order. Payroll, tax filings, and workplace registrations should be current and easy to produce.
  5. Follow recruitment rules. Confirm your provincial requirements, and never charge a worker for a job or recruitment.

A Practical Note on Your Express Entry Profile

IRCC’s online profile has not yet caught up with the policy. Until it does, IRCC instructs candidates to select that they have a positive LMIA, even though they do not need one, and to enter placeholder values in the “Work history” section: LMIA number 00000, issuance date 2026-01-01, and expiry date 2029-01-01. Follow IRCC’s current instructions on its job offer page exactly, because the online system may change.

Red Flags That Could Lead an Officer to Refuse a Job Offer

  • a business with no clear operations, staff, or revenue
  • a role the business does not reasonably need
  • a salary the business cannot afford, or one far below what the occupation usually pays
  • duties that do not match the NOC code claimed
  • a candidate whose background does not fit the job
  • a job offer that the candidate paid for
  • inconsistent details across documents

Officers can also look more closely at offers from employers connected to the candidate, such as a family business. These offers are not prohibited, but they need especially strong evidence that the role is real and needed.

Why Getting This Right Matters

A refused job offer can cost you the CRS points that earned your invitation, and that can put your application at risk. More seriously, if an officer finds that a candidate or employer misrepresented a job offer, the candidate can face a finding of misrepresentation, which carries a five-year ban on applying to come to Canada.

IRCC has also stated that a revoked job offer no longer qualifies. If your employer withdraws the offer, the points go with it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I still need an LMIA for an Express Entry job offer in 2026?

No. From November 3, 2026, an LMIA is no longer required for a job offer to earn the new CRS points. A temporary public policy also removes LMIA requirements for job offers used to meet Federal Skilled Worker or Federal Skilled Trades eligibility, for candidates invited on or after that date. IRCC officers will assess each job offer for genuineness instead.

Will having an LMIA give me extra CRS points?

No. IRCC states that having an LMIA will not add CRS points or give your profile priority. Points now depend on whether your job offer is in a high-wage occupation and its level: 100, 70, or 50 points. An LMIA may still help as supporting evidence, but it does not change your score on its own.

How does an IRCC officer decide if a job offer is genuine?

The officer considers whether the employer actively runs the business, whether the offer reflects a reasonable employment need, whether the employer can meet the terms, whether you can do the job and are likely to accept it, and whether the employer complies with employment and recruitment laws. Strong documents from the employer make each of these easier to prove.

Can a new or small business make a valid job offer?

Yes. IRCC says job offers can be for an existing or a new position, with any employer. However, small or new businesses should expect closer review. They should be ready to show real operations, a genuine need for the role, and the financial ability to pay the salary for at least one year after you become a permanent resident.

What happens if my employer withdraws the job offer?

IRCC has stated that a revoked job offer no longer qualifies. You would lose the job offer points, and if the offer was part of your program eligibility, your application could be affected. Tell IRCC about any change and speak with a licensed consultant quickly to understand your options.
