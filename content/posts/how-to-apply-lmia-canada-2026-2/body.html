Most employers who begin the LMIA process thinking of it as a government form exercise quickly realize they are in something more like a legal proceeding. Employment and Social Development Canada is not simply collecting data. It is conducting an evidence-based assessment of whether hiring a specific foreign worker for a specific position is justified by conditions in the Canadian labour market. The employer’s job is to build a file that satisfies that assessment beyond reasonable doubt.

That framing matters, because it changes how you approach every step of the process. You are not filling in boxes. You are constructing a case, and every document, every advertising record, every recruitment note, and every wage figure is a piece of evidence. Officers see hundreds of these files. They can tell the difference between an application built on genuine facts and one that was assembled to check boxes. The former gets approved. The latter, often, does not.

This guide walks through the complete LMIA application process for 2026, step by step, with the kind of practical detail that comes from working these files every day rather than reading the regulations from a distance.

Before You Begin: Four Questions Every Employer Must Answer

Question 1: Do You Actually Need an LMIA?

Before investing a dollar in an LMIA application, an employer should confirm that an LMIA is actually the right pathway for their specific situation. Many employers apply for LMIAs when an LMIA-exempt option would have achieved the same hiring outcome faster and at lower cost.

If the worker is an American or Mexican national in a profession listed under CUSMA, they may be able to obtain a work permit at the port of entry without any LMIA. If the employer is transferring an existing employee from a foreign affiliate of the same corporate entity, an intra-company transferee work permit may apply. If the worker already holds an open work permit as a graduate, as a spouse of a temporary resident, or as a bridging PR applicant, no LMIA is needed at all.

The cost of not asking this question first is the $1,000 LMIA fee, weeks of advertising effort, months of processing time, and in some cases a refused application, all of which could have been avoided by taking a half hour to evaluate LMIA-exempt alternatives with a qualified consultant.

Question 2: Which TFWP Stream Applies?

The Temporary Foreign Worker Program is divided into distinct streams, and the stream determines the rules for every subsequent step. The primary dividing line for most positions is the wage. If the offered wage meets or exceeds the median hourly wage for the specific NOC code in the province where the work will be performed, the position falls under the High-Wage Stream. If it falls below that threshold, it is a Low-Wage Stream application.

For technology and knowledge economy roles, the Global Talent Stream should always be evaluated before defaulting to the standard high-wage stream, because the GTS two-week processing target is a significant operational advantage. For agricultural positions, the Primary Agriculture Stream applies regardless of wage. For caregiver positions, the pathway has changed significantly in recent years, and employers should confirm whether the position falls under a pilot program rather than the traditional TFWP caregiver stream.

The NOC 2021 code for the position must be correctly identified at this stage because it determines both the wage threshold and whether certain stream eligibility conditions apply. An incorrect NOC code at the beginning of the process means every subsequent step is built on a flawed foundation.

Question 3: Is Your Business Ready for ESDC Scrutiny?

ESDC does not simply take an employer’s word that they are a legitimate, operating business with a genuine need for the position. Officers review business registration documents, financial statements, CRA registration, payroll records, and in some cases visit the physical premises. A business that cannot produce these records, or whose financial records raise concerns about its ability to sustain the position and the worker’s wages, will struggle to obtain a positive LMIA.

New businesses face heightened scrutiny. If your company has been operating for fewer than two years, expect questions about your ability to sustain the position long-term. If your business is seasonal, expect questions about how the foreign worker will be employed during the off-season. If your business has had any previous TFWP compliance issues, those will be visible to the officer reviewing your file and will inform how they approach the application.

Question 4: Can You Satisfy the Recruitment Requirement?

The recruitment requirement is not met simply by posting a job advertisement. ESDC requires a genuine, documented effort to hire qualified Canadians and permanent residents before turning to a foreign worker. This means running ads on the appropriate platforms, for the required duration, with the required content, and then reviewing every application received and documenting the outcome of each one.

The honest pre-application question is: if ESDC asked me to prove that I could not find a qualified Canadian for this position, could I do it? If the answer is uncertain, the recruitment phase needs more planning before any application is submitted.

Step 1: Identify the Correct NOC Code

The National Occupational Classification (NOC) 2021 system categorizes all jobs in Canada by TEER (Training, Education, Experience and Responsibilities) level and occupational group. Selecting the correct NOC code is the first technical decision in the LMIA process and one of the highest-stakes ones.

The NOC code determines the median wage ESDC will use to classify the position as high-wage or low-wage, influences which stream the application falls under, determines whether the position is eligible for the Global Talent Stream, and shapes the officer’s understanding of what qualifications are required for the role.

Employers should match the primary duties of the position, not the job title, to the NOC code descriptions at noc.esdc.gc.ca. Job titles vary widely across industries, but the lead statement and main duties for each NOC code are consistent. If the actual duties of the position match those described under a particular NOC code, that is the correct code regardless of what the employer has chosen to call the role internally.

Common Mistake: Choosing a NOC Code Based on Desired Wage Level

Some employers select a NOC code not because it accurately describes the position but because the median wage for that code would classify the position as high-wage rather than low-wage, allowing them to avoid the cap restrictions and accommodation requirements of the Low-Wage stream. ESDC officers cross-reference the NOC code against the duties described in the job offer and the recruitment advertising. A mismatch between the NOC code and the actual job duties is a material error that will prompt either an RFI or a refusal.

Step 2: Determine the Prevailing Wage

Once the correct NOC code is identified, the employer must look up the median hourly wage for that NOC in the province or territory where the work will be performed. ESDC publishes these wage figures through its Wage Report tool at Job Bank (jobbank.gc.ca). The median wage is the benchmark against which the offered wage will be compared.

The position must be offered at a wage that meets or exceeds the prevailing wage for the NOC in the region. For high-wage positions, the wage must be at or above the provincial median for the NOC. For low-wage positions, the wage must still be at or above the highest of: the federal or provincial/territorial minimum wage, the wage ESDC identifies as the prevailing rate for the occupation in the region, or the wage being paid to Canadians in the same position at the same location.

This last point is frequently missed. ESDC will not approve a wage for a foreign worker that is lower than what the employer is already paying its Canadian employees doing the same job. The principle of equal pay for equal work is embedded in the LMIA requirements, and officers check for it.

Step 3: Plan and Execute the Recruitment Campaign

The recruitment phase is where most LMIA files are won or lost. It is the phase that requires the most advance planning, the longest elapsed time (minimum four consecutive weeks), and the most careful documentation. It is also the phase where the temptation to take shortcuts is highest, because it is happening before any government involvement and before the consequences of shortcuts become visible.

Mandatory Advertising Requirements

ESDC requires that the position be advertised on the Government of Canada’s Job Bank (jobbank.gc.ca) for the duration of the recruitment period. Job Bank advertising is free and can be managed through an employer account on the platform. This is not optional and cannot be substituted with another platform regardless of how prominent or appropriate that platform is for the industry.

In addition to Job Bank, the employer must advertise on at least two other platforms appropriate to the occupation and the target audience. The choice of these additional platforms matters. ESDC’s guidance is that the advertising should be targeted toward the population most likely to include qualified candidates. For a software developer position, advertising only on general job boards when there are well-known tech-specific platforms is a weaker application than one that used sector-specific channels. For a trades position, advertising in the provincial trades association’s job board is more appropriate than advertising only on a general consumer job site.

The minimum advertising period is four consecutive weeks. The weeks must be consecutive and must include the period immediately preceding the LMIA application. ESDC has generally interpreted this to mean the recruitment was active and ongoing in the period leading up to submission, not that the employer ran ads for four weeks two years ago and is now submitting.

Content Requirements for Job Postings

ESDC specifies minimum content requirements for job postings submitted as recruitment evidence. Each posting must include the job title, the job duties, the wage or salary (or range), the location of work, the required qualifications and experience, and contact information for applying. Postings that advertise the role as ‘salary negotiable’ without providing any range have been challenged by officers as insufficient, because an applicant who cannot determine whether the wage is competitive may self-select out before applying, which means the employer’s recruitment effort has not genuinely reached the full potential candidate pool.

The language of the posting must match the language requirements of the job. Requiring fluency in a language other than English or French is only acceptable if the employer can demonstrate that this requirement is a genuine bona fide occupational requirement, not a mechanism to narrow the candidate pool to a pre-selected foreign worker.

Documenting the Recruitment Outcomes

Every application received during the recruitment period must be reviewed and the outcome documented. For each Canadian or permanent resident applicant who was not hired, the employer must have a documented reason for rejection. These reasons must be genuine, specific, and tied to bona fide occupational requirements.

The documentation does not need to be elaborate. A simple log that records each applicant’s name, the date the application was received, whether an interview was conducted, and the reason they were not selected is adequate. What the log must not do is show that every single Canadian applicant was rejected for the same uniform reason, because that pattern signals to officers that the rejection criteria were constructed after the fact rather than applied genuinely.

Officers are also alert to situations where the job requirements appear to have been written around a specific foreign worker’s existing qualifications. If the job posting requires a very specific combination of credentials and experience that happens to match the target foreign worker’s resume perfectly while excluding most Canadian applicants, this will attract scrutiny.

Step 4: Prepare the Transition or Accommodation Plan

Transition Plan (High-Wage Stream)

Employers applying under the High-Wage stream are required to submit a Transition Plan describing how they will reduce their reliance on foreign workers over time. The plan must identify specific activities the employer will undertake to recruit, retain, or train Canadians and permanent residents, and it must include timelines and measurable commitments.

A credible Transition Plan is one that describes activities the employer is actually capable of carrying out and that are appropriate to the size and nature of the business. A company with 20 employees promising to establish a full apprenticeship program with its own training facility will not be believed. The same company committing to sponsor one Canadian apprentice in the relevant trade over the next year, partnering with a local college for co-op placement, and actively advertising at local job fairs is offering something credible and proportionate.

The Transition Plan is reviewed again at the time of any subsequent LMIA application for the same employer. If the employer has not made progress on the commitments from a previous plan, the officer reviewing the new application will notice and may treat this as evidence that the employer is not genuinely committed to reducing foreign worker dependence.

Accommodation Plan (Low-Wage Stream)

Low-Wage stream employers must demonstrate that they have arrangements in place to provide adequate and affordable housing for foreign workers who are not local, or that workers will have access to affordable housing in the community. Unlike the Transition Plan, which is forward-looking, the Accommodation Plan is a present-tense commitment about living arrangements.

Employers in remote areas, particularly agricultural operations, face the most detailed accommodation requirements because workers may genuinely have no other housing option. The accommodation must meet provincial standards for habitability and must be documented with specific information about the type of housing, the location, the cost to the worker (which cannot be excessive or used as a mechanism to recoup LMIA costs), and the sleeping arrangements.

Step 5: Gather Your Supporting Documents

The LMIA application package for a standard TFWP application includes documents from multiple categories. A disorganized or incomplete submission is one of the most common causes of RFIs and delays.

Business Documentation

Documents that establish the employer’s legitimacy and operating status include: CRA Business Number registration, provincial or territorial business registration or incorporation documents, most recent federal or provincial business tax return, recent financial statements or bank statements demonstrating operational capacity, and if applicable, any professional licences or sector-specific certifications required to operate the business.

Job Offer Letter

The job offer must be in a specific format that satisfies ESDC’s requirements. It must be signed by the employer, identify the foreign worker by name, describe the position using the NOC code, state the wage and hours, specify the work location, describe the duties, state the anticipated start date, and confirm the position is a genuine offer of employment. The job offer is a legal document that creates obligations for the employer once the LMIA is positive and the worker is hired.

Recruitment Evidence

The complete recruitment package must be organized chronologically and clearly labeled. It should include copies of all job postings from all platforms with the URL and posting dates clearly visible, the recruitment log showing each applicant and the outcome, and any other documentation of genuine efforts including career fair participation records, outreach to employment agencies, or co-op program canvassing.

Wage Documentation

The employer must provide documentation showing the offered wage is consistent with the prevailing wage for the NOC in the region. This typically means printouts of the ESDC Wage Report for the specific NOC and province at the time of application. If the offered wage is above the prevailing wage, this is generally straightforward to document. If it is at or below the prevailing wage, additional documentation may be needed to confirm that it remains compliant.

Step 6: Submit Through the ESDC Employer Portal

All LMIA applications are submitted through the ESDC Employer Portal at employers.esdc.gc.ca. Employers who have not previously used the portal must create an account and register their business. This registration step can take time and should be completed well in advance of the intended submission date.

The portal guides the employer through the application form, which covers the employer’s business information, the job position details, the recruitment activities, and the supporting information relevant to the stream. Supporting documents are uploaded as attachments. The $1,000 application fee is paid at the time of submission by credit card or other accepted payment method.

Before clicking submit, take the time to review the complete application and all attachments as if you were the ESDC officer who will review it. Confirm that every form field is completed, that the information in the form is consistent with the information in the supporting documents, that all required attachments are present, and that the documents are organized in a way that makes it easy for the officer to find what they are looking for.

Critical Pre-Submission Check

The wage on the application form must exactly match the wage on the job offer letter. The NOC code on the form must exactly match the NOC code referenced in the job offer and used to determine the prevailing wage. The work location on the form must exactly match the address stated in the job offer. Inconsistencies across these three data points are among the most common triggers for RFIs and, in some cases, refusals.

Step 7: Manage the Processing Period

Once the application is submitted, the employer enters a waiting period during which ESDC processes the file. This is not a passive phase. There are active steps the employer should take during processing to prepare for what comes next.

Monitoring for Requests for Information

ESDC communicates with employers through the Employer Portal and by email. During the processing period, employers should check both regularly, ideally daily as the application approaches the expected decision date. A missed RFI, or an RFI response that arrives after the ESDC deadline, can result in a refusal on procedural grounds. Set up email notifications from the portal and designate a specific person as responsible for monitoring LMIA correspondence.

Maintaining Ongoing Recruitment

While the LMIA is processing, keep the job posting active or be prepared to explain why it was taken down. In some cases, particularly for applications with longer processing timelines, ESDC may ask whether qualified Canadians have become available since the application was submitted. Maintaining active recruitment during processing positions the employer to answer this question credibly and also demonstrates ongoing good faith.

Preparing the Worker for the Work Permit Application

While the LMIA is processing, the foreign worker should be preparing for their work permit application so they can move quickly once the positive LMIA is issued. This means gathering identity documents, completing credential assessments where required, arranging a medical examination if the work permit requires one, and working with an RCIC or immigration consultant on the work permit application package. Reducing the gap between LMIA issuance and work permit application directly reduces the risk of the LMIA expiring before it is used.

Step 8: Respond to Requests for Information

A Request for Information from ESDC is not a rejection. It is an opportunity. Officers issue RFIs when they need additional information or clarification to complete their assessment. How an employer responds to an RFI often determines the outcome of the application.

Read the RFI carefully and identify precisely what the officer is asking for. Do not provide a general response that does not address the specific question. If the RFI asks about a specific applicant’s rejection reason, address that specific applicant with specificity. If it asks for financial records, provide financial records in a clearly organized format.

Respond within the timeframe stated in the RFI. ESDC typically provides ten business days to respond. If additional time is genuinely needed, contacting Service Canada immediately and requesting an extension with an explanation is possible, though extensions are not guaranteed. Missing the RFI deadline without communication is one of the worst outcomes in the LMIA process because it can result in refusal without the employer having had the opportunity to address the concern.

Step 9: Act on the Decision

After a Positive LMIA

Immediately record the expiry date of the LMIA, which is typically six months from the date of issuance. Provide the positive LMIA letter and the signed job offer to the foreign worker along with a clear explanation of the work permit application process and the urgency of the timeline. Begin the compliance preparation process by reviewing the LMIA conditions and setting up the record-keeping systems you will need to satisfy any future ESDC inspection.

After a Negative LMIA

Read the refusal letter in full and identify every stated basis for the negative decision. Engage an RCIC or immigration lawyer if you have not already done so to assess whether the refusal reasons can be addressed and whether a reapplication is viable. Do not reapply immediately without addressing the specific issues that caused the refusal; a second identical application will receive a second identical result.

Common Application Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

After working hundreds of LMIA files, certain patterns of error appear repeatedly. Advertising on the wrong platforms for the occupation, or on the right platforms but for insufficient duration, is the most common single mistake. Offering a wage that is at the minimum wage rather than the prevailing wage for the NOC is the second most common. Submitting an application with inconsistent wage figures across the form, the job offer, and the payroll evidence is third.

A less common but equally damaging mistake is submitting an application for a position whose duties, as described in the job offer, do not match the NOC code claimed in the application. Officers cross-reference these elements, and a disconnect between stated duties and the NOC code description signals either an error or an attempt to misrepresent the nature of the position.

Finally, many employers underestimate the importance of the Transition Plan as a live document rather than a one-time exercise. If you have submitted a Transition Plan in a previous LMIA and you are now applying for another LMIA for the same operation, the officer will likely ask about progress on the previous plan. Being unable to demonstrate that you have followed through on previous commitments weakens your current application significantly.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I apply for an LMIA on the ESDC Employer Portal?

Go to employers.esdc.gc.ca and create or log in to your employer account. Navigate to the LMIA application section and select the appropriate TFWP stream. Complete the multi-section application form covering your business information, the position details, the recruitment activities, and the supporting information required for your stream. Upload all required supporting documents and pay the $1,000 application fee at submission. Ensure all information in the form is consistent with your supporting documents before submitting.

How long do I need to advertise a position before applying for an LMIA?

ESDC requires a minimum of four consecutive weeks of active advertising. The advertising must include the Government of Canada’s Job Bank plus at least two other platforms appropriate to the occupation and industry. The recruitment period should be recent and ideally ongoing or recently concluded at the time of application, not historical advertising from months or years ago. The four-week minimum is a floor, not a target; many successful applications include longer recruitment periods.

What documents do I need for an LMIA application?

Required documents typically include: CRA Business Number registration and business incorporation documents, recent financial statements or bank records, the signed job offer letter for the foreign worker, copies of all job postings from all advertising platforms with dates visible, a recruitment log documenting every applicant reviewed and the outcome, the Transition Plan (High-Wage stream) or Accommodation Plan (Low-Wage stream), and the prevailing wage documentation for the NOC and province. Stream-specific additional documents may apply.

Can I apply for an LMIA for multiple workers at once?

ESDC allows employers to include multiple positions in a single LMIA application under certain circumstances, but each position still typically requires its own $1,000 fee and its own documentation of recruitment effort. In the Primary Agriculture stream, LMIAs can cover unnamed groups of workers for a season. In standard TFWP streams, most LMIAs are position-specific and worker-specific. Discuss your specific situation with an RCIC or immigration consultant to determine the most efficient approach for multiple hires.

What happens if I miss an ESDC Request for Information deadline?

Missing an RFI deadline without prior communication to Service Canada risks having your application refused on procedural grounds without ESDC reviewing the merits of the file. If you receive an RFI and cannot respond within the stated timeframe, contact Service Canada immediately to request a brief extension and explain the reason for the delay. Do not simply ignore the deadline and hope for the best. Prompt, complete RFI responses are one of the most controllable factors in LMIA processing outcomes.
