When a foreign worker arrives in Canada on an LMIA-based work permit, one of the most consequential immigration facts of their life in Canada is printed directly on the permit itself: the employer name. That name is not cosmetic. It is a legal condition of the authorization to work. Working for an employer other than the one named on the permit, even briefly, even in a seemingly equivalent role, constitutes working without authorization. And working without authorization creates immigration consequences that can affect every future application the worker makes.

This structural tie between the work permit and the employer creates a power dynamic that the federal government has explicitly acknowledged is a vulnerability for TFW workers. An employer who knows a worker cannot easily leave creates conditions in which workers may tolerate wages, working conditions, or treatment they would otherwise refuse. The regulatory framework has responded to this dynamic by creating specific pathways through which workers can change employment — not easily, but legally.

This guide covers every pathway available in 2026 for workers who need or want to change employers, the immigration status implications of the transition period, how to protect status while making the change, and the situations in which staying or leaving requires the most careful planning.

Why LMIA Work Permits Are Employer-Specific: The Legal Framework

Under the Immigration and Refugee Protection Regulations, an employer-specific work permit authorizes the holder to work in Canada only for the employer named in the permit, in the occupation identified, and at the location stated. This is not a policy choice that ESDC or IRCC makes case by case. It is the legal architecture of the TFWP work permit, grounded in the regulatory conditions under which the LMIA was assessed.

The LMIA is an assessment of whether a specific employer, offering a specific position, at a specific location, for a specific wage, has a genuine need for a foreign worker that cannot be filled from the Canadian labour market. The work permit built on that LMIA is limited to that specific situation because the labour market assessment that justified the permit was specific to it. A worker who takes the LMIA-approved role but then moves to a different employer, occupation, or location is working under conditions that were never assessed by ESDC.

This is the important foundational point that workers sometimes do not fully absorb: the work permit is not permission to work in Canada generally. It is permission to work for one employer in one role in one place.

Pathway 1: Your New Employer Obtains a New LMIA

The most straightforward mechanism for legitimately changing employers on an LMIA work permit is the same mechanism that got the worker to Canada in the first place: the new employer applies for a new LMIA for the same or a comparable position, and once the LMIA is approved, the worker applies for a new work permit naming the new employer.

The Worker’s Status During the Transition

While the new LMIA is being processed, the worker’s current employer-specific work permit remains valid. The worker must continue working for the original employer during this period or cease working entirely. Working for the new employer before the new work permit is issued is not authorized. This is a hard line that many workers inadvertently cross, particularly in situations where the new employer needs the worker on site immediately and the worker assumes that having applied for a new work permit is sufficient.

The standard LMIA processing time of 8 to 20 weeks plus the subsequent work permit processing time means the total transition period from applying to actually being authorized to work for the new employer can be five to nine months. This creates a genuine operational problem: the worker wants to leave, the new employer wants them now, but the immigration framework requires them to wait. Managing this tension requires advance planning on the worker’s side, the original employer’s side, and the new employer’s side.

The Original Employer’s Role

The worker’s departure from the original employer does not legally terminate the original work permit or the original LMIA compliance obligations. The original employer continues to be the named employer on the worker’s existing permit until a new one is issued. However, if the worker has stopped working for the original employer, the employment relationship that the LMIA was built around no longer exists as a practical matter.

Workers who leave their original employer before their new work permit is issued should be aware that they are in a period where they have a technically valid work permit for an employer they are no longer working for, but no authorization to work for anyone else. During this period, they can legally be in Canada but cannot legally work. This status distinction matters for immigration record purposes.

Timing Strategies for Minimizing the Gap

Workers who know in advance that they want to change employers should initiate the process as early as possible. Ideally, the conversation with the new employer about an LMIA application begins while the worker still has significant time remaining on their current work permit, allowing the new LMIA to process and a new work permit to be issued before the original permit expires. Workers who wait until their situation with the original employer becomes untenable before beginning this process will face a compressed timeline that creates significant status risk.

Where the new employer’s position qualifies for the Global Talent Stream, the dramatically compressed processing timeline of approximately two weeks for the LMIA plus two weeks for the work permit makes the gap between leaving one employer and being authorized to work for the next substantially shorter than it would be under the standard TFWP stream. For technology workers in GTS-eligible positions, the transition pathway is considerably faster than for workers in standard stream positions.

Pathway 2: Transitioning to an Open Work Permit

For workers who qualify for an open work permit category, the job change problem is resolved entirely. An open work permit is not restricted to a specific employer, occupation, or location. A worker who holds an open work permit can work for any Canadian employer without a new LMIA or a new employer-specific permit.

Bridging Open Work Permit (BOWP)

Workers who have submitted a permanent residence application under an eligible stream and whose existing work permit is approaching expiry may qualify for a Bridging Open Work Permit. The BOWP is issued as an open permit, meaning it is not restricted to the original LMIA employer. A worker who receives a BOWP while their PR application is processing is free to change employers during the bridging period without a new LMIA.

To access the BOWP, the PR application must already be submitted and actively processing with IRCC. The BOWP application is made from within Canada, before the current work permit expires (or while on maintained status with a timely renewal application pending). The BOWP resolves the employer-tie constraint for eligible candidates who have reached a sufficient point in the PR process.

Post-Graduation Work Permit (PGWP)

Workers who previously studied at a designated Canadian learning institution and who meet the PGWP eligibility requirements can apply for a PGWP. A PGWP is an open work permit valid for up to three years. LMIA workers who completed a Canadian credential while already in Canada on a work permit, or who are eligible for a PGWP through a Canadian program completed before their LMIA period, may be able to transition to PGWP status.

This pathway is most relevant for workers who came to Canada on an LMIA work permit after completing studies in Canada, or for workers who are completing Canadian studies part-time while working on their LMIA permit. It is not available to workers who have not studied at a PGWP-eligible Canadian institution.

Spousal Open Work Permit

If the LMIA worker’s spouse or common-law partner holds a work permit or study permit in an eligible category (currently limited to TEER 0 or TEER 1 workers and master’s or doctoral students), the LMIA worker themselves might not have an open permit, but their spouse could hold one and be free to change employers. For couples navigating employment changes, knowing which partner holds which type of permit matters for joint immigration planning.

More relevantly, a worker in any immigration status whose spouse holds a TEER 0 or 1 work permit can apply for a spousal open work permit of their own, subject to current eligibility rules. This provides a mechanism through which the LMIA worker can access open permit status if their spouse holds a qualifying permit.

Pathway 3: Vulnerable Worker Open Work Permit — The Emergency Route

The Vulnerable Worker Open Work Permit (Vulnerable Worker OWP) is designed specifically for the most urgent employer-change situations: workers who are experiencing abuse, exploitation, or serious violations of their employment rights by the employer named on their LMIA permit. This pathway exists precisely because the structural tie between the LMIA work permit and the employer creates a power imbalance that can be — and is — exploited.

Who Qualifies

Workers on employer-specific work permits who are experiencing or are at risk of physical, sexual, psychological, or financial abuse from their employer, or who are experiencing exploitation through withheld wages, document confiscation, threats related to immigration status, or other coercive conduct, may apply for a Vulnerable Worker OWP from within Canada. The application does not require the worker to first leave the abusive employer; it is designed to be applied for while the worker is still in the situation.

What It Provides

The Vulnerable Worker OWP is an open work permit. It is not restricted to any employer, occupation, or location. Once issued, the worker can begin working for any employer in Canada immediately without any further LMIA or work permit process. The permit provides the legal foundation for leaving the abusive employer without losing work authorization.

How to Apply

Applications are submitted to IRCC from within Canada. Supporting documentation can include the worker’s own written statement describing the abusive situation, documentation from support organizations, medical records, police reports, or any other credible evidence. IRCC processes Vulnerable Worker OWP applications on a priority basis in recognition of the urgency of the circumstances.

Workers who are afraid to apply for this permit because they fear retaliation from the employer should know that employers are legally prohibited from retaliating against workers who exercise their rights under the TFWP, including the right to apply for a Vulnerable Worker OWP. Retaliation is itself a compliance violation with serious consequences for the employer.

TFW Worker Support Resources

Workers in abusive employment situations can access support before, during, and after applying for a Vulnerable Worker OWP. TFW Worker Protection Line: 1-800-367-5693 (toll-free, available in multiple languages). Community legal organizations including Migrant Workers Centre offices, ACCES Employment, and various provincial legal aid clinics. Provincial labour standards offices can address wage theft and other employment standards violations separately from the immigration process. Workers do not have to navigate this alone.

What Happens to the Original LMIA When a Worker Leaves

When a worker leaves an LMIA employer, the LMIA itself remains on record at ESDC. The positive LMIA decision is not revoked simply because the employment relationship ended. However, the LMIA’s practical utility ends when the work permit that was built on it expires, because the LMIA is no longer needed to maintain any active work authorization.

The original employer’s compliance obligations continue for the period during which the worker was employed under the LMIA. If the worker was employed for six months under the LMIA work permit and ESDC conducts an inspection covering that period, the compliance conditions for those six months are assessed against what actually happened during the employment. The fact that the worker has since left does not insulate the employer from findings related to the period of employment.

For the worker, leaving the original employer does not affect any immigration records that were created during the period of employment: the work experience accumulated counts toward Express Entry CRS scoring, CEC eligibility, and PNP eligibility based on what was actually worked, regardless of how the employment ended.

Protecting Immigration Status During a Job Change: The Maintained Status Mechanism

Workers who apply for a work permit renewal before their current permit expires may be eligible for maintained status while the renewal is processing. Maintained status allows the worker to continue the activities authorized under the expired permit while the renewal is pending with IRCC.

However, maintained status is tied to the activities authorized under the expired permit. A worker on maintained status from an LMIA-based employer-specific work permit is still restricted to the activities authorized by that permit — which means they are still restricted to the original employer and occupation. Maintained status does not automatically expand the scope of work authorization.

This is an important nuance: a worker who is on maintained status from their original LMIA permit while a new LMIA-based work permit for a different employer is processing is not yet authorized to work for the new employer. The authorization for the new employer begins when the new work permit is issued, not when the application is submitted.

Changing Roles Within the Same Employer: A Grey Area

Workers who want to change their role while staying with the same employer face a different set of questions. Minor changes in duties within the same occupation are generally acceptable without a new LMIA. Significant changes in duties that move the worker into a substantially different NOC code, a substantially different wage level, or a substantially different occupation may constitute a material change that requires a new LMIA.

For example, a cook who is promoted to head chef within the same restaurant has moved to a different NOC code with different duties and typically a higher wage. Whether this requires a new LMIA depends on the degree of difference between the two roles and whether the change is within the spirit of the original LMIA position. An employer facing this situation should obtain advice from an RCIC before implementing the role change to avoid creating a compliance issue by proceeding without assessment.

International Mobility Program as an Alternative: Can the New Employer Use an Exemption?

Before the original employer and the worker invest in the timeline and cost of a new LMIA, both should assess whether the new employer qualifies for an LMIA-exempt work permit category. If the new position qualifies for a CUSMA work permit, an intra-company transfer, or another IMP exemption, the transition can be accomplished without any LMIA. The new work permit can typically be processed significantly faster than an LMIA-based permit.

The relevant question is whether the new position and the new employer’s circumstances meet any of the IMP exemption criteria. This assessment is worth making before committing to the LMIA route, both to potentially save time and to avoid the $1,000 ESDC fee if an exemption applies.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I change jobs if I have an LMIA work permit?

Your LMIA work permit legally restricts you to the employer named on it. To change employers, you have three main options: your new employer obtains a new LMIA and you apply for a new employer-specific work permit; you qualify for an open work permit category (Bridging OWP, PGWP, or other); or, if you are experiencing abuse from your current employer, you apply for a Vulnerable Worker Open Work Permit. Working for a new employer before a new work permit is issued is unauthorized work, regardless of whether a new work permit application is in progress.

What happens if I leave my LMIA employer?

If you leave your LMIA employer before your work permit expires and before a new work permit is issued authorizing work for a different employer, you have a valid permit but no employer to legally work for under its terms. You can remain in Canada legally, but you cannot work until you have valid authorization for a new employer. You should not work for any new employer during this period without authorization. Your accumulated Canadian work experience for the period you were actually employed remains valid for Express Entry and CEC purposes regardless of how the employment ended.

How do I get a new work permit for a different job?

The most common route is having the new employer apply for a new LMIA from ESDC. This takes approximately 8 to 20 weeks for standard streams, plus additional time for the work permit application itself. Total transition time is typically five to nine months. If the new position qualifies for the Global Talent Stream, the timeline is compressed to approximately four to six weeks total. If you qualify for an open work permit category, you can apply for that independently without the new employer needing an LMIA. Consult an RCIC to identify the fastest and most appropriate pathway for your specific situation.

What is a Vulnerable Worker Open Work Permit?

A Vulnerable Worker Open Work Permit is an open work permit issued by IRCC to workers on employer-specific work permits who are experiencing abuse or exploitation from their employer. It is not tied to any employer, occupation, or location. Once issued, the holder can work for any Canadian employer immediately. Applications are made from within Canada and are processed on a priority basis. Supporting documentation can include a personal statement, records from support organizations, medical records, or police reports. Employers are prohibited from retaliating against workers who apply for this permit.

Can I work for a new employer while my new work permit application is being processed?

No. Working for a new employer before a new work permit naming that employer is issued is unauthorized work, even if the application is actively being processed by IRCC. The work permit application being in progress does not create interim authorization to work for the new employer. You must wait for the permit to be issued before starting work with the new employer. An exception applies if you qualify for maintained status while renewing an open work permit — but even then, the maintained status scope is limited to the activities authorized under the expired permit.
