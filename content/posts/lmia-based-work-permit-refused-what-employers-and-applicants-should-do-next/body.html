An LMIA-based work permit refusal can create immediate stress and uncertainty for both foreign workers and employers. For workers, a refusal may affect employment plans, immigration status, family circumstances, and even future permanent residence goals. For employers, the refusal can disrupt staffing needs, delay projects, create operational challenges, and result in losing valuable time and money spent obtaining a positive Labour Market Impact Assessment (LMIA).

Many people assume that once a positive LMIA is approved, the difficult part of the process is over.

Unfortunately, that is not always true.

One of the biggest misunderstandings in Canadian immigration is believing that a positive LMIA automatically guarantees a work permit approval.

It does not.

In reality, obtaining a positive LMIA and obtaining a work permit are two separate stages involving two different government assessments. Service Canada reviews whether an employer genuinely requires a foreign worker and whether hiring that worker will have a positive or neutral effect on Canada’s labour market. IRCC, however, conducts a separate assessment focused on the foreign worker themselves.

This distinction becomes extremely important because many applicants receive work permit refusals despite holding valid and approved LMIAs.

Understanding what happened and deciding what to do next becomes critical.

Understanding the Difference Between LMIA Approval and Work Permit Approval

Many employers and applicants mistakenly believe that a positive LMIA is the final approval.

It is not.

A positive LMIA primarily answers one question:

“Can the employer hire a foreign worker because there is a labour market need?”

A work permit application asks a very different question:

“Does this particular worker qualify for this specific position?”

When reviewing a work permit application, immigration officers frequently assess whether the foreign worker actually meets the position requirements outlined in the LMIA and job offer.

Officers may review educational background, employment history, work experience, licensing requirements, qualifications, language ability, previous immigration records, admissibility concerns, and whether the overall application appears credible.

This means an employer may successfully prove the need for a foreign worker, while IRCC may still conclude that the specific worker does not sufficiently meet the requirements.

Many refusals happen because applicants do not understand this distinction.

Why LMIA-Based Work Permit Applications Get Refused

Work permit refusals can happen for many reasons.

Some refusals involve eligibility issues. Others involve weak evidence, inconsistencies, employer concerns, licensing issues, or broader credibility questions.

One of the biggest mistakes applicants make after refusal is assuming there must have been only one issue.

Officers frequently assess applications holistically.

Sometimes multiple concerns combine together and ultimately lead to refusal.

Understanding the actual concern becomes far more important than simply reading the refusal letter.

Insufficient Proof of Required Work Experience

One of the most common refusal reasons involves concerns regarding work experience.

Applicants often assume that job titles determine qualification. Immigration officers, however, focus heavily on actual duties and responsibilities.

For example, an applicant applying for a Construction Supervisor role cannot simply provide a letter stating they worked as a supervisor. Officers often examine whether job duties, progression, and experience align with the requirements of the offered Canadian position.

Problems frequently arise where employment letters contain copied duties, generic wording, vague descriptions, or duties that do not realistically reflect the industry.

Applicants also face issues where the LMIA itself required specific experience thresholds.

For example, if the LMIA required three years of directly related experience and supporting documentation appears weak, officers may conclude that the requirement was not met.

In many situations, the issue is not that applicants lack experience.

The issue is proving it properly.

Educational Background Does Not Match the Position

Educational concerns also contribute to many refusals.

Some occupations require specific diplomas, certifications, apprenticeships, or formal training. Immigration officers frequently assess whether educational history reasonably supports the offered role.

For example, concerns sometimes arise where applicants with unrelated academic backgrounds apply for highly specialized occupations without strong practical experience.

Employers occasionally assume willingness to train a worker resolves qualification concerns.

Immigration officers may view matters differently.

The officer often assesses whether the applicant currently appears qualified.

Not whether qualifications may develop later.

Licensing and Regulatory Issues

Licensing concerns frequently create unexpected problems.

Certain occupations in Canada require licensing, certification, trade recognition, or registration.

This issue commonly arises in occupations involving healthcare, transportation, skilled trades, engineering, and other regulated professions.

Applicants sometimes assume licensing can be addressed after arrival.

In some cases, that is possible.

In others, officers may expect evidence showing realistic eligibility for licensing or steps already taken toward meeting requirements.

For example, if a skilled trade position requires provincial certification and the applicant provides no evidence showing they can realistically obtain certification, officers may raise concerns.

Licensing problems often remain unnoticed until refusal occurs.

Purpose of Visit Concerns

Many applicants assume that once they receive a positive LMIA, concerns regarding purpose of visit disappear.

That assumption can create problems.

Purpose of visit remains a common refusal ground.

Immigration officers frequently ask whether the overall application makes sense.

  • Questions may include:
  • Does the offered role align with previous employment?
  • Does career progression appear logical?
  • Does the applicant’s background support the position?
  • Do the circumstances appear realistic?

For example, if an applicant suddenly shifts into a completely unrelated occupation without explanation, officers may question whether the position genuinely aligns with the individual’s background.

Purpose concerns often become broader credibility concerns.

Employer and Position Credibility Concerns

Applicants often focus entirely on their own documents.

However, officers sometimes review employer-related issues as well.

Questions may arise regarding the business itself, staffing structure, offered duties, organizational size, wage structure, and whether the position appears genuine.

Although a positive LMIA addresses many labour market concerns, immigration officers still retain authority to assess overall credibility.

For example, officers may question whether the position realistically fits within business operations or whether the role appears consistent with the employer’s activities.

Employer-side evidence can sometimes influence work permit outcomes more than applicants realize.

Previous Immigration History and Credibility Issues

Immigration history frequently receives more scrutiny than applicants expect.

Officers often review previous applications, refusals, study permits, visitor records, work permits, travel history, and personal disclosures.

If earlier applications contain inconsistent information regarding employment, marital status, education, or travel history, officers may identify broader credibility concerns.

For example, if an applicant previously claimed different employment dates or omitted information in earlier applications, officers may begin questioning overall reliability.

Immigration systems retain records.

Consistency matters.

Inconsistencies Across Documents

Small inconsistencies frequently become larger problems.

Immigration officers compare resumes, work experience letters, LMIA details, educational records, application forms, and previous submissions.

Differences that appear minor to applicants may create credibility concerns.

  • For example:
  • Resume:
  • Employment from 2018 to 2023
  • Reference letter:
  • Employment from 2019 to 2022
  • Application forms:
  • Employment from 2018 to 2021

These differences sometimes lead officers to question accuracy.

Applicants frequently overlook inconsistencies.

Officers usually do not.

Missing Documents and Weak Supporting Evidence

Some refusals occur because of simple omissions.

Applicants occasionally assume immigration officers will request additional documentation if something important is missing.

Unfortunately, that assumption can become costly.

Missing employment evidence, educational records, translations, licenses, experience documents, or supporting explanations can create avoidable refusals.

Immigration applications should function as complete evidence packages rather than upload checklists.

Missing pieces sometimes create larger consequences than applicants expect.

What Applicants Should Do Immediately After Refusal

One of the biggest mistakes applicants make after refusal is reacting emotionally.

Many people immediately prepare another application.

Others assume their immigration plans have ended completely.

Neither approach is ideal.

The first step should always involve reviewing the refusal carefully.

Refusal letters frequently provide broad conclusions rather than complete explanations.

Officer notes often provide deeper insight.

  • For example, a refusal letter may state:
  • “Qualifications not established.”
  • However, officer notes may reveal:
  • “Employment duties copied. Progression unclear. Experience insufficient.”

That distinction matters.

Applicants should identify actual concerns rather than making assumptions.

What Employers Should Do After Refusal

Employers frequently assume their involvement ends once the LMIA receives approval.

That is not always the case.

After refusal, employers should review job descriptions, qualification requirements, employer documents, supporting materials, and LMIA details carefully.

Some employers later discover that job descriptions became overly restrictive or failed to properly explain why the worker genuinely qualified.

Future applications sometimes become stronger when employers provide more detailed support.

Employer involvement frequently remains important even after LMIA approval.

Should Applicants Reapply Immediately?

  • One of the most common questions after refusal is:
  • “Can I apply again right away?”
  • Technically yes.
  • Strategically, the more important question becomes:
  • “What changed?”

If nothing changed, another refusal often becomes more likely.

A stronger application should involve stronger evidence, clearer organization, improved explanations, and direct responses to previous concerns.

Speed rarely solves immigration problems.

Preparation often does.

Can Judicial Review Become an Option?

Not every refusal results from a weak application.

Sometimes officers make mistakes.

There are situations where evidence may have been ignored, facts misunderstood, requirements misapplied, or fairness concerns created.

In these situations, Judicial Review before the Federal Court may become worth assessing.

However, if the problem involves genuinely weak evidence or unmet requirements, a stronger reapplication often becomes more practical.

  • The key question becomes:
  • Was the problem the application?
  • Or was the problem the decision?

Immigration Status and Restoration Considerations

Applicants already inside Canada should immediately review their immigration status after refusal.

Many workers misunderstand restoration periods, maintained status, and departure obligations.

Depending on the circumstances, strict timelines may apply.

Waiting too long can create additional complications.

Status concerns should never be ignored while planning next steps.

Final Thoughts

LMIA-based work permit refusals create significant stress because employers and workers often feel they already completed the most difficult step by obtaining a positive LMIA.

However, LMIA approval and work permit approval remain two different assessments.

Many refusals happen not because applicants lacked qualifications, but because qualifications were not properly demonstrated.

Others involve documentation gaps, inconsistencies, credibility concerns, or misunderstandings.

The goal after refusal should not simply be submitting another application.

The goal should be understanding what actually happened.

Because stronger applications are built through stronger analysis.
