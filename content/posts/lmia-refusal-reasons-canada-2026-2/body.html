An LMIA refusal costs the employer the $1,000 non-refundable application fee, the cost of the recruitment advertising campaign, weeks or months of processing time, and in many cases the professional fees associated with preparing the application. If the application was handled by internal staff, it also costs the significant administrative hours invested in preparation. When the refusal letter arrives, the instinct is to feel that something went wrong. Often, something did, and often it was something that could have been avoided with better pre-application preparation.

What follows is an honest account of the most common reasons LMIA applications fail in 2026, drawn from the practical experience of managing these files across industries and across Canada. For each refusal reason, the analysis covers not just what the rule says but why applications fail against that rule in practice, and what specifically to do differently.

Refusal Reason 1: Insufficient or Non-Genuine Recruitment Effort

This is the most common single reason for LMIA refusal, and it encompasses a wide range of application failures from technical non-compliance with advertising requirements to the deeper problem of a recruitment effort that was not designed to find a Canadian worker.

What Insufficient Recruitment Looks Like

Technically insufficient recruitment includes: advertising on fewer than the required number of platforms, running advertisements for fewer than four consecutive weeks, posting ads with missing required content such as wage information, using platforms clearly inappropriate for the occupation, or submitting advertising evidence that does not clearly document when and where the ads ran.

Non-genuine recruitment is a more serious category. It refers to situations where the advertising technically meets the minimum requirements but was not conducted with genuine intent to find a Canadian worker. ESDC officers identify this pattern through several indicators: all qualified Canadian applicants being rejected for uniform reasons, rejection reasons that are pretextual relative to the stated job requirements, job postings with requirements specifically tailored to exclude most Canadian candidates, wages offered below what would attract qualified Canadians in that market, or advertising limited to platforms with minimal visibility to the relevant professional population.

How to Avoid This Refusal

Run a genuinely open recruitment campaign on appropriate platforms including Job Bank, for a minimum of four weeks, with a posting that honestly describes the position and offers a wage competitive for the local market. Review every application received on its individual merits. Document rejection decisions with specific, individualized reasons tied to actual occupational requirements. If the recruitment effort produces no suitable Canadian candidates, that outcome will be well-supported by a thorough and genuine process. If it does produce a suitable Canadian, hire that person.

Refusal Reason 2: Wage Below the Prevailing Rate

Wage compliance is one of the clearest and most precisely reviewable elements of an LMIA application. ESDC officers compare the offered wage directly to the median hourly wage for the NOC in the province. If the wage falls below the prevailing rate, the application will be refused unless an exceptional and documented justification exists.

The Most Common Wage Errors

The most frequent wage error is offering the minimum wage rather than the prevailing wage. Provincial minimum wage is the legal floor for employment in Canada, but it is not the relevant benchmark for ESDC. The prevailing wage for a cook in British Columbia is not the provincial minimum wage. It is the median hourly wage for the cook NOC in BC, which is considerably higher. Employers who base their LMIA wage on minimum wage without checking the ESDC Wage Report are setting up for refusal.

A second common error is paying Canadian employees in the same position a higher wage than the foreign worker being hired. ESDC requires that the foreign worker be offered wages equal to what Canadians performing the same job at the same location earn. Even if the offered wage technically meets the ESDC median, offering the foreign worker less than comparable Canadian employees is a compliance failure that officers check for during both the application review and any subsequent inspection.

How to Avoid This Refusal

Before writing the job offer letter, look up the current ESDC Wage Report figures for the specific NOC code in the province where the work will be performed. Set the offered wage at or above the median, and confirm that it is equal to or greater than what you are paying Canadian employees in comparable roles. Document the wage determination process and include the Wage Report printout in the application package.

Refusal Reason 3: Business Legitimacy Concerns

ESDC has broad authority to assess whether an employer is a legitimate operating business before approving an LMIA. Applications from businesses that cannot demonstrate legitimacy and operational capacity face refusal regardless of how well the rest of the application is prepared.

What Triggers Business Legitimacy Scrutiny

Newly incorporated businesses with no financial history, businesses with financial records showing minimal revenue or operating capital, businesses with no physical presence or with premises inconsistent with the described operations, businesses operating in sectors with a documented history of TFWP abuse, and businesses where the foreign worker being sought appears to be the only or primary planned employee are all more likely to face legitimacy questions.

A specific scenario that appears with some regularity involves employers who incorporate a business specifically to hire a foreign worker, rather than hiring a foreign worker because an existing business has a genuine need. Officers can often identify this pattern from the timing of incorporation relative to the LMIA application and from financial records that show no genuine operating history. These applications face a very high refusal rate.

How to Avoid This Refusal

Prepare comprehensive business legitimacy documentation as described in the document checklist. If the business is new, include a detailed business plan with financial projections and a clear explanation of the business model. If the financial records show a loss position, include a brief explanatory letter from the accountant or employer explaining the financial context. Ensure the business registration name exactly matches the name used in the LMIA application.

Refusal Reason 4: Inadequate or Vague Transition Plan

For High-Wage stream applications, a Transition Plan that does not meet ESDC’s standards for content and credibility is a refusal risk that is entirely within the employer’s control to eliminate. Officers consistently identify Transition Plans that are too vague, lack timelines, or contain commitments that are disproportionate or unconnected to the foreign worker’s occupation.

What Weak Transition Plans Look Like

Plans consisting of one or two paragraphs of generic language about future hiring intentions, plans that list activities without any associated timelines or measurable outcomes, plans that make the same commitments as previous applications without reporting on progress, and plans that commit to activities clearly beyond the employer’s operational capacity are all assessed as inadequate.

How to Avoid This Refusal

Write a Transition Plan that describes two to four specific, realistic, time-bound activities with measurable outcomes appropriate to the employer’s size and industry. If this is not the employer’s first LMIA application, open the plan with a progress report on previous commitments before describing new ones. Treat the plan as a real operational commitment, because it will be reviewed again in the next application.

Refusal Reason 5: Documentation Inconsistencies

Applications where the information in the application form does not match the information in the supporting documents are a reliable source of both RFIs and refusals. Inconsistencies create doubt about the accuracy of the application and signal either carelessness or an attempt to misrepresent the facts.

The Most Common Inconsistencies

The wage stated in the application form differs from the wage in the job offer letter. The NOC code in the application does not match the duties described in the job offer. The work location in the application form differs from the address in the business documentation. The foreign worker’s name in the application differs from the name on their identity documents. The business name in the application differs from the legal name on the incorporation certificate.

How to Avoid This Refusal

Complete a cross-referencing review before submission. Check every key data point in the application form, specifically the wage, the NOC code, the work location, the employer’s legal name, and the worker’s full legal name against the corresponding information in every supporting document. Any discrepancy, however minor, must be corrected before submission. A second set of eyes on the complete application package before it is submitted is a simple and highly effective quality control measure.

Refusal Reason 6: Adverse Labour Market Conditions

Even an employer who has followed every procedural step correctly can receive a negative LMIA if ESDC’s labour market data for the specific occupation and region shows that there is a sufficient supply of qualified Canadians available without the need for a foreign worker. This is a case where following the rules does not automatically produce a positive outcome, because the rules require more than process compliance.

When Adverse Conditions Apply

Rising unemployment in the relevant occupation and region, recent layoffs in the sector, or ESDC’s own assessment data showing an adequate supply of qualified workers can all lead to a negative determination based on adverse labour market conditions. This is more common during economic downturns and for occupations where the supply situation has improved significantly since a previous positive LMIA was issued.

How to Respond to Adverse Conditions

If you believe that ESDC’s labour market data for your specific occupation and region does not accurately reflect the actual shortage you are experiencing, provide your own labour market evidence. Industry association reports, published wage surveys, documented recruitment history over an extended period, and specific evidence of the impact the vacancy is having on your operations are all potentially relevant. These elements will not guarantee a positive outcome but can shift the weight of the evidence.

Refusal Reason 7: Low-Wage Cap Violations

Employers in sectors subject to the low-wage TFW workforce cap must ensure that their LMIA application does not result in TFW workers comprising more than the permitted percentage of their workforce at the applicable location. Submitting an application that would violate the cap is grounds for refusal.

How Cap Refusals Happen

Employers who do not track their current TFW proportion carefully, or who are not aware of the cap limits applicable to their sector, sometimes submit applications without realizing they are already at or above the cap. Officers check the employer’s existing LMIA history and can calculate the current TFW proportion at the location based on the payroll information in the application.

How to Avoid Cap Violations

Before submitting any Low-Wage LMIA application, calculate the current TFW proportion at the specific work location (not across the entire company). If that proportion is close to the applicable cap for your sector, confirm whether the new LMIA position would push you over the limit before submitting. If it would, the options are to reduce the TFW proportion through attrition before applying, to explore whether the position can be structured at a wage that qualifies it for the High-Wage stream (which has no cap), or to defer the application until the proportion falls within the limit.

Refusal Reason 8: Failure to Demonstrate Genuine Need for the Position

ESDC can refuse an LMIA if it is not satisfied that the employer has a genuine need for the position being applied for. This is most commonly an issue when the business is new, when the position is not clearly related to the employer’s core operations, or when the employer’s financial records do not support their claimed need to add staff.

What Genuine Need Looks Like

A genuine need is demonstrated when the employer can show that the position is an established role within their operations (or a clearly justified new role as the business grows), that the duties of the position are substantive and directly connected to the employer’s business activities, that the employer has been trying to fill this position through Canadian recruitment without success, and that the employer’s financial position supports their ability to employ someone in this role.

Refusal Reason 9: LMIA-Exempt Pathway Exists

This is a refusal reason that surprises employers: ESDC declining to process an LMIA application because an LMIA-exempt pathway clearly exists for the situation. While this is not the most common refusal scenario, officers can and do note in refusal decisions that the employer appeared to be applying for a position through the LMIA pathway when an exemption applied.

The more common version of this problem is not an explicit refusal on these grounds but an employer investing significantly in an LMIA application for a position and worker that could have been handled faster and at lower cost through an LMIA-exempt pathway. Evaluating LMIA-exempt options before committing to the LMIA process is both a cost-saving measure and a risk-reduction strategy.

What to Do After an LMIA Refusal

Receiving a negative LMIA decision is disappointing, but it is not necessarily final. The first step is to read the refusal letter in its entirety and identify every specific reason the officer cited for the negative determination. Some refusal letters are detailed and specific; others are frustratingly general. If the letter is general, consider engaging an RCIC or immigration lawyer to review the complete file and provide a professional assessment of the most likely reasons for the refusal based on the application’s content.

Do not reapply with the same application. A second identical application is very likely to produce a second identical result. Address every identified deficiency in the original application before preparing a new one. If the refusal cited insufficient recruitment, conduct a stronger and better-documented recruitment campaign. If it cited wage below prevailing rate, increase the wage. If it cited inadequate Transition Plan, rewrite the plan with specificity and realistic commitments.

In cases where there is reason to believe the officer made a factual error in the assessment, a request for reconsideration can be submitted to ESDC. This is not a formal appeal mechanism, and ESDC is not obligated to review the file again, but in cases of clear factual error it is worth pursuing. Judicial review in Federal Court is also theoretically available but is typically reserved for cases involving a legal error in the decision, not simply a disagreement with the outcome.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I reapply after an LMIA refusal?

Yes. There is no mandatory waiting period between an LMIA refusal and a new application (except in cases where the refusal was based on employer non-compliance, which may result in a ban from the TFWP). Before reapplying, read the refusal letter in full, identify every cited reason for the negative decision, and address each one specifically in the new application. A resubmission that does not address the refusal reasons will face the same outcome as the original application.

How do I appeal an LMIA decision?

There is no formal administrative appeal mechanism for negative LMIA decisions comparable to the Immigration Appeal Division for IRCC decisions. Options after a negative LMIA include: a request for reconsideration sent to ESDC if there is a specific factual error in the decision, reapplication with a strengthened application addressing the refusal reasons, or in cases of legal error in the decision-making process, judicial review in Federal Court. Consulting with an RCIC or immigration lawyer after a refusal is advisable before choosing a course of action.

How long must I wait before reapplying after an LMIA refusal?

There is no mandatory waiting period for most LMIA refusals. The constraint is practical rather than regulatory: a reapplication submitted before the refusal reasons are addressed will almost certainly fail again. The time needed before reapplying depends on how long it takes to correct the specific deficiencies, which could range from a few weeks (for a documentation inconsistency) to several months (for a new four-week recruitment campaign on different platforms).

Is an LMIA refusal publicly disclosed?

An LMIA refusal itself is not publicly disclosed. What is publicly disclosed is employer non-compliance with LMIA conditions after approval, which is a separate matter from the refusal of an application. ESDC maintains a public list of employers found to be non-compliant with TFWP conditions following an inspection. This list is separate from LMIA refusal decisions, which are communicated only to the employer.

What is the most common reason LMIA applications are refused in Canada?

Based on practitioner experience across hundreds of files, insufficient or non-genuine recruitment effort is the most common reason for LMIA refusals in Canada. This includes both technical failures such as inadequate advertising duration or missing platforms, and substantive failures such as recruitment efforts that were not designed to genuinely attract and consider qualified Canadian applicants. Wage below the prevailing rate for the NOC is the second most common refusal reason, followed by business legitimacy concerns for newer or financially limited employers.
