Many employers who obtain a positive LMIA think primarily about what the LMIA permits: it permits them to hire a specific foreign worker for a specific position without violating Canadian immigration law. What employers less commonly think about is what the LMIA obligates: a comprehensive set of duties toward the worker that begin the moment employment starts and continue for the full duration of the employment period.

These obligations are not merely regulatory formalities. They exist because TFW workers are recognized in Canadian law and policy as a population with structural vulnerabilities that are inherent to the employer-tied temporary immigration status. A worker whose legal right to remain in Canada depends on maintaining employment with a specific employer is in a fundamentally different power relationship with that employer than a Canadian citizen who can simply quit and find another job. Parliament recognized this imbalance and responded with a set of mandatory protections that employers cannot waive, contract around, or ignore.

This article is both an employer guide to meeting these obligations and a worker guide to understanding what you are entitled to under the law. Both perspectives matter, because the most effective protection for workers is workers who know their rights, and the most effective protection for employers is employers who understand and fulfill their obligations.

Obligation 1: Pay the Wages You Promised — Always

The wage obligation is the most fundamental and the most frequently violated employer obligation under the TFWP. Under IRPR 209.3, the employer must pay the TFW the wages stated in the LMIA and the job offer for the entire duration of the employment. This obligation does not diminish because the worker has been employed for a long time, because the business is going through financial difficulties, or because the employer believes the worker is not performing at the expected level.

What the Wage Obligation Requires

The employer must pay the exact wage committed to in the LMIA. If the LMIA stated a wage of $22 per hour, the worker must receive $22 per hour or more for every hour worked. If the LMIA stated an annual salary of $75,000, the worker must receive $75,000 annually or more. Paying a wage lower than the LMIA commitment, even by a small amount, is a violation from the first pay period in which it occurs.

The wage must also comply with provincial employment standards regarding overtime rates, minimum wage thresholds, and any other wage-related requirements. If the provincial minimum wage increases during the TFW’s employment period to a level above the LMIA wage, the employer must adjust the worker’s pay to the new minimum. The LMIA wage is a floor, not a ceiling, and it must always remain at or above both the LMIA commitment and the applicable provincial minimum.

Equal Pay for Equal Work

The employer must pay the TFW wages equal to or greater than wages paid to Canadian employees performing substantially the same duties at the same location. If a Canadian employee in the same position earns $25 per hour and the LMIA committed to $22 per hour for the foreign worker, the employer must pay the foreign worker at least $25 per hour. The LMIA wage cannot be used as a justification for paying TFW workers less than their Canadian counterparts.

From a worker’s perspective, this is one of the most important protections in the TFWP. If you discover that Canadian co-workers in the same position are earning more than you, you have a right to raise this with your employer and, if unresolved, to report it to ESDC. Employers cannot legally pay TFW workers less than equivalent Canadian workers performing the same job in the same workplace.

Prohibited Wage Deductions

Certain deductions from TFW wages are prohibited regardless of what the employer and worker have agreed. Under IRPR 209.2, the employer cannot deduct from wages any amount that was incurred in connection with the employer’s obligations under the TFWP, including LMIA fees, advertising costs, recruitment costs, or any other expenses that are the employer’s responsibility under the program.

Beyond the TFWP-specific prohibition, all wage deductions must comply with applicable provincial employment standards legislation. Unauthorized deductions for tools, uniforms, damaged property, drive-through errors, or other business costs that are not explicitly permissible under provincial law are violations of both the TFWP obligations and the provincial standards. Employers who routinely deduct unauthorized amounts from TFW wages are accumulating compliance risk with every pay period.

Obligation 2: Provide Working Conditions That Match the LMIA and Provincial Standards

The working conditions obligation encompasses everything about the employment relationship beyond the wage. Under IRPR 209.3, the employer must provide working conditions that comply with applicable provincial employment standards legislation and that are consistent with the working conditions described in the LMIA and job offer.

Hours of Work

The hours requirement in the LMIA defines the expected weekly hours of the position. An LMIA for a full-time 40-hour-per-week position cannot be treated as authorization to schedule the worker for 60-hour weeks without overtime compensation. Overtime must be calculated and paid in accordance with provincial employment standards: the applicable overtime threshold (which varies by province), the overtime rate (typically 1.5 times the regular rate), and the method of calculating the overtime hours worked per week.

Employers who need TFW workers to work longer hours than described in the LMIA are not free to simply impose those hours. If the operational reality of the position requires significantly more hours than the LMIA described, the employer should consider whether this represents a material change to the employment conditions that warrants updating the LMIA representation through the appropriate process.

Rest Periods and Days Off

Provincial employment standards set minimum requirements for daily rest periods (the break between the end of one shift and the start of the next) and weekly days of rest. These minimums apply to TFW workers in exactly the same way they apply to Canadian employees. Employers who deny TFW workers rest periods or mandatory days off because of operational pressures are violating both provincial employment standards and the TFWP working conditions obligation.

Vacation Entitlements and Statutory Holidays

TFW workers are entitled to vacation pay and statutory holiday pay in accordance with the employment standards of the province where they work. In most provinces, a minimum of two weeks of vacation (calculated as a percentage of wages) accrues in the first year of employment, increasing with years of service. Statutory holidays entitle workers to either a day off with pay or premium pay for working on the holiday, depending on provincial rules.

Employers who do not track and pay TFW vacation entitlements are accumulating an unpaid liability that will be visible to an ESDC inspector reviewing payroll records. Some employers have assumed that TFW workers, because they are temporary, are not entitled to vacation benefits. They are. The temporary nature of the work permit does not diminish any employment standard that would apply to a Canadian worker in the same position.

Obligation 3: Employ the Worker in the Described Occupation at the Described Location

The occupation and location obligations are the most operationally specific of the employer’s duties. They require the employer to deploy the TFW in the work they were hired to do, at the place they were hired to do it.

Occupation Alignment

Every significant change in a TFW worker’s duties should be assessed against the LMIA occupation description. Routine adjustments within the broad scope of the occupation, such as a cook working on different stations in a kitchen or an engineer working on different aspects of a project, are generally acceptable. But sustained deployment in a different occupation, such as a cook performing primarily cleaning or serving duties, or an engineer primarily performing administrative work, is a violation of the occupation condition.

For workers, the occupation obligation is a protection against being coerced into performing work that was not what you agreed to and that you may not have the training or physical capacity to perform safely. If your employer is regularly assigning you to duties substantially different from those described in your job offer, you have a right to raise this concern with ESDC.

Location Compliance

The TFW must work at the location stated in the LMIA. Temporary deployment to a different location for a brief, defined period may be acceptable in some circumstances, but sustained or permanent relocation to a different work location without notification to ESDC and a corresponding update to the LMIA or work permit is a compliance violation.

Employers who have acquired new locations, merged with other operations, or restructured their business in ways that change where TFW workers are physically located should assess whether these changes affect the work permit and LMIA compliance for affected workers.

Obligation 4: Provide Written Information About Worker Rights at the Time of Hiring

Under IRPR 209.2, employers must provide TFW workers with specific written information about their rights at the time of hiring. This obligation is simple to fulfill and yet is one of the most frequently overlooked employer duties, particularly for employers who are focused on the operational aspects of bringing a new worker on board.

What the Rights Document Must Cover

The worker rights document must describe: the worker’s right to be free from abuse, exploitation, and retaliation; the right to change employers; the right to access emergency healthcare; the right to contact ESDC or a worker protection organization; the prohibition on employers recovering immigration costs; and contact information for ESDC’s TFW worker protection services. ESDC publishes a standard document that covers all required elements; employers can use this document or one that contains equivalent information.

The document must be provided in a language that the worker understands. Providing a rights document only in English to a worker whose primary language is Tagalog, for example, satisfies the letter of the obligation but not the underlying purpose, and ESDC inspectors will assess whether the worker actually understood their rights when interviewing workers during inspections.

The Signed Acknowledgment

Employers should obtain the worker’s signed and dated acknowledgment of receipt of the rights document and retain this acknowledgment in the compliance file. The acknowledgment is the employer’s primary evidence that this obligation was fulfilled. An employer who verbally provided rights information but has no written record will have difficulty demonstrating compliance if an inspector asks an interviewed worker whether they received this information and the worker says no or does not remember.

Obligation 5: Protect Workers from Abuse, Exploitation, and Retaliation

The anti-abuse and anti-retaliation obligations are the most fundamental worker protections in the TFWP framework and, when violated, attract the most serious penalty consequences. These obligations exist because the structural vulnerability of TFW workers, whose immigration status is tied to a specific employer, creates conditions that can be exploited by unscrupulous actors.

The Prohibition on Abuse and Exploitation

Employers cannot physically harm workers, sexually assault workers, psychologically abuse workers, or engage in any conduct toward workers that a reasonable person would recognize as abusive or exploitative. These prohibitions should be obvious and yet cases documented by ESDC, labour standards offices, and worker protection organizations confirm that they are not universally observed.

Beyond direct physical or psychological abuse, exploitation through the employment relationship takes more subtle forms that are equally prohibited. Confiscating a worker’s passport or immigration documents is one of the most clearly prohibited forms of exploitation. It is also a criminal offence under the Criminal Code of Canada, and employers who confiscate worker documents face both criminal prosecution and ESDC compliance consequences including permanent ban from the TFWP.

Using the worker’s immigration status as a threat or coercive tool, for example telling a worker who raises a safety concern that they will be deported if they do not comply, is a specific form of psychological exploitation that is prohibited and that ESDC treats with particular seriousness. Workers in this situation should contact ESDC’s TFW worker protection line or a community legal organization immediately.

The Anti-Retaliation Obligation

Employers cannot take adverse action against a TFW worker in response to the worker exercising their rights under the TFWP. This includes the worker filing a complaint with ESDC, participating in an ESDC inspection interview, contacting a union or worker protection organization, or asserting rights under provincial employment standards. Retaliation can take the form of termination, demotion, reduced hours, changed working conditions, or any other negative employment consequence imposed because the worker exercised their rights.

The anti-retaliation protection is one of the most important structural features of the TFWP compliance framework because it is what makes worker complaints viable as an enforcement mechanism. Without this protection, workers who are being abused or exploited would have every rational reason to remain silent for fear of losing their job and their immigration status simultaneously. The protection is only as effective as workers’ knowledge of it, which is why the rights information document requirement exists.

Obligation 6: Low-Wage Stream Specific Obligations

Adequate and Free Housing

Low-Wage stream employers must ensure that foreign workers who are not locally based have access to adequate housing. For most Low-Wage stream contexts, this means providing employer-supplied housing that meets provincial habitability standards at no charge to the worker. The housing must be safe, clean, and consistent with the number of occupants. Overcrowding, hazardous conditions, or housing that would not meet the standards applied to Canadian tenants is a violation of the LMIA conditions.

The housing must be provided free of charge or at a charge that is within the limits set by applicable provincial standards. Charging workers for housing at rates above what the provincial employment standards permit is both a Low-Wage stream compliance violation and potentially an illegal deduction from wages. Employers who operate housing as a revenue-generating activity, charging workers full market rates or above for accommodation in employer-owned or leased properties, are almost certainly in violation.

Round-Trip Transportation

Low-Wage stream employers are obligated to pay for round-trip transportation for the foreign worker from their country of origin to the Canadian work location and back at the end of employment. This obligation covers international airfare, domestic transportation from the arrival airport to the work site, and return transportation at the end of the employment period.

The return transportation obligation is frequently neglected because it arises at the end of the employment relationship when the employer’s attention is elsewhere. An employer who paid for the worker’s initial airfare but does not arrange or fund the return flight at the end of employment is in violation of the Low-Wage stream conditions. ESDC inspectors may ask at later inspections whether return transportation was provided to workers whose employment has ended.

Private Health Insurance During the Coverage Gap

Most Canadian provinces have a waiting period before new residents become eligible for provincial health coverage. During this waiting period, Low-Wage stream employers are obligated to provide or arrange private health insurance for the foreign worker covering emergency healthcare and essential medical services.

Workers who experience medical emergencies during the coverage gap period must be able to receive care without being denied treatment due to lack of coverage. Employers who have not arranged the required coverage and whose workers incur medical expenses during the gap period face both the compliance finding and potential civil liability for unpaid medical costs that resulted from their failure to provide required coverage.

What Workers Should Do If Their Rights Are Being Violated

Workers who believe their employer is not meeting the obligations described in this article have several avenues available to them. The most direct is contacting ESDC’s TFW worker protection line at 1-800-367-5693, which allows workers to report concerns about employer compliance confidentially. Workers can also contact provincial labour standards offices about violations of provincial employment standards, contact the Migrant Workers Centre or other worker support organizations for guidance and advocacy support, and if facing imminent physical danger, contact local police.

Workers on employer-specific work permits who are leaving an abusive employment situation should know that they may be eligible for a Vulnerable Worker Open Work Permit, which provides work authorization that is not tied to the specific employer while the situation is addressed. This pathway is specifically designed to address the structural vulnerability that the employer-tied permit creates, and workers in abusive situations should ask about it immediately.

TFW Worker Protection Resources

TFW Worker Protection Line: 1-800-367-5693 (toll-free, confidential). Provincial labour standards offices can be reached through each province’s official government website. The Migrant Workers Centre (various cities), Metro Toronto Chinese and Southeast Asian Legal Clinic, and other community legal organizations provide free advocacy and legal support to TFW workers. Workers are protected against retaliation for contacting any of these organizations.

Frequently Asked Questions

What must an employer provide TFW workers in Canada?

Employers must provide: wages equal to those committed in the LMIA throughout the employment period; working conditions consistent with the LMIA and applicable provincial employment standards including hours, overtime, rest periods, and vacation; employment in the described occupation at the described location; written information about the worker’s rights at the time of hiring; a workplace free from abuse, exploitation, and retaliation; and for Low-Wage stream positions: free adequate housing, round-trip transportation, and private health insurance during the provincial coverage waiting period. These obligations apply throughout the worker’s employment, not just at the start.

Can an employer reduce a TFW’s wage after LMIA approval?

No. The employer must pay the TFW the wages committed to in the LMIA for the full duration of the employment. Reducing wages below the LMIA commitment at any point during the employment period is a violation of the wages compliance condition under IRPR 209.3 regardless of the employer’s reason. If the employer genuinely needs to change the wage structure, the appropriate process is to apply for a new LMIA reflecting the new wage. Unilateral wage reductions are non-compliant from the first pay period in which they occur.

Are employers required to provide housing to low-wage TFWs?

Yes. Low-Wage stream employers must ensure that foreign workers who are not local have access to adequate housing. For most Low-Wage stream situations, this means providing employer-supplied housing at no charge or at a charge within the limits of provincial employment standards. The housing must meet applicable provincial habitability standards for safety, cleanliness, and occupancy density. Charging workers market-rate rent for employer-supplied housing, or providing housing that does not meet habitability standards, is a compliance violation.

What rights do LMIA workers have in Canada?

TFW workers on LMIA-based work permits have the right to: receive the wages stated in the LMIA equal to those paid to Canadian employees in the same position; work the hours and in the conditions described in the LMIA and compliant with provincial employment standards; work in the occupation described in the LMIA; be free from physical, sexual, and psychological abuse; not have their immigration documents confiscated; not be threatened or retaliated against for raising workplace concerns or exercising legal rights; contact ESDC or worker protection organizations confidentially; and, in situations of abuse, access a Vulnerable Worker Open Work Permit to leave the employer without losing work authorization.

What happens if a TFW worker files a complaint against their employer?

A worker complaint triggers an ESDC complaint-driven inspection of the employer. The complaint can be filed confidentially with ESDC at 1-800-367-5693. Employers cannot legally retaliate against workers for filing complaints or participating in ESDC inspections. Retaliation against a worker for exercising their rights under the TFWP is a separate compliance violation that can result in additional penalties and is treated with particular seriousness by ESDC. Workers who face retaliation after filing a complaint should document the retaliatory action and report it to ESDC immediately.
