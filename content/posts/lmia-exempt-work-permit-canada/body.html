Most Canadian employers hiring a foreign worker first need a Labour Market Impact Assessment (LMIA), a process that involves weeks of advertising, a $1,000 fee per position and months of waiting. But a large share of work permits are issued without one. Through the International Mobility Program, Canada exempts many workers from the LMIA because their work brings broader economic, cultural or competitive benefits, or because an international agreement requires it.

This guide sets out the main LMIA-exempt work permit categories in Canada in 2026, explains how each one works, and walks employers and workers through the steps to use them correctly.

Quick Summary

LMIA-exempt work permits fall under the International Mobility Program. Major categories include international trade agreements such as CUSMA, CETA, CPTPP and the Canada-UK agreement; Canadian interests, such as significant benefit, reciprocal employment, intra-company transfers and francophone mobility; open work permits, such as PGWP, bridging and spousal permits; and humanitarian categories. Employer-specific LMIA-exempt permits usually require the employer to pay a $230 compliance fee and submit an offer of employment through the Employer Portal.

How LMIA Exemptions Work

An LMIA tests whether hiring a foreign worker will hurt the Canadian labour market. An LMIA exemption means the government has decided, in advance, that certain types of work do not need that test. Exemptions come from the Immigration and Refugee Protection Regulations, mainly sections 204 (international agreements), 205 (Canadian interests), 206 (no other means of support) and 207 (permanent residence applicants), plus public policies.

Category 2: Canadian Interests

Significant benefit

For work that brings significant social, cultural or economic benefits to Canada. Officers look for strong evidence, such as awards, expertise, media recognition or a clear economic impact.

Intra-company transferees

For employees of multinational companies transferring to a related Canadian office as executives, senior managers or specialized knowledge workers. IRCC uses separate exemption codes for new enterprises, executives and managers, and specialized knowledge workers.

Reciprocal employment

For work where Canadians enjoy similar opportunities abroad, including International Experience Canada (working holiday, young professionals and international co-op) and certain exchange programs.

Francophone mobility

For French-speaking workers hired outside Quebec in many occupations, supporting francophone communities across Canada.

Research, academic and charitable work

Including certain researchers, guest lecturers, post-doctoral fellows, and charitable or religious workers.

Category 3: Open Work Permits

Open work permits are not tied to one employer, so no offer of employment is required. Common types include:

  • Post-Graduation Work Permit for eligible graduates of Canadian institutions.
  • Bridging open work permit for eligible economic PR applicants.
  • Spousal open work permits for eligible spouses of skilled workers and certain students, under rules narrowed in January 2025.
  • Open work permits for sponsored spouses in Canada.
  • Open work permits for vulnerable workers experiencing abuse.

Category 4: Humanitarian and Other Categories

These include work permits for refugee claimants, protected persons and certain people who cannot support themselves without working, as well as public policies created for specific situations.

Employer Steps for LMIA-Exempt Hiring

Step 1: Confirm the exemption

Identify the correct exemption and confirm your worker meets every requirement.

Step 2: Pay the employer compliance fee

Pay the $230 employer compliance fee, unless an exemption from the fee applies.

Step 3: Submit an offer of employment

Submit the offer through the IRCC Employer Portal. You receive a 7-digit offer of employment number.

Step 4: Share the number with your worker

The worker uses the number to apply for the work permit.

Step 5: Stay compliant

Respect the job, wage and working conditions in your offer, and keep records for possible inspections.

Common Mistakes With LMIA Exemptions

  • Choosing an exemption that does not fit the worker’s actual job.
  • Submitting weak evidence for significant benefit claims.
  • Failing to pay the compliance fee or submit the offer of employment.
  • Changing job duties or wages without considering compliance rules.
  • Assuming every exemption allows applying at the border; most flagpoling ended in December 2024.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an LMIA-exempt work permit?

An LMIA-exempt work permit is a Canadian work permit issued without a Labour Market Impact Assessment, usually under the International Mobility Program. Exemptions apply when work brings significant benefits to Canada, when an international agreement requires it, or for specific groups such as eligible graduates, spouses and permanent residence applicants.

Which jobs are LMIA-exempt in Canada?

Exemptions depend on the worker’s situation rather than the job alone. Common categories include CUSMA and other trade agreement professionals, intra-company transferees, International Experience Canada participants, francophone mobility workers, significant benefit cases and open work permit holders such as PGWP and bridging open work permit holders.

Do employers pay a fee for LMIA-exempt work permits?

For most employer-specific LMIA-exempt work permits, the employer pays a $230 employer compliance fee and submits an offer of employment through the IRCC Employer Portal. Employers do not need to pay this fee or submit an offer for workers who hold open work permits.

Is the francophone mobility work permit LMIA-exempt?

Yes. The francophone mobility program allows eligible French-speaking workers to receive LMIA-exempt work permits for jobs outside Quebec in many occupations. Employers still need to submit an offer of employment and pay the employer compliance fee, and workers must meet the program’s French language requirement.
