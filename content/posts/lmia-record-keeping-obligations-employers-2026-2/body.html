When an ESDC compliance inspector arrives at your workplace, announced or not, the single most important factor determining the outcome of that inspection is the quality and completeness of your records. An employer with perfect compliance practices but poor record-keeping is nearly indistinguishable from a non-compliant employer in the eyes of an inspector who cannot verify what the employer claims. An employer with a genuine, documented compliance record, organized and immediately producible, can demonstrate compliance within the first hour of any inspection.

The six-year record-keeping obligation for LMIA employers is not an administrative nicety. It is a legal requirement grounded in IRPR 209.9, which requires employers to retain evidence of compliance with the LMIA conditions for six years from the date of the LMIA issuance. Failure to maintain adequate records is itself a compliance deficiency that can be cited as a finding in an inspection, independent of whether the underlying employment conditions were actually compliant.

This guide covers every category of document that ESDC inspectors look for, the specific content standards for each type of record, the format requirements (physical versus digital), the organization approach that serves employers best during an inspection, and the most common record-keeping failures that put otherwise-compliant employers at risk.

The Six-Year Retention Rule: What It Means in Practice

The six-year retention period runs from the date of LMIA issuance, not from the date of the worker’s arrival or the date of the worker’s departure. This means that if an LMIA was issued in January 2020 and the worker worked under that LMIA from April 2020 to March 2022, the employer must retain all compliance documentation for that position until January 2026. An ESDC inspector conducting an inspection in 2025 can validly request all compliance records from 2020.

This six-year horizon means that record-keeping must be designed for longevity. Records stored in formats that become inaccessible over time, on platforms that may be discontinued, or in physical locations that may not be maintained for six years create genuine retention risk. An employer who stored payroll records in a cloud system that was discontinued two years ago and cannot produce those records during an inspection is in a difficult position, regardless of whether the underlying wages were actually compliant.

The Trigger Date Matters

The LMIA issuance date, not the employment start date or end date, is the trigger for the retention period. For employers who had a positive LMIA but for some reason the worker never arrived (perhaps the work permit was refused, or the worker changed their mind), the retention obligation still runs from the LMIA issuance date. All documentation generated during the application process, including the LMIA letter, the job offer, and any ESDC correspondence, must be retained for six years from that date.

Category 1: The LMIA Application Record

Every employer must retain the complete LMIA application file, including all documents submitted to ESDC in support of the application. This includes:

  • The positive LMIA letter issued by ESDC, including all conditions and the expiry date stated on the letter.
  • The LMIA application form as submitted, including all schedules and supplementary forms.
  • All supporting documents included in the LMIA submission: business registration, financial statements, the job offer as submitted, the transition or accommodation plan, all recruitment advertising copies with dates, and the recruitment log with all applicant records.
  • Any correspondence with ESDC during the application process, including requests for information and the employer’s responses.
  • The IRCC Employer Portal job offer submission confirmation and the associated reference number, if the employer submitted a job offer to IRCC for Express Entry purposes.

The LMIA application record serves two compliance purposes. First, it establishes the baseline commitments against which the employer’s actual employment practices will be measured during an inspection. Second, it provides evidence that the LMIA was legitimately obtained through a genuine application process, which is relevant to the business legitimacy compliance condition.

Category 2: Wage and Payroll Records

Wage compliance is the most actively inspected condition and the condition that generates the most non-compliance findings. Robust payroll records are the primary defence against a wage compliance finding. These records must demonstrate, for every pay period of the worker’s employment, that the wages actually paid were consistent with the wages committed to in the LMIA.

Required Payroll Documents

  • Payroll records for each pay period showing: the pay period dates, the worker’s name and employee ID, the hours worked during the period (regular and overtime separately), the gross wages calculated from those hours, any authorized deductions and their amounts, and the net wages paid.
  • Payroll run confirmations or payroll register entries showing the employer’s total payroll for each period, providing context for the individual worker’s wages within the broader payroll.
  • Bank transfer records or cancelled cheques confirming payment to the worker in the amount shown on the payroll records. The payment method must create an auditable trail from the payroll calculation to the actual transfer of funds to the worker.
  • Pay stubs issued to the worker for each pay period, which must include the same information as the payroll records.
  • Annual T4 slips issued to the worker for each year of employment, which ESDC uses to cross-reference aggregate annual wages against the committed wage rate.
  • Records of any wage increases, decreases, or changes applied during the employment period, with the effective date and reason documented.

The Equal Pay Documentation

ESDC inspectors compare the wages paid to the TFW against wages paid to Canadian employees in the same or substantially similar positions at the same location. Employers must retain payroll records for all employees, not just TFW workers, to be able to demonstrate this comparison. If a Canadian employee doing substantially the same work as the TFW was paid more, this constitutes a wage compliance failure even if the TFW’s wage was at or above the LMIA commitment.

For employers who give raises to Canadian staff during the TFW’s employment period, those raises must be reflected in corresponding adjustments to the TFW’s wage to maintain the equal pay condition. Employers who routinely give annual wage increases to Canadian employees but do not update TFW wages commensurately are accumulating a compliance problem with every pay cycle. Record-keeping that documents both tracks of wage history makes this comparison straightforward.

Documentation of Authorized Deductions

Any deductions from the TFW’s wages must be authorized under applicable provincial employment standards and must be documented as authorized. Permissible deductions typically include income tax, employment insurance premiums, Canada Pension Plan contributions, and any deductions explicitly authorized by the worker in writing for specific purposes such as employee benefits contributions. Deductions for accommodation are permissible in some provinces for Low-Wage stream employers but subject to maximum amounts set by provincial standards.

Employers must retain the written authorization for any discretionary deductions, the calculation basis for each deduction, and the confirmation that the deduction amount was within the permissible limits. Unauthorized deductions, or deductions that exceed permissible limits, are compliance violations even if the employer believed they were appropriate.

Category 3: Hours-of-Work and Attendance Records

The hours-of-work condition requires that the TFW’s actual hours be consistent with the employment standards of the applicable province and with the hours described in the LMIA. Attendance records must document:

  • The start and end time of each shift worked by the TFW, for every day of the employment period.
  • Total regular hours per day and per week, with overtime hours identified separately.
  • Rest days, days off, and any paid leave periods.
  • Any statutory holidays and whether they were worked, and whether the worker was compensated in accordance with provincial holiday pay requirements.
  • Any periods of absence including sick leave, vacation leave, or other authorized leave, and the basis for payment during those absences.

Time records should be maintained in a system that is not retrospectively alterable without creating an audit trail. Paper time sheets signed by both the worker and the supervisor are a traditional and legally defensible format. Digital time tracking systems that create tamper-evident records are an equally acceptable and often more practical approach for employers with larger workforces.

Inspector Alert: Paper Time Sheets Signed Only by Supervisors

ESDC inspectors are alert to time records that are completed solely by supervisory staff without any worker acknowledgment. Records that show the supervisor’s signature but not the worker’s may be viewed with skepticism because they do not demonstrate that the worker was aware of or agreed with the hours recorded. Where feasible, having workers sign or electronically confirm their time records for each pay period creates a more legally defensible record than supervisor-only documentation.

Category 4: Occupation Evidence

The occupation compliance condition requires that the TFW be employed in the occupation described in the LMIA throughout the employment period. Documenting occupation compliance is less intuitive than documenting wage compliance, because there is no standard ledger entry that tracks what duties a worker performed on any given day. Employers need to create occupation evidence through other means.

  • Job descriptions that accurately reflect the duties the worker is performing, updated whenever duties change significantly, and signed by both the employer and the worker.
  • Supervisor notes or logs confirming the worker’s primary activities during regular review periods.
  • Production or service delivery records that show the worker contributing to the outputs associated with the LMIA occupation (a cook’s contribution to meal production records, an engineer’s contribution to project deliverables, a nurse’s patient care records).
  • Any written directives, task assignments, or performance evaluations that reference the worker’s occupational activities.
  • Evidence of training or professional development activities consistent with the LMIA occupation.

Employers should be attentive to situations where operational pressures lead to task reassignment outside the LMIA occupation. Any significant or sustained reassignment of duties outside the LMIA occupation should be treated as a potential compliance event, not just an operational decision. If the duties have changed substantially and permanently, the employer should seek advice on whether a new LMIA is required rather than simply documenting the new duties as if they were consistent with the original LMIA.

Category 5: Work Location Records

The location compliance condition requires that the TFW work at the location described in the LMIA. For most employers this is straightforward: the workplace is a fixed address and the worker reports to it consistently. But for employers with multiple locations, contractors who send workers to client sites, businesses with mobile workforces, and employers who have adopted hybrid work arrangements, the location condition creates specific documentation requirements.

  • Attendance records that specify the location where work was performed, particularly for employers with multiple sites.
  • For hybrid or remote work arrangements: a documented written agreement specifying the remote work location, the employer’s approval of that location, and any conditions on the arrangement.
  • For mobile workers (service technicians, tradespeople, delivery workers): records of job sites or service locations visited, not simply a general confirmation of employment with the employer.
  • For workers temporarily deployed to client sites: documentation confirming that the work performed at the client site is consistent with the LMIA occupation and that the primary employer relationship remains with the LMIA employer.

Category 6: Worker Rights Information Records

Under IRPR 209.2, employers must provide TFW workers with written information about their rights under the TFWP at the time of hiring. Retaining documentation that this information was actually provided is a separate record-keeping obligation from all other categories.

  • A copy of the Worker Rights information document as provided to the worker. ESDC publishes a standard document for this purpose; employers can use this document or one that contains equivalent information in plain language.
  • Evidence that the document was provided in a language the worker understands, not only in English or French if the worker’s primary language is different.
  • A signed and dated acknowledgment from the worker confirming receipt of the document. This acknowledgment should ideally confirm the worker’s understanding of the key rights described, not simply acknowledgment of receipt.
  • For employers who provide rights information verbally in addition to the written document: notes of the conversation, the date, and who was present.

Category 7: Low-Wage Stream Specific Records

Employers operating in the Low-Wage stream have additional record-keeping obligations related to the accommodation, transportation, and health insurance conditions that are specific to this stream.

Accommodation Records

  • Documentation of the housing provided: address, description of the premises, number of bedrooms, number of occupants.
  • Evidence that the accommodation meets applicable provincial habitability standards: any provincial inspection certificates, housing assessments, or self-assessment documentation.
  • Records of the cost charged to the worker for accommodation, confirming this is within permissible limits and that no amounts were charged that would violate the prohibition on recovering LMIA costs.
  • Any written housing agreements signed between the employer and the worker specifying the terms of the accommodation arrangement.

Transportation Records

  • Documentation of the round-trip transportation provided or arranged for the worker: flight booking confirmations, transportation contracts, or receipts showing the employer bore the transportation cost.
  • Confirmation that return transportation was arranged and paid for at the end of the employment period.
  • Any receipts or records of domestic transportation provided between the arrival airport and the work location.

Health Insurance Records

  • Documentation of the private health insurance coverage provided during any period before provincial health coverage begins.
  • Insurance policy details showing the effective dates of coverage and the coverage amounts.
  • Confirmation that the worker was enrolled in provincial workers’ compensation insurance as required.

Digital vs. Paper Records: What ESDC Accepts

ESDC accepts both digital and paper records as compliance documentation, provided the records meet three standards: they must be legible, they must be complete, and they must be accessible on request during an inspection.

Digital Record Requirements

Digital records stored in cloud systems, accounting software, HRIS platforms, or other electronic formats are acceptable provided the employer can access and print or display them during an inspection. Records stored in systems that require internet connectivity should have backup access mechanisms in case of connectivity issues. Records stored in formats that have become obsolete or that require software the employer no longer has must be migrated to accessible formats as part of ongoing record maintenance.

Electronic payroll systems used by employers should produce records that are audit-trail capable, meaning that any retroactive changes to records are logged with the date, the person who made the change, and the reason for the change. Inspectors who find evidence of retroactive alterations to payroll records without an audit trail will treat this as a serious concern regardless of what the altered records show.

Physical Record Requirements

Physical records must be stored in a location and format that prevents deterioration over the six-year retention period. Paper documents stored in damp basements, in files that have become disorganized over several years, or in offsite storage facilities that would take days to retrieve from during an inspection are all problematic. The ability to produce any requested record within the first day of an inspection is the practical standard that should guide storage and organization decisions.

Recommended Record Organization System

The most practical record organization system for LMIA compliance purposes is a position-specific file (physical or digital) for each TFW position, containing all compliance records for that position organized chronologically within each category. A cover sheet listing the LMIA number, the worker’s name, the work permit number, the employment start and end dates, and the LMIA issuance and expiry dates creates an at-a-glance index that allows an inspector to quickly identify the parameters of the file they are reviewing.

Within each position file, organize records into labeled sections corresponding to the six compliance conditions: wages, working conditions, occupation, location, worker rights, and business legitimacy. Within each section, organize documents chronologically from oldest to most recent. This organization mirrors the structure of an ESDC inspection assessment and makes the inspector’s review as efficient as possible.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do employers need to keep LMIA records?

Employers must retain all documentation relevant to LMIA compliance conditions for six years from the date of the LMIA issuance. This is not from the date of the worker’s arrival or departure but from the LMIA issuance date. For example, an LMIA issued in June 2020 requires retention of all associated compliance records until June 2026. ESDC inspectors can request records from any point within this six-year window during a compliance inspection.

What records must I keep for an ESDC compliance inspection?

ESDC inspectors assess compliance with six conditions and expect documentation for each. Required records include: payroll records, pay stubs, T4s, and bank transfer records (wages); time and attendance records (working conditions); job descriptions and task assignment logs (occupation); location attendance records (work location); a signed worker rights acknowledgment and copy of the rights document (worker information); and financial statements and business registration (business legitimacy). Low-Wage stream employers must also retain housing, transportation, and health insurance records.

Can I store LMIA compliance documents digitally?

Yes. ESDC accepts digital records provided they are legible, complete, accessible during an inspection, and maintained in formats that remain readable over the full six-year retention period. Electronic payroll systems should be audit-trail capable, meaning retroactive changes are logged with dates and the identity of the person making the change. Records stored in cloud platforms should have backup access mechanisms. Paper records are equally acceptable if stored in organized, accessible, deterioration-resistant formats.

Do I need to keep payroll records for Canadian employees as well as TFW workers?

Yes. ESDC inspectors compare the wages paid to TFW workers against wages paid to Canadian employees performing the same or substantially similar work at the same location. To demonstrate equal pay compliance, employers must retain payroll records for all employees in comparable positions, not just TFW workers. If Canadian employees received wage increases during the TFW’s employment period, those increases must be reflected in the TFW’s wage records as well, otherwise the equal pay condition is violated.

What happens if I cannot produce records during an ESDC inspection?

Failure to produce requested records during an ESDC inspection is treated as a compliance deficiency and can be cited as a separate finding in addition to any substantive non-compliance identified through other means. Inspectors cannot verify compliance without documentation, and the absence of records shifts the assessment toward a finding of non-compliance for the conditions that the missing records were intended to document. In practice, employers who cannot produce payroll records are assessed as potentially non-compliant on the wages condition regardless of whether the actual wages were paid correctly.
