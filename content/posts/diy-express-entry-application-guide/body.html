Every year, thousands of skilled workers prepare their own Express Entry applications for Canadian permanent residence. Many succeed. Others are refused for reasons that had nothing to do with whether they deserved to immigrate: a NOC code that did not match their duties, a reference letter that left out hours, a language score entered one band too high or a police certificate missing for a country they lived in years ago. In almost every case, the problem was preventable.

This guide is written for applicants who want to file on their own and do it properly. It does not just tell you what to upload. It explains the legal standard behind each part of your application under the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act (IRPA) and the Immigration and Refugee Protection Regulations (IRPR), shows you how officers assess each element and gives you practical methods, checklists and examples to build an application with no weak points. No one can guarantee an outcome, because only IRCC officers make decisions. But you can control the quality of what you submit, and that is what this guide is about.

Quick Summary

A strong Express Entry application is one in which every claim is accurate, every requirement is met under the IRPR and every point in your profile is proven by a document. To build one: confirm which program you qualify for under sections 75, 87.1 or 87.2 of the IRPR; choose your NOC code by matching your real duties to the lead statement and main duties; obtain reference letters that state titles, dates, hours, salary and detailed duties; claim only Comprehensive Ranking System (CRS) points you can prove; audit your profile before you accept an Invitation to Apply (ITA); and submit a complete application, including police certificates and upfront medical exam information, within 60 days. Section 11.2 of IRPA requires you to meet the criteria and hold the qualifications you were ranked on both when invited and when IRCC receives your application.

Part 1: What "A Strong Application" Means in Law

Before you open your IRCC account, it helps to understand how the law sees your application. Three principles shape everything that follows.

Principle 1: You must meet the requirements at two moments

Subsection 11.2(1) of IRPA provides that an officer may not issue a permanent resident visa to an invited applicant who, at the time the invitation was issued or at the time the officer received the application, did not meet the criteria or did not have the qualifications on which they were ranked. In plain terms, your profile and your application must both be true and provable on those dates. Subsection 11.2(2) provides limited exceptions, such as a birthday after your invitation that lowers your age points.

Principle 2: The burden of proof is on you

Subsection 16(1) of IRPA requires every applicant to answer truthfully all questions put to them and to produce all relevant evidence and documents that the officer reasonably requires. Officers do not have to search for evidence on your behalf, and they generally do not have to warn you about evidence the law already requires you to provide.

Principle 3: Accuracy is not optional

Paragraph 40(1)(a) of IRPA makes a person inadmissible for directly or indirectly misrepresenting or withholding material facts that induce or could induce an error in the administration of the Act. Under paragraph 40(2)(a), that inadmissibility lasts five years. An application that is “mostly true” is not a strong application. Every detail matters.

The golden rule of DIY applications

Write this down and keep it next to your computer: Do not enter anything in your profile or application that you cannot prove with a document. If you follow that rule throughout, you will avoid most of the problems that lead to refusals.

Part 2: Confirm Which Program You Actually Qualify For

Express Entry manages applications for three economic programs. Your CRS score decides whether you are invited, but your program decides whether you are eligible. Many applicants focus on their score and overlook the program requirements entirely.

Work that does not count

Paragraph 87.1(3) of the IRPR excludes certain periods from Canadian Experience Class work experience:

  • Work done while you were engaged in full-time study.
  • Self-employment.
  • Unauthorized work, such as work outside your permit conditions.
  • Work done without temporary resident status.

For all programs, volunteer work and unpaid internships do not count, because qualifying experience must be paid work. Section 2 of the IRPR defines work as an activity for which wages are paid or commission is earned.

Calculating full-time and part-time work

Section 73(1) of the IRPR defines full-time work as at least 30 hours of work over a period of one week. IRCC generally treats one year of full-time work as 1,560 hours. Part-time work can count if it adds up to the equivalent, for example 15 hours a week for two years. Hours above 30 per week do not make your experience count faster.

A practical eligibility self-check

  • Write down every job you held in the last 10 years, with exact start and end dates.
  • Mark the hours per week for each job.
  • Mark whether each job was in Canada or abroad.
  • Cross out any period of full-time study, self-employment for CEC, unauthorized work or unpaid work.
  • Add up what remains for each program you might qualify for.

If you do not clearly meet at least one program’s requirements, stop here and get advice before you create a profile.

Part 3: Choosing the Right NOC Code

Choosing your National Occupational Classification (NOC) code is the single most consequential decision in a DIY application. It affects your program eligibility, your CRS points for work experience, your eligibility for category-based draws and how an officer reads every reference letter you submit.

The legal test

For each program, the IRPR requires that you performed the actions described in the lead statement of your occupation and a substantial number of its main duties, including all of the essential duties. This test appears in paragraphs 75(2)(b) and (c) for the Federal Skilled Worker class and paragraphs 87.1(2)(b) and (c) for the Canadian Experience Class. The Federal Skilled Trades class applies a similar duties-based assessment for trade occupations.

The key insight: your NOC code is determined by your duties, not your job title, your employer’s preference or a list of in-demand jobs.

Use this six-step method to choose your NOC code.

Step 1: Write your real duties first

Before you open the NOC website, write down what you actually did in a typical week, in your own words. List 8 to 12 tasks, with the most important first. Include who you reported to, who reported to you and what decisions you made.

Step 2: Search the official NOC 2021 database

Search using keywords from your duties, not only your title. Read several possible occupations, not just the first result.

Step 3: Test the lead statement

Read the lead statement for each candidate occupation. Ask yourself honestly: does this sentence describe my job overall? If it describes a more senior or more technical role, it is probably not your code.

Step 4: Test the main duties

Compare your list with the main duties. You should have performed a substantial number of them, including those that define the occupation. Mark each one you performed.

Step 5: Check the TEER level and employment requirements

Confirm the occupation’s Training, Education, Experience and Responsibilities (TEER) level qualifies for your program. Review the typical employment requirements, such as education or licensing, and consider whether your background is consistent with them.

Step 6: Check consistency with everything else

Make sure the NOC you choose is consistent with your salary, your employer’s business and your resume. A “Financial Manager” earning a junior clerk’s wage in a small retail store will draw scrutiny.

Warning signWhat it may mean
Your title is senior but your duties are operationalYour NOC may be a supervisor or worker code, not a manager code

Your employer’s business does not match the occupationThe role may not exist as described

When you held several jobs

Each period of work is assessed separately. If you were promoted, list each role with its own dates and NOC code. Only the periods that match a qualifying NOC count toward your program and your CRS experience points.

Category-based draws and your NOC

If you hope to be invited in a category-based draw, such as healthcare, STEM, trades, education or transport, your NOC must appear on that category’s list and your experience must meet the category requirement. According to IRCC, most occupation-based categories require at least 12 months of full-time work, or an equal amount of part-time work, in a single eligible occupation within the past three years. The experience does not need to be continuous. Never choose a NOC to fit a category; choose the NOC that fits your work and then see which categories you qualify for.

Part 4: Getting an Experience Letter That Proves Your Case

Your employment reference letter is the main evidence of your work experience. A strong letter allows an officer to confirm, without guessing, that you meet every element of the legal test: the right occupation, the right duties, the right hours, the right dates and paid employment.

How to ask your employer

Employers are often happy to help but do not know what IRCC needs. Make it easy for them:

  • Send a short, polite request explaining that the letter is for a Canadian permanent residence application.
  • Provide a checklist of the required elements.
  • Provide your own written list of duties for them to review, edit and confirm in their own words.
  • Ask them to use their real letterhead and sign the letter.
  • Ask for the letter well before you need it, ideally before you enter the pool.

Never ask an employer to state anything that is not true, and never write and sign a letter on an employer’s behalf.

Writing duties that work

Strong duties are specific, factual and written in the employer’s own words. They describe what you did, how often and with what responsibility. Weak duties are vague or copied word for word from the NOC description, which can lead an officer to question whether the letter reflects your real job.

Weak dutyStronger duty
Responsible for accountsPrepared monthly bank reconciliations for four company accounts and resolved discrepancies with the bank
Handled customersResponded to an average of 40 customer service requests per day by phone and email and escalated complex complaints to the operations manager
Managed staffScheduled and supervised a team of six warehouse associates, assigned daily tasks and conducted quarterly performance reviews
Did software developmentDesigned and coded new features for the company’s web-based booking platform using JavaScript and Python and reviewed code submitted by two junior developers

Sample reference letter structure

The example below shows the structure and level of detail a strong letter usually contains. It is an illustration only. Your letter must describe your real job, in your employer’s own words, and must not copy this text or the NOC description.

If you held several roles with one employer

The letter should list each role separately, with its own title, dates, hours, salary and duties. Do not combine years in different roles into one description.

When your employer will not cooperate

Some employers refuse to provide letters, have closed or cannot be reached. In that case, you can generally submit:

  • A letter of explanation describing why a standard letter is not available and what you did to obtain one.
  • A sworn statement, such as a statutory declaration or affidavit, setting out your job titles, dates, hours, salary and duties.
  • Supporting evidence, such as employment contracts, offer letters, pay stubs, tax documents, T4 slips, Records of Employment and bank statements showing salary deposits.
  • Where possible, a letter from a former supervisor or colleague confirming your role.

The more independent evidence you provide, the more weight an officer is likely to give your sworn statement.

Self-employment

Self-employment cannot be counted for the Canadian Experience Class under paragraph 87.1(3) of the IRPR. For the Federal Skilled Worker class, self-employment can count, but it requires strong third-party evidence, such as business registration, tax filings, client contracts, invoices, letters from clients and proof of payment.

Proving that your work was paid

Officers increasingly look for traceable evidence that you were paid. Practitioners have reported refusals where salary could not be traced. Include supporting evidence such as pay stubs, tax slips, Notices of Assessment and bank statements showing salary deposits, especially if any part of your pay was in cash.

Part 5: Claiming CRS Points You Can Prove

The Comprehensive Ranking System is set out in Ministerial Instructions issued under section 10.3 of IRPA. Your CRS score determines whether you are invited, but under section 11.2, every point must be supportable at the time of invitation and when your application is received. The table below summarizes the main factors and the evidence each requires.

AgePassport or birth certificate

Remember that, since March 25, 2025, a valid job offer no longer adds CRS points. If any guide, calculator or agent tells you otherwise, check the date of the information.

Age

Age points are calculated on the date of your ITA and are highest between ages 20 and 29. A birthday after your ITA does not prevent visa issuance under subsection 11.2(2) of IRPA, but a birthday before your ITA lowers your score, so time your language tests and profile updates with your age in mind.

Education and the ECA

For foreign education, you need an Educational Credential Assessment from an organization designated by IRCC, such as World Education Services (WES), the International Credential Assessment Service of Canada (ICAS), the International Qualifications Assessment Service (IQAS), Comparative Education Service (CES) or International Credential Evaluation Service (ICES). Medical doctors and pharmacists use their professional bodies. ECAs are generally valid for five years.

Common mistakes:

  • Claiming the level you believe you earned rather than the level stated in your ECA.
  • Claiming two or more credentials without an ECA for each one you rely on.
  • Letting your ECA expire before you submit your application.

Language tests

IRCC accepts results only from designated testing organizations: IELTS General Training, CELPIP-General and PTE Core for English, and TEF Canada and TCF Canada for French. Results are generally valid for two years and must be valid on the date you submit your application.

  • Enter your scores exactly as printed, for each of the four abilities.
  • Enter the correct test date, result date and registration or certificate number.
  • Do not round up or estimate.
  • If you retake a test, update your profile with the new result and keep both result sheets.

Language is often the single most effective way to raise your CRS score, but only real, valid results count.

Canadian and foreign work experience

Count only experience that meets the legal tests in Part 2 and Part 3. For Canadian experience, also make sure you held valid status and authorization while working. Count years based on exact dates; 11 months and 3 weeks is not one year.

Spouse or common-law partner

Your marital status affects your score significantly.

  • If your spouse or partner is a Canadian citizen or permanent resident, or will not come with you, you are generally scored as a single applicant. They must still be declared, and a non-accompanying spouse must generally still be examined.
  • If your spouse is accompanying you, their education, language and Canadian work experience can add points, but only with valid evidence.
  • Couples who both qualify can calculate both scores and choose the higher-scoring principal applicant.

Never leave out a spouse, partner or child to change your score. It is misrepresentation under section 40 of IRPA, and under paragraph 117(9)(d) of the IRPR, an undeclared family member who was not examined generally cannot be sponsored by you later.

Sibling in Canada

You can claim points if you or your accompanying spouse have a brother or sister aged 18 or older who is a Canadian citizen or permanent resident living in Canada. You must prove the relationship, usually with birth certificates showing a common parent, and the sibling’s status and residence.

Provincial nomination

A provincial nomination adds 600 points, but only if it is valid when you are invited and when you apply. Make sure your nomination has not expired and that the details match your profile.

French-language skills

Strong French skills can earn additional points and access to French-language category draws. You must prove your level with valid TEF Canada or TCF Canada results.

Part 6: Proof of Funds

Under subparagraph 76(1)(b)(i) of the IRPR, Federal Skilled Worker applicants must hold transferable and available funds, unencumbered by debts or other obligations, equal to half the minimum necessary income for their family size. Federal Skilled Trades applicants face a similar requirement under section 87.2. Canadian Experience Class applicants are exempt, as are applicants with a valid job offer who are authorized to work in Canada.

What countsWhat does not count
Savings in your own accountsBorrowed money
Funds in your spouse’s accounts, with proof of accessEquity in a home or other property
Funds you can readily access and transferFunds you cannot legally access or move
Documented gifts that are genuinely yoursFunds encumbered by debts or obligations

According to IRCC, official bank letters must be on the institution’s letterhead and include its contact information, your name, outstanding debts, account numbers, the date each account was opened, the current balance and the average balance for the past six months. IRCC updates minimum amounts regularly, so check the current figures before you create your profile and again before you submit.

Practical tips:

  • Keep your settlement funds in a well-established bank for at least six months where possible.
  • Explain the source of any large deposits with documents.
  • For gifts, provide a gift deed, proof of the donor’s relationship and financial capacity and proof of transfer.
  • Keep funds available until your visa is issued.

Part 7: Building Your Express Entry Profile

Your profile is a legal declaration. Treat it like one.

Before you start

Gather every document you will rely on: passport, language results, ECA, reference letters, status documents, nomination certificate if any, proof of funds and family documents. Enter your information from the documents, not from memory.

While completing your profile

  • Enter names exactly as they appear in your passport.
  • Enter dates exactly; do not estimate.
  • Select your primary occupation NOC carefully.
  • List all work experience accurately, even periods that do not earn points.
  • Declare your marital status and family members truthfully.
  • Answer every background question honestly, including previous refusals from any country.

The profile self-audit

Before you submit your profile, print it or save it as a PDF and check each answer against a document. Ask yourself: “If an officer asked me to prove this tomorrow, which document would I give them?” If you have no answer, correct the profile.

Your profile remains in the pool for up to 12 months. Keep it updated if anything changes, such as new language results, a new job, a birthday, a marriage or a child.

Part 8: Before You Accept an ITA

An ITA is the moment of truth. Before you accept, recalculate your score using only what your documents prove.

Question to askWhy it matters
Are all language results still valid?Expired results cannot support your score
Is my ECA still valid?An expired ECA cannot support education points
Do my reference letters prove every month of experience claimed?Unsupported experience reduces points
Does my NOC match my duties?A mismatch can remove experience and category eligibility
If I was invited in a category draw, did I meet the category requirement on the invitation date?IRPA s. 11.2 applies to category requirements
Is my verified score at or above the cutoff for my draw?If not, and no exception applies, refusal is likely

If your verified score falls below the cutoff for your round, you do not meet the program requirements or a category requirement, or you cannot realistically gather the documents in time, get advice about declining the ITA. Declining is usually far better than submitting an application you cannot support, and you can generally re-enter the pool with a corrected profile.

Part 9: The 60 Days After Your ITA

You generally have 60 days from your ITA to submit a complete electronic application for permanent residence. Plan the 60 days carefully.

TimingTask
Days 1 to 5Download your personalized document checklist; request police certificates; book medical exams; request any missing reference letters
Days 6 to 20Gather identity, civil status, education, language and work documents; arrange certified translations
Days 21 to 40Complete all online forms; prepare letters of explanation; organize proof of funds
Days 41 to 50Conduct a full review of forms and documents for consistency
Days 51 to 60Final checks, fee payment and submission, leaving a margin for technical problems

Police certificates

Police certificates are among the most common reasons Express Entry applications are returned as incomplete. You generally need a certificate for each country or territory where you, and any family members aged 18 or older, lived for six months or more in a row since age 18. Rules vary by country. If you have applied for a certificate but cannot get it before your deadline, upload proof that you applied, along with an explanation.

Upfront medical exams

According to IRCC, Express Entry applicants have been required to submit immigration medical exam information with their application since August 21, 2025. After a facilitation period ended on October 21, 2025, applications received without it may be rejected. Book your exam with an IRCC panel physician immediately after your ITA. Section 30 of the IRPR requires applicants for permanent residence and their family members to submit to a medical examination.

Personal history and addresses

Account for every month in your personal history and address history for the period IRCC requires, usually the last 10 years or since age 18. Include periods of unemployment, study, travel and job searching. Gaps create questions about your background.

Translations

Documents not in English or French must be submitted with translations. If the translator is not certified, include an affidavit from the translator. Translations must be complete, including stamps and notations.

Letters of explanation

Use letters of explanation to address anything an officer might question: a document you cannot obtain, a gap in history, a name difference, a large deposit or a change since your profile was created. Keep them short, factual and supported by evidence.

Consistency across everything

Your forms, reference letters, resume, ECA, language results, previous applications and profile must tell the same story. Officers may compare your current application with previous applications you made to IRCC, including visitor, study and work permit applications.

Part 10: The Final Pre-Submission Audit

Before you submit, work through this checklist line by line.

  • Every name matches your passport.
  • Every date matches your documents.
  • Every CRS claim is supported by a valid document.
  • Your NOC code matches your duties in each reference letter.
  • Every reference letter includes letterhead, contact details, signature, titles, dates, hours, salary and detailed duties.
  • No excluded work, such as full-time study periods, self-employment for CEC or unauthorized work, is counted.
  • Language results and ECAs are valid on the submission date.
  • Proof of funds meets current IRCC amounts, if required, and bank letters include all required details.
  • Police certificates are uploaded for every required country, or proof of application with an explanation.
  • Medical exam information is included for you and each family member.
  • Personal history and address history have no gaps.
  • All family members are declared, including non-accompanying family members.
  • Previous refusals from any country are disclosed.
  • All documents in other languages include translations.
  • All forms are signed and complete.
  • Fees are paid.

Part 11: After You Submit

Your obligations continue after submission.

  • Monitor your IRCC account and email, including spam folders, for requests.
  • Provide biometrics when instructed.
  • Respond to every request before the deadline.
  • If you receive a procedural fairness letter, respond carefully with evidence and address every concern.
  • Report material changes, such as marriage, the birth of a child or a change in employment. Under section 51 of the IRPR, you must also disclose material changes when you are examined for permanent residence.

Part 13: When DIY Is Not Enough

Many applicants can prepare a strong application on their own. Consider professional help if:

  • You have a previous refusal from Canada or any other country.
  • Your experience was paid in cash or is hard to document.
  • Your employer will not provide a reference letter.
  • You are unsure which NOC code fits your duties.
  • You were invited in a category draw and are unsure you meet the requirement.
  • You have any criminal history, medical concern or complex family situation.
  • You receive a procedural fairness letter.

Under section 91 of IRPA, only authorized representatives can charge a fee for immigration advice or representation. These include Regulated Canadian Immigration Consultants (RCICs) licensed by the College of Immigration and Citizenship Consultants, lawyers and paralegals licensed by a provincial or territorial law society and notaries in Quebec. Verify the licence of anyone you pay. A professional file review before submission can be a cost-effective middle ground for DIY applicants.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I prepare a strong Express Entry application without a consultant?

Yes. Many applicants prepare successful applications on their own. The key is understanding the legal requirements in IRPA and the IRPR, choosing your NOC code based on your real duties, obtaining compliant reference letters, claiming only CRS points you can prove and submitting a complete application. A professional review before submission can catch problems you may miss.

How do I choose the right NOC code for Express Entry?

Start by writing down your real duties, then search the official NOC 2021 database by keywords. Compare each candidate occupation’s lead statement and main duties with your work. Under sections 75 and 87.1 of the IRPR, you must have performed the lead statement and a substantial number of main duties, including all essential duties.

What makes an Express Entry reference letter strong?

A strong letter is on company letterhead, includes the employer’s contact details and is signed by a supervisor or human resources officer. It states each job title with dates, hours per week, annual salary and benefits, and specific duties written in the employer’s own words. It should not copy the NOC description word for word.

Should I accept every ITA I receive?

Not automatically. Before accepting, recalculate your CRS score using only what your documents prove and confirm that you meet your program and any category requirements. Under section 11.2 of IRPA, unsupported qualifications can lead to refusal. If your verified score or eligibility falls short, declining and correcting your profile may be safer.

How long do I have to submit my application after an ITA?

You generally have 60 days from your ITA to submit a complete electronic application for permanent residence. Start police certificates and medical exams immediately, because they often take the longest. An incomplete application can be returned under sections 10 and 12 of the IRPR, and a returned application does not restore your ITA.
