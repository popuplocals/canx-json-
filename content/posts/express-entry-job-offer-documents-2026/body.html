From November 3, 2026, a qualifying job offer in a high-wage occupation can be worth up to 100 Comprehensive Ranking System (CRS) points, and it no longer needs a Labour Market Impact Assessment (LMIA). That has led many candidates and employers to a simple assumption: all you need now is a signed letter from an employer.

Legally, that assumption is risky. A letter can show what an employer has offered. It cannot, on its own, prove that the employer is a real, operating business, that it genuinely needs the role, that it can pay for it, that the candidate can do the job, or that the employer complies with employment and recruitment laws. Yet those are exactly the questions an IRCC officer must answer before accepting a job offer as genuine. This post explains the legal framework, how officers are likely to test each factor, and what a complete job offer package should contain.

Quick Summary

IRCC requires a qualifying job offer to be in writing and to set out the job’s terms, including pay, deductions, duties, and conditions of employment. But a written offer is only the starting point. Under Canadian immigration law, the onus is on the applicant to satisfy the officer that they meet every requirement, including that the offer is genuine. Officers will assess five genuineness factors, and a letter alone cannot prove most of them. Candidates should be ready to support their job offer with evidence about the employer, the business need, its ability to pay, their own qualifications, and the employer’s legal compliance.

Key Legal Terms

Immigration law uses specific language for job offers. Understanding it helps explain how officers think:

  • Offer of employment: The general term in Canadian immigration law for a job offer made by an employer to a foreign national.
  • Arranged employment: The term used in the Immigration and Refugee Protection Regulations for a qualifying job offer under the Federal Skilled Worker Program selection factors. It is also the concept behind job offer points in the CRS.
  • Genuineness: Whether an offer of employment is real, in the sense that the employer intends to employ the candidate on the stated terms and has a legitimate need to do so. An offer is sometimes described as bona fide, meaning made in good faith.
  • Onus or burden of proof: The responsibility to prove a fact. In immigration applications, the onus is on the applicant to satisfy the officer that they meet the requirements of the program.
  • Balance of probabilities: The usual standard of proof for facts in these applications. The officer asks whether it is more likely than not that a fact is true.
  • Material terms: The essential terms of an employment offer, such as position, duties, wage, hours, location, start date, and duration.
  • Procedural fairness: The principle that, where an officer relies on concerns the applicant may not be aware of, the applicant should generally have a chance to respond, often through a procedural fairness letter.
  • Misrepresentation: Directly or indirectly misrepresenting or withholding material facts relating to a relevant matter. Under the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act, it can lead to inadmissibility for five years.

The Legal Framework

The Applicant Carries the Onus

Under the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act, a foreign national applying for permanent residence must satisfy an officer that they meet the requirements of the Act and its regulations. The Act also requires applicants to answer truthfully and to produce the documents and evidence that an officer reasonably requires. In practice, this means it is the candidate’s job to prove that the job offer is genuine. It is not the officer’s job to prove that it is not.

IRCC's Written Offer Requirements

IRCC’s job offer guidance states that a valid job offer must be recent and in writing, must not come from an embassy, high commission, or consulate in Canada, and must set out the job details, such as pay and deductions, duties, and conditions like hours of work. A qualifying offer must generally be for paid, full-time, continuous work in Canada for at least one year after the candidate becomes a permanent resident.

The Five Genuineness Factors

IRCC’s October 16, 2026 backgrounder states that an officer must determine that a qualifying job offer is genuine, including whether:

  1. the employer is actively engaged in the business for which the offer is being made
  2. the offer reflects the employer’s reasonable employment needs
  3. the employer can reasonably meet the terms of the offer
  4. the candidate is qualified and able to perform the work and is likely to accept and carry out the employment
  5. the employer has complied with applicable federal and provincial employment and recruitment laws

A job offer letter, however well drafted, speaks directly to only part of the third and fourth factors: the terms offered. Everything else must come from other evidence.

What a Complete Job Offer Letter Should Include

In the real world, a serious job offer is a detailed document. An Express Entry job offer should be no different. At a minimum, it should set out the material terms clearly:

  • The parties: The employer’s full legal name, operating name, business address, business number, and the signatory’s name and position. The candidate’s full name.
  • The position: Job title, National Occupational Classification (NOC) code, and the main duties, written to reflect the actual work.
  • The work location: The address or region where the work will be performed.
  • Hours and status: Full-time hours per week, and confirmation that the position is permanent or for at least one year after permanent residence.
  • Compensation: The wage or salary, pay frequency, and deductions, plus any benefits such as health coverage, vacation, or pension contributions.
  • Start date: The expected start date, which in most cases follows the candidate becoming a permanent resident.
  • Standard terms: Probation period, termination provisions that comply with provincial employment standards, reporting relationship, and any relevant company policies.
  • Conditions: Any conditions of the offer, such as obtaining permanent residence or a required licence. Conditions unrelated to the role can weaken the offer.
  • Signatures and acceptance: Signed and dated by an authorized representative of the employer, on company letterhead, and signed by the candidate to show acceptance.

A letter that leaves out material terms, or uses vague, copied duties, invites questions. A detailed, specific letter shows that the employer has thought seriously about the role.

How Officers Are Likely to Test Each Factor

IRCC has not yet published a document checklist for job offers under the new rules. Based on how officers assess genuineness in other programs, the following table shows how each factor is likely to be tested and what evidence can help.

Genuineness factorWhat the officer is likely to askEvidence that can help
Employer actively engaged in the businessIs this a real, operating business that does this kind of work?Business registration and licences, Canada Revenue Agency business number, recent financial statements or tax filings, payroll records, lease or proof of premises, website
Reasonable employment needsDoes this business genuinely need this role?Organizational chart, explanation of the role and why it is needed, evidence of growth or a vacancy, recruitment records
Ability to meet the termsCan the employer pay the wage and sustain the job for at least one year after PR?Financial statements, notices of assessment, payroll history, bank records
Candidate qualified and likely to acceptCan the candidate do this job, and will they take it?Degrees, Educational Credential Assessment, licences, reference letters, signed acceptance, evidence of contact with the employer
Compliance with employment and recruitment lawsHas the employer followed the law?Provincial registrations where required, records of fair recruitment, absence of fees charged to workers, compliance history

How Officers Can Verify a Job Offer

Officers do not rely only on what applicants submit. Depending on the case, they may:

  • review the documents provided and compare them for consistency
  • check public information, such as business registries and the employer’s online presence
  • compare the offer with the candidate’s profile, work history, and earlier applications
  • contact the employer to confirm the offer and its terms
  • request additional documents
  • send a procedural fairness letter setting out concerns and inviting a response
  • in some cases, interview the applicant

The more consistent and complete your evidence is from the start, the less likely an officer is to need further steps.

So, Is a Plain Paper Job Offer Enough?

Technically, IRCC’s published requirement is a written offer with specific content. But meeting the minimum format is not the same as proving genuineness. Because the onus is on the applicant, and because officers must be satisfied on all five factors, a single page with a title and a salary is unlikely to be enough where an officer has any doubt. That is especially true for new or small employers, offers to people the employer has never employed, and offers that do not obviously fit the candidate’s background.

Our advice is simple: treat the job offer letter as the cover page, not the whole file. Build a supporting package that answers each genuineness factor before an officer has to ask.

A Practical Job Offer Package

From the Employer

  1. A detailed, signed job offer letter on company letterhead
  2. Proof of business registration and licences
  3. Recent financial statements or tax filings
  4. Recent payroll records or remittances
  5. An organizational chart showing where the role fits
  6. A short letter explaining the business need for the position
  7. Records of how the candidate was found and selected
  8. Proof of any provincial employer registration that applies

From the Candidate

  1. A signed acceptance of the job offer
  2. Proof of education and, where relevant, an Educational Credential Assessment
  3. Reference letters from past employers describing relevant duties
  4. Licences or certifications, or evidence of progress toward them
  5. A brief explanation if the offer is in a different field from your current job

Legal Consequences of a Weak or False Offer

  • Refusal of the points: If an officer is not satisfied the offer is genuine, the job offer points will not count.
  • Refusal of the application: If your score without those points falls below the cut-off of the round in which you were invited, your application could be refused.
  • Misrepresentation: If an officer finds that the offer, or information about it, was false or misleading on a material point, you could be found inadmissible for five years.
  • Review options: Applicants can seek leave for judicial review of a refusal in the Federal Court, but this is a narrow and time-limited process. It is far better to submit strong evidence from the start.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a job offer letter enough to claim Express Entry job offer points?

A written offer is required, but it may not be enough on its own. The officer must be satisfied that the offer is genuine, considering the employer’s business, its needs, its ability to pay, your qualifications, and its legal compliance. Because the onus is on you, supporting documents that address each of these factors greatly strengthen your application.

What must an Express Entry job offer letter include?

IRCC requires a recent, written offer that sets out the job details, such as pay and deductions, duties, and conditions like hours of work. In practice, a strong letter also includes the employer’s legal details, the NOC code, work location, start date, duration, benefits, probation, termination terms, and signatures from both the employer and the candidate.

What documents will IRCC ask for to prove a job offer is genuine?

IRCC has not yet published a specific checklist for job offers under the new rules. Officers may ask for evidence such as business registration, financial statements, payroll records, an explanation of the business need, and proof of your qualifications. Preparing these in advance can prevent delays and procedural fairness letters.

Can IRCC contact my employer about my job offer?

Yes, officers may verify information, including by contacting an employer to confirm the job offer and its terms. Make sure your employer knows about the offer, understands its terms, and can answer questions about the role, your selection, and the business.

What happens if the officer doubts my job offer?

The officer may request more documents or send a procedural fairness letter explaining the concerns. Respond fully, on time, and with evidence that addresses each concern directly. If the officer is still not satisfied, the job offer points may be refused, which could affect your application. Professional help is strongly recommended at this stage.
