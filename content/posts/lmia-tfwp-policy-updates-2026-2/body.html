Between 2022 and 2024, Canada’s TFWP expanded dramatically in response to historic domestic labour shortages across almost every sector of the economy. The program issued record volumes of LMIA approvals and work permits. LMIA-based work permit arrivals reached 184,008 in 2023. For many employers, the TFWP became not an emergency labour measure but a routine staffing channel.

Beginning in late 2024 and accelerating through 2025 and into 2026, the federal government reversed course sharply. The 2025 to 2027 Immigration Levels Plan reduced annual permanent residence targets and explicitly targeted a reduction in the temporary resident population to 5% of Canada’s total population by end of 2026. LMIA-based work permit allocations were cut from 184,008 in 2023 to a target range of 80,000 to 84,800 annually. Policy changes were layered on top of one another with a speed and comprehensiveness that many employers were not prepared for.

This article provides a structured, current account of every significant LMIA and TFWP policy change that affects employer applications in 2026. Changes are organized by effective date and presented with their practical operational consequences. This is not a comprehensive immigration regulation textbook. It is a working document for employers who need to know what the rules are right now and what to expect going forward.

Change 1: LMIA Validity Reduced from 12 Months to 6 Months (2024)

Effective during 2024, ESDC reduced the standard validity period for a positive LMIA from twelve months to six months from the date of issuance. Previously, an employer who received a positive LMIA had up to a year for the foreign worker to use it to apply for a work permit. The reduction to six months significantly compresses the window between LMIA approval and work permit application.

Practical Consequence

Employers and workers must now coordinate the work permit application process much more aggressively after the LMIA is issued. The worker should begin preparing their work permit application package immediately upon receiving the positive LMIA letter, not as an afterthought weeks later. Any delays in biometric submission, document collection, or application preparation now carry a real risk of the LMIA expiring before the work permit is issued.

For employers with workers in countries where visa processing or biometric submission adds additional time to the work permit application timeline (such as India, Philippines, or Nigeria where TRV and VAC requirements apply), the six-month window requires particularly active management. Workers in these situations should have their work permit applications submitted within the first four to six weeks of receiving the positive LMIA to create a realistic buffer.

Change 2: Low-Wage Workforce Cap Reduced (2024)

The maximum proportion of TFW workers in low-wage positions at a single employer location was reduced during 2024. The overall TFWP workforce cap was reduced from 30% to 20% (except in selected high-priority sectors such as construction and healthcare). Within the low-wage stream specifically, the cap was reduced from 20% to 10% in food service, accommodation, and retail sectors, and the maximum employment duration for low-wage positions was reduced from two years to one year.

Practical Consequence for Food Service and Accommodation

The 10% cap in food service and accommodation represents a severe constraint for employers who had been operating with higher proportions of TFW workers in their kitchen, housekeeping, and front desk operations. Many operators who had been using the TFWP as a structural staffing tool at volumes above 10% of their workforce were required to wind down those positions as permits expired without renewal, or to reduce their TFW proportion through hiring Canadians.

Employers still in these sectors in 2026 must calculate their current TFW proportion at each individual location before submitting any new low-wage LMIA application. A miscalculation that results in an application pushing a location above 10% will result in refusal of the application and waste the associated fees and advertising investment.

High-Wage Stream: No Cap Applies

The cap reductions apply to Low-Wage stream positions. High-Wage stream positions continue to have no cap on the proportion of TFW workers an employer can have at a given location. This structural difference provides an incentive for employers who can genuinely restructure position wages to the High-Wage threshold to do so, both to access the no-cap High-Wage stream and because the wage increase may be consistent with market rates in shortage-affected sectors.

Change 3: Low-Wage LMIA Moratorium in High-Unemployment CMAs (September 26, 2024)

Effective September 26, 2024, ESDC implemented a moratorium on processing low-wage LMIA applications in Census Metropolitan Areas (CMAs) where the unemployment rate exceeds 6%. The policy reflects the core TFWP principle that foreign workers should fill genuine shortages, and that in labour markets where unemployment is elevated, genuine shortages are less likely.

How the Moratorium Works

ESDC updates the list of CMAs subject to the moratorium quarterly based on Statistics Canada regional unemployment data. An application submitted for a work location within a CMA that is on the moratorium list will be refused to process — the application is returned with a note that ESDC is not accepting low-wage applications for that area. The $1,000 application fee is refunded in these cases because the application was never accepted for processing.

As of early 2026, 24 CMAs remained on the moratorium list including Toronto, Calgary, and Ottawa. Seven CMAs including Vancouver, Halifax, and Winnipeg were removed from the list in a January 2026 quarterly update as their unemployment rates dropped below the 6% threshold. The list changes quarterly, and an area that is clear in one quarter may be added in the next if unemployment rises.

Critical Pre-Application Check

Before submitting any low-wage LMIA application, verify the current moratorium list at Canada.ca using the specific postal code of the work location. Do not rely on knowledge of whether a CMA was on or off the list in a previous quarter. The list is updated every three months, and an application submitted without checking current status risks either refusal to process (if the area was added) or unnecessary delay while you confirm eligibility. The moratorium check should be the first step in any low-wage application preparation process.

Sectors Exempt from the Moratorium

Not all low-wage positions are subject to the moratorium. ESDC has carved out specific sectors from the unemployment rate restriction in recognition that labour shortages in these areas are considered critical regardless of regional unemployment. The exempt sectors include:

  • Primary agriculture (SAWP and Agricultural Stream positions on farms, ranches, nurseries, and greenhouses).
  • Healthcare and social assistance positions (recognizing the systemic healthcare labour shortage).
  • Construction sector positions (reflecting ongoing infrastructure and housing demand).
  • Food processing and manufacturing positions in certain categories.

Employers in these exempt sectors can submit low-wage LMIA applications in any CMA regardless of the unemployment rate. However, ‘exempt’ means exempt from the moratorium, not exempt from all LMIA requirements. The standard recruitment evidence requirements, wage compliance requirements, and all other conditions of a low-wage LMIA application still fully apply.

High-Wage Positions Are Not Affected

The CMA unemployment rate moratorium applies exclusively to low-wage stream LMIA applications. High-wage stream applications can be submitted for any location regardless of the regional unemployment rate. This is another structural factor that may make the decision to offer wages at or above the provincial median operationally significant for employers in high-unemployment urban areas.

Change 4: Direct Apply Feature on Job Bank Made Mandatory

ESDC introduced the mandatory Direct Apply feature on Job Bank as part of the enhanced recruitment requirements. Under this requirement, employers posting positions on Job Bank as part of their LMIA recruitment advertising must activate the Direct Apply feature, which allows Canadian job seekers to apply directly through Job Bank without needing to navigate to an external employer website.

Practical Consequence

Employers who previously used Job Bank postings that directed all applicants to an external application portal rather than accepting applications through Job Bank itself must update their recruitment process. The purpose of the Direct Apply requirement is to lower barriers for Canadian job seekers and to ensure that the Job Bank posting is genuinely accessible as a recruitment channel rather than functioning as a nominal listing that redirects to a harder-to-access application process.

For ESDC officers reviewing recruitment evidence, a Job Bank posting that did not have Direct Apply activated during the minimum advertising period may be viewed as not fully compliant with the advertising requirements. Employers should confirm that Direct Apply is activated on all Job Bank postings submitted as LMIA recruitment evidence.

Change 5: Doubled Low-Wage Advertising Period — 8 Weeks (April 1, 2026)

Effective April 1, 2026, the minimum advertising duration for low-wage LMIA applications was doubled from four to eight consecutive weeks. All Job Bank postings and additional platform postings for low-wage positions must now run for at least eight continuous weeks before the LMIA application can be submitted.

Practical Consequence for All Low-Wage Employers

This single change adds four weeks to the minimum pre-application preparation timeline for every low-wage LMIA application. An employer who was previously planning for a six-week preparation period (four weeks advertising plus two weeks document collection) now needs at least ten to twelve weeks before submission. Combined with the 8 to 20 week processing time after submission and the work permit processing time after approval, the total timeline from decision-to-hire to worker-starting-work for a low-wage position is now frequently over nine months.

Employers who are planning workforce around specific seasonal or project-based labour needs must build this extended timeline into their operational planning from the outset. A restaurant planning for summer season staffing that begins its LMIA recruitment in April 2026 is simply too late to have workers in place for a June opening under the current rules. The planning cycle for low-wage positions must start in the fall for the following summer.

The Youth Recruitment Requirement

Alongside the doubled advertising period, employers applying for low-wage LMIAs must now demonstrate that they made specific efforts to recruit youth — defined as job seekers between 15 and 30 years of age — before seeking a foreign worker for the position. This requires advertising through youth-targeted channels such as post-secondary institution job boards, youth employment centers, career fairs at high schools or colleges, and online platforms commonly used by young job seekers.

The youth recruitment requirement is new enough as of 2026 that ESDC’s specific expectations for what constitutes adequate youth recruitment documentation are still being clarified through operational guidance and early application reviews. Employers should document their youth-targeted recruitment efforts specifically and separately from their general recruitment documentation, and should retain records of all youth-specific outreach conducted during the recruitment period.

Change 6: Rural Employer Cap Temporarily Expanded to 15% (April 1, 2026 — March 31, 2027)

Effective April 1, 2026, and running as a temporary measure until March 31, 2027, employers in rural areas of Canada can employ up to 15% of their workforce in low-wage TFW positions — increased from the standard 10% limit. This expansion recognizes that rural labour markets face structural challenges attracting and retaining workers that are distinct from the challenges in major urban centres.

Who Qualifies as a Rural Employer

The rural designation for this purpose is governed by the specific definition used by ESDC, which generally applies to employers outside Census Metropolitan Areas and Census Agglomerations — essentially employers located in smaller communities with populations below the thresholds that trigger CMA or CA designation. Employers should confirm their specific work location qualifies under ESDC’s rural definition before planning operations around the 15% cap.

Practical Consequence

For agricultural processors, manufacturing operations, hospitality employers, and other businesses in rural Canada who have historically been constrained by the 10% cap, the temporary expansion to 15% provides meaningful additional capacity for the year in which it is in effect. Employers who want to take advantage of this temporary measure should submit any LMIA applications that would benefit from the higher cap before March 31, 2027, recognizing that the cap reverts to 10% unless further action is taken by the government.

The temporary nature of this measure is a planning consideration for employers who are building workforce strategies that depend on TFW proportions above 10%. Building a long-term operational model around a cap that is explicitly described as a temporary measure creates vulnerability if the cap returns to 10% at the end of the measure’s term.

Change 7: Annual TFWP Work Permit Volume Targets Dramatically Reduced

The federal government’s 2025 to 2027 Immigration Levels Plan cut the annual target for LMIA-based work permit arrivals to 80,000 to 84,800 per year — reduced from 184,008 in 2023. This reduction reflects the government’s broader policy goal of reducing Canada’s temporary resident population to 5% of the total population by end of 2026.

What This Means for Approval Rates and Processing Priorities

Lower annual targets do not automatically mean lower approval rates for individual, well-prepared applications from employers with genuine needs in appropriate sectors. ESDC continues to process applications and issue positive LMIAs for genuine shortages. However, the reduced overall volume target does mean that applications in sectors where labour market evidence of shortage is weaker will face more scrutiny, and that ESDC officers operate in an environment where the program is intended to serve more targeted and strategic needs rather than functioning as a routine staffing channel for all industries.

Employers in sectors that ESDC has explicitly identified as priorities — healthcare, construction, agriculture, and technology — are better positioned in this environment than employers in sectors where domestic labour market conditions are considered adequate for the positions being sought. The political and policy direction is clearly toward a more targeted, sector-specific TFWP, and employers should assess their long-term workforce strategies in light of the likelihood that program accessibility will continue to be shaped by this direction.

Change 8: Signal of Further Reform from the Federal Government

Prime Minister Mark Carney, in an address in September 2025, signaled that further TFWP reform is coming with an approach that targets ‘specific, strategic sectors and needs in specific regions.’ No legislative or regulatory proposals had been published by May 2026, but the political direction is clear: the TFWP is moving away from being a broadly accessible labour supply mechanism toward being a precisely targeted tool for documented, critical, regionally specific shortages.

What this means practically for employers in 2026 is that the regulatory environment will likely continue to evolve. Employers who have built workforce models with significant TFWP dependency should be actively developing parallel domestic recruitment and retention strategies rather than assuming the program’s current form and scope will remain stable. The employers who are best positioned for a more targeted TFWP are those who can clearly demonstrate that their foreign worker needs are genuinely tied to documented, regionally specific shortages in nationally important sectors.

Change 9: Express Entry Arranged Employment Points Eliminated (March 25, 2025)

While this is an IRCC policy change rather than an ESDC policy change, it directly affects the immigration strategy calculations of every LMIA employer who has been supporting workers’ Express Entry profiles. Since March 25, 2025, LMIA-backed job offers no longer generate any CRS bonus points in Express Entry. The 50 or 200-point arranged employment bonus has been eliminated entirely.

For employers who obtained LMIAs specifically to support workers’ Express Entry scores, this change altered the immigration benefit the worker receives from the LMIA. Workers who previously used arranged employment points as a primary PR strategy must now pivot to accumulating Canadian work experience and pursuing PNP nominations. Employers who understood their LMIA as a combined staffing and worker-PR-support tool need to reframe the PR support component around the PNP pathway rather than Express Entry CRS points.

How These Changes Affect Applications in Progress

Employers with LMIA applications already submitted before April 1, 2026 are not affected by the new eight-week advertising requirement for applications that were submitted with complete four-week recruitment documentation before that date. However, applications submitted on or after April 1, 2026 must meet the new eight-week minimum regardless of when the recruitment advertising was initiated.

Workers who received an Invitation to Apply before March 25, 2025 and who had arranged employment points in their profile at the time of the ITA are not affected by the points elimination. Candidates who submitted complete PR applications before March 25, 2025 are similarly unaffected. All other candidates in the pool had arranged employment points removed from their profiles as of that date.

The CMA moratorium applies to applications submitted on or after September 26, 2024, based on the unemployment rate of the work location’s CMA at the time of submission. Applications submitted before that date were not affected. Applications submitted after that date must verify the current moratorium status of the work location regardless of when the recruitment advertising was conducted.

Strategic Recommendations for Employers Navigating the New Environment

The cumulative effect of these changes is a TFWP that is significantly more restrictive, more administratively demanding, and more oriented toward genuinely critical sector-specific needs than the program that existed in 2022 or 2023. Employers who built workforce models around the program’s previous accessibility need to assess which elements of those models remain viable and which need to be restructured.

For low-wage sector employers specifically, the combination of the 10% cap, the eight-week advertising requirement, the youth recruitment obligation, the CMA moratorium, and the annual volume reduction represents a fundamentally different operating environment than what existed two years ago. These employers should honestly assess what proportion of their workforce is TFW-dependent, what the realistic timeline for LMIA applications now looks like, and what domestic recruitment and training investments would be necessary to reduce that dependency over time.

For high-wage sector employers, the changes are less severe but still significant. The LMIA validity reduction from twelve to six months requires more active timeline management. The Express Entry arranged employment point elimination changes the nature of the immigration support value proposition to workers. The political signal of further reform toward targeted sectors means that employers in sectors with strong shortage documentation are better positioned than those in sectors where the evidence base is mixed.

The universal recommendation across all employer types and sectors is to work with immigration professionals who are tracking these changes in real time and updating their practice accordingly. The pace of policy change in 2024, 2025, and 2026 has been fast enough that advice from professionals who are not current on the specific changes described in this article may be dangerously outdated.

Frequently Asked Questions

What LMIA rules changed in 2026?

The most significant 2026 change to LMIA rules took effect April 1, 2026: the minimum advertising period for low-wage LMIA applications was doubled from four to eight consecutive weeks, and employers must now demonstrate specific youth recruitment efforts alongside their general recruitment activities. A rural employer exception allows businesses outside CMAs and CAs to employ up to 15% of their workforce in low-wage TFW positions (up from 10%) as a temporary measure until March 31, 2027. These changes come on top of significant 2024 and 2025 changes including the LMIA validity reduction, cap reductions, the CMA unemployment moratorium, and the elimination of arranged employment Express Entry points.

How has the CMA unemployment moratorium changed LMIA processing?

Since September 26, 2024, ESDC does not accept low-wage LMIA applications for work locations in Census Metropolitan Areas where the unemployment rate is 6% or higher. The list of affected CMAs is updated quarterly. As of early 2026, 24 CMAs were on the moratorium list including Toronto, Calgary, and Ottawa, while 7 others including Vancouver, Halifax, and Winnipeg had recently dropped off the list. Agriculture, healthcare, construction, and food processing are exempt from the moratorium. High-wage stream applications are not affected by the moratorium regardless of the CMA’s unemployment rate.

How does the 8-week advertising requirement affect my LMIA timeline?

For low-wage LMIA applications submitted on or after April 1, 2026, all advertising must run for at least eight consecutive weeks before the application can be submitted. Combined with the 8 to 20 week processing time after submission and subsequent work permit processing, employers should plan for a total timeline of nine to twelve months or more from recruitment advertising start to worker start date. Seasonal employers and project-based operators must begin LMIA planning at least ten to twelve months before the needed start date to create a realistic timeline.

Are there new LMIA restrictions for specific sectors in 2026?

The most significant sector-specific restrictions affecting 2026 are: the 10% low-wage TFW workforce cap in food service, accommodation, and retail (reduced from 20% in 2024); the CMA unemployment moratorium that excludes agriculture, healthcare, construction, and food processing; and the government’s political signal of further reform toward a more sector-targeted TFWP. The temporary rural employer cap expansion to 15% provides some relief for rural operations across sectors. High-wage stream positions in all sectors are not subject to any workforce cap restriction.

What should employers do to adapt to the 2026 LMIA policy environment?

Employers should: verify the current CMA moratorium status of every low-wage application work location before starting the recruitment process; build the eight-week advertising period plus documentation time into all low-wage LMIA planning timelines; document youth recruitment efforts specifically for all low-wage applications; calculate current TFW proportions at each location before submitting new low-wage applications; consult with an experienced RCIC about whether any positions can be restructured as high-wage to avoid cap and moratorium restrictions; and develop parallel domestic recruitment and retention strategies to reduce long-term TFWP dependency in anticipation of further program reform.
